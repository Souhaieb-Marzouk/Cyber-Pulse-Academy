What this exam teaches: TA0042 Resource Development (a Tactic)

Welcome. This exam is about one page of the MITRE ATT&CK framework: the tactic TA0042 — Resource Development. Here is what you will learn, in short and simple words.

What is MITRE ATT&CK? ATT&CK is a free, public knowledge base. It describes what real attackers do, step by step. ATT&CK is not a tool you install. It is a shared language. Security teams everywhere use it to name and compare attack behavior.

What kind of object is a "tactic"? In ATT&CK, a tactic is a goal: the "why" of an attack step. A technique is the "how". This object is a Tactic, so it describes one goal of the attacker. The goal of TA0042 is simple: the attacker gets ready before the attack. They build, buy, rent, or steal the things they will need later. In the ATT&CK Enterprise matrix, TA0042 sits in the second column: after Reconnaissance (TA0043) and before Initial Access (TA0001).

What does the attacker build, buy, or steal? ATT&CK puts this into six techniques:

  • T1583 Acquire Infrastructure — they rent or buy things: domain names, servers, a VPS (a virtual computer rented in the cloud), a botnet (a network of infected computers built by someone else), a CDN (a content network that hides the real server), or space on web services.
  • T1584 Compromise Infrastructure — instead of buying, they take over things that belong to others: a hijacked domain, a hacked server, an abused web service.
  • T1585 Establish Accounts — they create new accounts: a fake persona on social media, a fresh mailbox, a new cloud account.
  • T1586 Compromise Accounts — they take over accounts that already exist.
  • T1587 Develop Capabilities — they make their own tools: malware, exploits, or certificates for their software.
  • T1588 Obtain Capabilities — they get tools from others: they buy malware, download hacking tools, or buy exploits and information about unpatched software flaws.

Why is TA0042 hard to see? This phase usually happens outside your company's network. The attacker registers a domain, rents a server, and opens accounts — none of that touches your firewalls yet. Your EDR (the security agent on laptops) and your logs often record nothing. So defenders use outside sources: newly-registered-domain feeds, certificate transparency logs (public lists of website certificates), passive DNS (a history of which IPs a domain used), whois lookups (public domain ownership records), and brand monitoring. ATT&CK itself marks most TA0042 techniques as hard to mitigate inside the enterprise; the mitigation category M1056 Pre-Compromise collects the actions you can still take from the outside.

Who works with TA0042 in a real job?

  • A CTI analyst (cyber threat intelligence) tracks the domains, accounts, and tools that threat actors buy. They write reports that name techniques like T1583.001, and they brief the CISO (the security leader).
  • A SOC analyst (security operations center) triages alerts about lookalike domains and decides: watch, verify, or escalate.
  • A threat hunter looks for infrastructure patterns: many fresh domains on one rented server, certificates for lookalike names, botnet nodes waking up.
  • An incident responder maps the attacker's infrastructure to ATT&CK IDs during a case and prepares evidence for takedowns.
  • A detection engineer builds analytics on NRD and CT-log feeds and tunes false positives.
  • A red teamer uses TA0042 to plan and document their own preparation, so the exercise stays in scope.
  • A GRC or risk analyst maps this phase to controls: external attack surface management, brand protection, and supplier risk (for example NIST CSF's Identify and supply-chain categories, or CIS Control 13).

Daily work and deliverables around TA0042 include CTI reports with ATT&CK IDs, takedown requests to registrars, detection rules on domain feeds, external asset registers, and short briefings for leaders. The main risk it addresses: attacks start before the first email arrives. If you only watch your own network, you see the attack too late. Learning TA0042 teaches you to see the attack earlier, on the public internet, while the attacker is still preparing.
