You will learn about this ATT&CK object

Imagine you work in the security team of a company that makes electricity. One morning your manager asks you: “What do we actually know about the group called ALLANITE?” This exam teaches you the answer, step by step.

You will learn that G1000 is a MITRE ATT&CK object of the type Group. ATT&CK is a big, free catalogue of attacker behaviour. A Group object describes one real threat actor: who they are, who they attack, and which steps they use. Those steps are called techniques. On this page, the actor is named ALLANITE, and Palmetto Fusion is listed as an associated group name (an alias). The profile was contributed by the vendor Dragos Threat Intelligence. ATT&CK says ALLANITE is a suspected Russian cyber espionage group. Espionage means spying. The group mainly targeted the electric utility sector in the United States and the United Kingdom. Its tactics and techniques are reportedly similar to the group Dragonfly. The profile also says something very important: ALLANITE has not shown disruptive or destructive abilities. It spies — it does not break things on purpose. It is thought to keep a quiet presence inside industrial control systems (ICS) to understand how the plant works and to stay inside.

You will learn the four techniques the page lists: T0817 Drive-by Compromise (infection through a website the victim visits — a “watering hole”), T0865 Spearphishing Attachment (infection through a malicious email attachment), T0859 Valid Accounts (signing in with credentials collected through phishing and watering hole attacks), and T0852 Screen Capture (taking screenshots of ICS screens such as operator HMIs). You will learn where ATT&CK places them (Initial Access, Persistence, Lateral Movement, Collection), which telemetry shows them, and which controls blunt them.

Where you will use this at work: a SOC analyst triages alerts that match these techniques. A CTI analyst tracks aliases, reads vendor profiles (Dragos, ICS-CERT) and briefs managers. A threat hunter looks for quiet valid-account use on engineering and HMI systems. An incident responder recognises this playbook during an incident. A detection engineer turns the techniques into alert rules. A control owner or GRC analyst checks that user training, web restrictions, MFA and account policies are really in place. The tools around this work are the ATT&CK website and Navigator layers, mail gateways, web proxies, SIEM queries and authentication logs. The documents you will produce are actor profiles, detection backlog entries and control gap reviews. The risk all of this addresses: spies quietly learning how a power grid operates.

EXTREMELY HARD — what this tier means

This tier mirrors a senior analyst's shift: three or more chained concepts, long artefact sets with realistic noise and missing information, and conflicting signals you must resolve explicitly. Some correct answers are “gather more evidence” or “accept a real business cost”. Widely used best practices are sometimes the wrong answer in this specific context.
