📘 About this ATT&CK object: the Group G0138 — Andariel

What kind of ATT&CK object is this? This topic is about a Group (also called a threat actor profile). In the MITRE ATT&CK framework, a Group page describes one real adversary team: who they are, who they work for, who they attack, and — most important — which Techniques they are known to use. The framework gives the group a stable ID. Here that ID is G0138, and the group's name is Andariel. A Group page does not teach you a single attack trick. It maps a whole intruder playbook onto the ATT&CK matrix, so you can recognise the team behind an intrusion and defend against its habits.

The group in simple words

Andariel is a North Korean state-sponsored threat group. It has been active since at least 2009. ATT&CK describes it as a sub-set of Lazarus Group, and public reporting links it to North Korea's Reconnaissance General Bureau (RGB), a military intelligence body. Think of Lazarus as a large family of intruders; Andariel is one branch of that family. Researchers track this branch under other names too: Silent Chollima (CrowdStrike), and PLUTONIUM and Onyx Sleet (Microsoft). Because North Korean group definitions overlap a lot, some researchers simply report all of this activity as Lazarus Group. You must live with that uncertainty — it is part of the job.

What does Andariel want? Two things. First, strategic disruption and espionage: it has attacked South Korean government agencies, military organizations, and domestic companies, sometimes with destructive results. Second, money: it runs cyber financial operations against ATMs, banks, and cryptocurrency exchanges. Its known operations include Operation Black Mine, Operation GoldenAxe, and Campaign Rifle, and in 2021 reporting showed it hitting South Korean companies with ransomware.

How the group works (its documented playbook)

Andariel scouts victims before attacking: it has limited watering hole attacks to specific IP address ranges (T1590.005) and has used scripts on compromised websites to learn a visitor's browser, system language, and Flash Player version (T1592.002). It gains entry in two main ways: watering hole attacks — infected websites that its chosen victims visit — often using zero-day exploits (T1189), and spearphishing emails with malicious Word or Excel attachments (T1566.001) that try to trick the user into enabling macros (T1204.002). It has also exploited many ActiveX vulnerabilities, including zero-days (T1203). Once inside, it downloads tools (T1105), hides executables inside PNG or BMP images using steganography (T1027.003), runs tasklist to list processes (T1057) and netstat -naop tcp to list network connections (T1049), collects large numbers of files from compromised systems (T1005), and uses mostly publicly available RATs (T1588.001) such as gh0st RAT and Rifdoor.

Where you will use this knowledge at work

Almost every cyber role touches group profiles like this one:

  • SOC analysts match live alerts against the group's documented techniques ("this netstat line plus a gh0st beacon fits Andariel's playbook") and decide the next triage step.
  • CTI analysts write group profiles and executive briefings, keep alias maps current (Silent Chollima / PLUTONIUM / Onyx Sleet), and reason carefully about attribution and its limits.
  • Threat hunters turn the group's TTPs into hunting hypotheses: PNG files with appended executables, IP-range-scoped watering holes, tasklist used to search for specific process strings.
  • Incident responders use the playbook to scope intrusions, choose safe evidence steps, and explain attacker behaviour in reports.
  • Detection engineers build and tune analytics anchored in the group's best telemetry: process command lines, registry Run Keys, macro lures, proxy egress to watered sites.
  • GRC and control owners map the playbook to controls — macro blocking, exploit protection, patching, user training, DLP — and audit that they actually work.
  • Red teamers emulate the group realistically to test whether the blue team would catch this specific playbook.

The tools you meet along the way include ATT&CK Navigator layers, STIX/TAXII threat-intel feeds, SIEM and EDR consoles, email gateways, and proxy logs. The documents you will produce include group profiles, detection rules, hunting notes, risk entries, and board-level briefings. The risks this knowledge addresses are targeted intrusion, financial theft, destructive attacks, and ransomware against unprepared organizations.

📋 How this exam works

  • Passing score: 70%. Your final percentage is computed only over the exercises you actually answered.
  • Questions: 16 exercises, one per screen. Each screen always shows three labelled blocks in the same order: SCENARIO, QUESTION, YOUR ANSWER.
  • Exercise types: realistic SOC triage, drag-and-drop onto tactics and slots, matching, timeline ordering, fill-in-the-blank report drafting, log-viewer analysis with a filter box, multi-step decision scenarios, a fake terminal on the hardest tier, and a small number of classic multiple-choice questions with tricky distractors.
  • Timer: this exam gives you 35 minutes in total. The timer state is always visible in the top bar.
  • Partial credit: exercises with several correct items are scored per item with the formula credit = max(0, (H − W) / N). H is the number of correct items you selected or placed. W is the number of wrong items you selected or placed — wrong picks subtract. Selecting everything to be safe gives you zero, not full marks. A partially correct answer still earns partial credit, and the feedback shows each item as ✓ Correct, ✗ Wrong or ○ Missed.
  • Check auto-pause: the moment you press Check, the timer stops automatically. You get unlimited time to read the feedback and the explanation. The timer only starts again when you press Next (or Final submit on the last exercise). This is different from the manual pause and is shown with its own message in the top bar.
  • Manual pause: press Pause for a real break. The timer stops and the exercise is hidden. Press Resume to continue.
  • Hint: one hint per exercise. A hint is a nudge, not the answer. Using one costs 10% of that exercise's credit (never 10% of the whole exam).
  • Skip: every exercise has a Skip button. Skipping hides no answer and never reveals feedback, and the timer keeps running. Before you can finish, a Review skipped exercises screen lets you return to any skipped exercise and answer it, as many times as you need, until the timer ends. Skipped exercises that are never answered are excluded from both your score and the total possible.
  • Retake: if you do not pass, you can retake the exam — questions and answer choices are shuffled again.
Severity level — MEDIUM: This tier connects two ideas per exercise: identify the behaviour, then choose the control; recognise the group, then pick the technique that fits its profile. Scenarios run 4–9 sentences and may combine two short artefacts. Two options may look similar — only one survives both reasoning steps. The goal is stated, but you must decide what to look at first.
