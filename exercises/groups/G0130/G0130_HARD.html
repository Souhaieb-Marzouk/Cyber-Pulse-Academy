Learn first: what is this ATT&CK object?

You will learn what a “Group” object is in MITRE ATT&CK, using G0130 as your example. ATT&CK is a big public knowledge base about how real attackers work. A Group object is a profile card of one real threat actor team. It collects what that team does: the tricks they use (called techniques), the tools they write or use, who they attack, and who they might be. Analysts use these profile cards every day to know who may be attacking them and how.

Imagine you are a new analyst. Here is what you should know about the Ajax Security Team (G0130):

  • Who they are: a threat actor group active since at least 2010, believed to operate out of Iran. The name “Ajax Security Team” is their profile card name, not a real company.
  • What changed over time: before 2014 they damaged websites (defacement). By 2014 they moved to spy operations with malware. Spy operations try to steal information quietly, not to break things.
  • Who they attack: the United States defense industrial base (companies that work with the US military) and Iranian people who use anti-censorship tools (tools that help people open blocked websites).
  • How they get in: they send spearphishing — fake, personal messages (by email attachment, or through social media services) that trick a person into opening a malicious file.
  • What their malware does: it records everything a person types (keylogging, T1056.001), steals passwords saved in the Firefox browser (T1555.003), and downloads more malware from the internet (T1105). They have also used SQL-injection tools (Havij, sqlmap) to attack public web applications (T1190).
  • Other names (aliases): Operation Woolen-Goldfish, AjaxTM, Rocket Kitten, Flying Kitten, Operation Saffron Rose. Different research teams found the same actor and gave it different names.

Where you will meet this object at work:

  • SOC analyst: alerts from your email gateway, proxy, or EDR may match this group’s tricks. You will triage them and decide the next step.
  • Threat hunter: you will search logs for behaviour chains this group is known for, like a document opening and then a hidden tool starting.
  • Incident responder (IR): if the group gets in, you will contain hosts, keep evidence safe, and write a report that names the techniques with ATT&CK IDs.
  • Cyber threat intelligence (CTI) analyst: you will build and update group profile cards like this one, brief your team, and judge if new activity really belongs to this group (attribution).
  • Detection engineer: you will turn the group’s techniques into detection rules and tune them, for example keylogger API monitoring or browser password-store access alerts.
  • Red teamer: you may imitate this group in a controlled test so the blue team can practise.
  • GRC analyst / control owner: you will map the group’s techniques to controls (like user training, software configuration, or network filtering) and check they really work.
  • Vulnerability management: their SQL-injection tools attack public applications, so keeping web apps patched and scanned matters.

The risk it addresses: quiet spying, stolen passwords, stolen keystrokes, and follow-on access sold or reused by other criminals. Knowing this group helps you catch real attacks earlier.

How the exam works

  • Goal: master the G0130 group profile at HARD level. The pass mark is 70%.
  • Exercise types: realistic SOC scenarios, alert triage, log reading with a filter box, drag-and-drop mapping, matching, ordering, fill-in-the-blank reports, branching decisions, ranking, and a fake shell exercise in some tiers. Everything is simulated — nothing real runs.
  • Partial credit: exercises with several correct items are scored per item: credit = (correct items − wrong items) / total items, never below zero. Wrong picks subtract. A partially right answer still earns partial credit.
  • Check buys time: when you press Check, the timer stops automatically and the feedback appears. Study it for as long as you want. The timer starts again only when you press Next. The header shows “PAUSED — reviewing feedback” during this state.
  • Manual pause: the Pause button stops the timer and hides the exercise for a real break. Resume brings you back exactly where you were.
  • Hints: one hint per exercise. It costs 10% of that exercise’s credit. The hint nudges you; it never gives the answer.
  • Skipping: every exercise has a Skip button. Skipped exercises go to a review pool. Before you finish, you can return to them and answer, as long as the timer has not ended. A skipped exercise you never answer is simply excluded from your score — it does not count against you.
  • Timer: 45 minutes (45:00 countdown) in total for this HARD tier. When it ends, the exam finishes automatically.
  • Retake: if you do not reach 70%, you can retake the exam — questions and options are shuffled again.
