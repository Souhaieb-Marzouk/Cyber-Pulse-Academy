What you will learn here

Welcome to the EXTREMELY HARD level on T1608.001 — Upload Malware. This is a Sub-Technique of T1608 (Stage Capabilities), and it lives in the Resource Development tactic (TA0042) on the PRE platform — a label that simply means everything happens before any victim contact. The adversary uploads malware — payloads, droppers, post-compromise tools, backdoors — to third-party or adversary-controlled infrastructure so the content sits there, reachable, when the operation needs it.

Why stage at all? A parked file can wait for a victim to pull it — ATT&CK links that pull to Ingress Tool Transfer (T1105), and the chance install of a backdoored package to User Execution (T1204.002). The venues range from rented or hacked servers to everyday services: Google Drive, GitHub, Pastebin, Dropbox, OneDrive, WeTransfer, even the Discord CDN. Some homes are hard to evict — IPFS (peer-to-peer storage found by content hash) and blockchain smart contracts survive takedowns that would kill a normal server. One wording matters at this level: ATT&CK says staged capabilities serve victims or malware — an already-implanted machine can also pull from staging, so one upload feeds two kinds of clients.

You will use this in senior work. As a CTI analyst, you will track crews that hop between services, hold attribution discipline when two reports name different actors for one shared bucket, and feed curated staging IOCs to MISP, Recorded Future or Anomali. As a SOC or IR lead, you will own the costly calls: sinkhole now and warn the adversary, or keep quiet monitoring and accept a window of victim risk. As a detection engineer or threat hunter, you will arm downstream detections on the pull and the execution, because DET0824's internet-scan view rarely reaches your telemetry. And in GRC, you will map M1056 Pre-compromise — the only mitigation this technique has — to controls that truly touch staging: OSINT review, Shodan and Censys attack-surface monitoring, lookalike-domain watch, and intel platforms.

This file is deliberately uncomfortable. Every scenario hides a conflict: sandbox verdicts that disagree, one shared bucket tied to two actor names, infrastructure that overlaps an old campaign but sits on a clean shared service. You will have to say which signal wins, accept that the correct action sometimes costs money or carries tip-off risk, and more than once the best answer is to do nothing yet — escalate and gather more evidence instead. Hints only restate a constraint; they never hand you the answer.

Real campaigns drive the questions: TA2541 hopping Google Drive, Pastetext, Sharetext and GitHub; Kimsuky blending Blogspot hosting with Dropbox drops; Mustang Panda and RedDelta mixing Dropbox PlugX staging with owned domains and cloud storage; EXOTIC LILY abusing TransferNow, WeTransfer and OneDrive; Saint Bear hiding droppers behind Discord CDN links; and Moonstone Sleet staging content that serves human lures and implanted malware alike. Read every artefact twice — the loudest line is often the decoy. Good luck.
