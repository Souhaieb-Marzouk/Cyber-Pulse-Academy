ⓘWhat you will learn here

This exam page covers one entry from MITRE ATT&CK: T1598.002, Phishing for Information: Spearphishing Attachment. It is a Sub-Technique of T1598 Phishing for Information, which lives in the Reconnaissance tactic, TA0043. The idea is simple: an attacker sends a crafted email with an attachment and wants the recipient to hand over information. The attachment can be a form to fill in and return, or a document that quietly opens a fake sign-in page. No malware has to run on a workstation for this technique to work.

You will learn why attackers pick this path. They want targeting data: credentials, org charts, contact lists, supplier details, and internal process knowledge. That information makes every later step cheaper and safer for them. Some groups collect it in bulk from generic lures; others build one careful lure after weeks of rapport. What they collect here often comes back later as valid accounts.

Defenders meet this technique every day. SOC analysts triage user reports in the SIEM. Mail gateway admins read message traces and authentication results. Detection engineers turn cluster signals into rules. Threat hunters chase the same signals in mail and flow data. Incident responders search mailboxes and hold or purge messages. Threat intel analysts compare artefacts with actor profiles like Dragonfly, SideCopy, Sidewinder, and Star Blizzard. GRC owners push anti-spoofing controls such as SPF, DKIM, and DMARC.

You will practise the tasks where this page shows up at work: triaging a reported attachment, reading a 16-line gateway export, deciding if dmarc=fail means spoofing or just a rewritten newsletter, detonating a document in a sandbox, ordering the response steps after a credential harvest, and writing an attribution note with the right confidence level.

The risk is concrete: one filled form or one typed password gives the attacker a working identity, and the story moves from Reconnaissance to account misuse. This exam drills the judgement calls: information intent versus execution intent, real cluster signals versus bulk noise, alarming-looking but benign mail, and the least aggressive action that still keeps evidence safe.
