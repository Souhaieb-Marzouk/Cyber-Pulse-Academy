What you will learn here

HARD level treats T1601.002 — Downgrade System Image the way a senior analyst sees it: as a discrimination problem. The artefacts no longer announce themselves. A downgrade can be an approved vendor migration, an attacker rolling a router back to a known-weak image, or a maintenance script with a bug — and the logs look almost identical. Two options will often both look defensible; only one is right in this world.

You will work through the detection strategy the ATT&CK page describes (DET0569): image transfers over TFTP, FTP or SCP; boot variables repointed at older files; version strings that go backwards after a reload; checksums that fail against the approved baseline — and the nasty detail that an attacker can tamper with what the CLI prints, so you must validate out-of-band. You will meet real-world texture from public reporting: the 2015 Synful Knock era of modified Cisco IOS images, the DRYHOOK tooling patched into VPN appliances, and the 2025 Static Tundra reporting on exploitation of unpatched, end-of-life Cisco devices via CVE-2018-0171.

You will make the calls a senior makes: when to leave a device running to preserve evidence, when to fail over traffic, which near-miss command answers a different question, and which control in the NIST 800-53 set actually binds to this behaviour. If you can pass this file, you can take a real T1601.002 case.
