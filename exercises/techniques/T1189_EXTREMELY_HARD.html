📘 What you will learn — T1189 Drive-by Compromise

This object is a Technique in the MITRE ATT&CK framework. A technique describes how an attacker does one specific thing. T1189 describes how an attacker gets inside your company through your web browser. Its ID is T1189 and its name is Drive-by Compromise. It sits in the Initial Access tactic (TA0001) — the first step of an attack.

Here is the idea in short. You browse the web every day. An attacker infects a website — or pays for a bad advert on a real website. You visit that site during normal work. A script in the page looks at your browser and its plugins. If it finds a weak version, it attacks the browser without asking you. If the attack works, the attacker runs code on your computer with your user rights. You do not need to click anything. Visiting the page is enough. Sometimes the site asks you to "allow notifications" or to install a "browser update" — that is the trap, and it uses the User Execution technique to finish the job.

There is a special version called a watering hole (also called a strategic web compromise). The attacker does not attack a random site. They attack a site that your specific group already trusts and visits — for example a portal for your industry, a government page, or a regional news site. Then they wait, like a lion waits at a water hole. Only the visitors they want get attacked.

Do not confuse T1189 with its neighbours. T1190 (Exploit Public-Facing Application) attacks the company's own server from outside. T1189 attacks the employee's browser, so the attacker lands inside the internal network. T1566.002 (Spearphishing Link) is the email that carries the link; the attack page behind the link is T1189. T1204 (User Execution) needs the user to run a file — in T1189 the plain visit is enough.

Who uses this knowledge at work? Almost every security role. A SOC analyst triages the alerts a drive-by leaves: a proxy line, a strange child process of the browser, an identity anomaly. A threat hunter searches proxy and EDR telemetry for the pattern "uncommon fetch, then a child process, then a file drop, then a C2 call" — that is the ATT&CK detection strategy DET0176 with its analytics AN0498 to AN0501. An incident responder captures memory and preserves evidence before reimaging the machine. A CTI analyst maps campaigns to T1189 and briefs the CISO about groups such as APT28, Darkhotel or Bad Rabbit. A detection engineer writes the proxy, DNS and EDR rules. A red teamer may emulate a watering hole in an authorised exercise. A GRC or control owner checks that browsers and plugins are patched (M1051), that exploit protection is on (M1050), that browsers run sandboxed (M1048), that ad and script blockers are allowed (M1021), and that staff are trained (M1017). The daily tools are the web proxy, the EDR agent, the SIEM, DNS logs and identity-provider logs.

The risk is simple: a drive-by turns one normal page view into a foothold inside your network, and the attacker then moves to Execution, Persistence and Command and Control.
