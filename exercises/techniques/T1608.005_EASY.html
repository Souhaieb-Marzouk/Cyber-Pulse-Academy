What you will learn here

Welcome! In this exam you will learn about T1608.005 — Link Target. It is a Sub-Technique of T1608 (Stage Capabilities) and it lives in the Resource Development tactic (TA0042), on the PRE platform: everything happens before the attacker touches a victim. In plain words: before a phishing link is sent, the attacker prepares the page (or file) that the link points to — the click target.

What does that destination look like? Usually it is an HTML page. It can carry small client-side JavaScript (code that runs in the visitor's browser) that decides what content to serve. Very often the page is a cloned login page — a copy of a real sign-in screen, made to trick people into typing their passwords. Sometimes the link simply points at a hosted malware file instead.

One rule makes this sub-technique special: the victim must act. They must actively click the link, or copy and paste the URL into a browser. That is the difference from the sibling T1608.004 Drive-by Target, which needs only a visit. No click, no payload — and that deliberate click is exactly what the downstream technique T1204.001 Malicious Link describes.

You will use this in daily security work. A SOC analyst checks reported links before anyone clicks. A CTI analyst tracks phishing panels (fake login sites that collect stolen data) and records their URLs as intelligence. A threat hunter pivots on staging URLs found in internet scans. In IR (incident response) you trace where a clicked link really went. A detection engineer writes rules for the channel that carries the link, and awareness training teaches people to spot odd links and report them before a click happens.

Finally, the link itself can lie. A link shortener wraps a long URL in a short one on a widely trusted domain. A typosquat imitates a real domain with a tiny change. And display text can hide a completely different true destination. The fifteen exercises below take you from the definition to your first safe, renderless look behind a short link.
