💡 What you will learn here

This is part of the T1593.002 — Search Engines exam series. Imagine you are a new analyst. An attacker who wants to break into a company often starts with something completely innocent-looking: a search box. In this exam you will learn about a real technique from the MITRE ATT&CK framework: T1593.002 — Search Open Websites/Domains: Search Engines. It is a sub-technique — a specific child of T1593 (Search Open Websites/Domains) in the ATT&CK catalogue, which describes what real attackers do. Its job: adversaries use search engines to collect information about victims. Search engines send crawlers that read and index public web pages, and they offer special syntax — called operators (like site:, filetype:, inurl:, intitle:) — that makes searches very precise.

With plain searches an attacker learns general facts about a company. With crafted queries — often called "Google dorking", and catalogued for years in the Google Hacking Database — they can find spillage: sensitive files that were never meant to be public, such as network details, exposed directories, configuration files, or even credentials. Real actors do this: the APT41 DUST campaign used search engines to research victim servers, and Kimsuky searched Google for vulnerabilities, tools, and geopolitical trends to prepare attacks. Everything found here feeds later phases: other reconnaissance, setting up resources, and initial access with phishing or valid (leaked) accounts.

This object appears in many real jobs. CTI analysts read reports that say "the actor used advanced search operators" and map them to T1593.002. SOC analysts learn a hard truth: the searching happens on the search engine's side, outside your visibility (platform PRE), so your web logs cannot show who dorked you — the detection strategy (DET0811) points to later stages instead. Security engineers and EASM teams run the same dorks against their own domain to find indexed secrets before an attacker does, remove the files, and ask the engine to de-index them. Red teamers do this in the passive phase of engagements. You will also learn why robots.txt is not a security control (it actually lists hidden paths for attackers) and why the real fix is removing the file and requiring authentication (M1056 — Pre-compromise: minimise the sensitive data you expose).

By the end of this exam you will recognise dork-based recon in evidence, read and write basic dorks, and pick the defences that truly work.
