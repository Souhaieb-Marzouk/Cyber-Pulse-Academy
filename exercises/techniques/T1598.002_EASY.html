ⓘWhat you will learn here

This page is a Sub-Technique page in MITRE ATT&CK. It covers T1598.002, Phishing for Information: Spearphishing Attachment, a child of the parent technique T1598, Phishing for Information. In one plain sentence: the attacker sends an email with an attachment, often a form or a document, and asks the recipient to fill it in and send it back. The goal is to collect sensitive information, not to run malware.

Attackers use this technique because it is quiet and cheap. There is no exploit and no alarm on a server; the victim does the work by replying. What they get is targeting data: organisation details, staff names, contact lists, business processes, and sometimes credentials. Documented actors such as Dragonfly, SideCopy, Sidewinder and Star Blizzard have used attachment phishing to harvest credentials for later stages.

Defenders touch this technique from many roles. A SOC analyst triages user reports in the ticketing system. A mail gateway admin reads SPF, DKIM and DMARC results in the email gateway console. A threat hunter looks for one unusual sender hitting many mailboxes, an incident responder detonates attachments in a sandbox, a detection engineer writes rules like the DET0865 strategy, and a GRC control owner checks mitigations M1017 and M1054.

You will meet this technique in daily tasks: triaging a report of a strange attachment, reading Authentication-Results headers, tuning the gateway, writing the right ATT&CK label in a ticket, briefing the CISO about the risk, or reading a threat-intel report about a campaign. In this exam you work at Helios Freight, a fictional logistics company, on tickets like HF-1042 with Sam Okafor and Priya Nair.

The risk is that one reply can hand the attacker what they need for the next stage, and leaked credentials can reappear later as T1078 Valid Accounts. This exam drills the basics: the tactic and platform, the fill-and-return lure and its social-engineering levers, the email authentication checks, the safe response workflow, and the two mitigations. Read each scenario, look at any artefact, and pick the simple, correct step.
