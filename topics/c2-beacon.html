C2 Beacon

Endpoint & MalwareExtremeKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A command-and-control beacon is a small piece of code on an infected machine that quietly contacts a server the attacker controls. It checks in on a regular schedule to ask for new orders or to wait until it is needed. The word beacon comes from the idea of a signal that keeps repeating.

Why should you care?

That channel is the attacker's lifeline. It lets them send new commands, move to other machines, and steal data over days or weeks. If you cut the channel, the intrusion goes blind. Because beacons repeat on a timer, they leave a pattern in network records. Learning that pattern helps you find intruders even when all traffic is encrypted.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Harmful code gets a foothold on a machine, often through a document or a weak password.
  2. It reaches out to a server the attacker controls, using a common protocol like web traffic.
  3. The server replies with instructions, or simply says wait for now.
  4. The code waits a fixed time, plus a small random delay, then checks in again.
  5. Small amounts of stolen data may ride out along the same channel.
  6. That regular timing creates a pattern that network records can slowly reveal.

Real-world example

Think of a spy in a hotel who must report in every night at a slightly different time. Too regular, and the front desk notices. So the spy varies the minute a little. The spy never gets a long message, just a short call, a code word, and a hang-up. If the calls stop, the handler knows something is wrong. A command-and-control beacon behaves the same way. It is quiet and repeating, easy to miss one at a time, but obvious once you plot the timings on a chart.

Key takeaways

  • A beacon is the attacker's ongoing connection, not the first break-in.
  • Regular check-in timing, with small random delays, is its clearest signature.
  • Look for a process talking to the internet at a steady rhythm with no user activity.

In more detail

A command-and-control beacon is the component of an intrusion that maintains a channel between implanted code on a victim host and infrastructure the operator controls, periodically checking in for instructions or waiting to be activated. The beacon's design goal is reliability under adverse conditions: it must survive intermittent connectivity, survive the loss of individual servers, and remain inconspicuous in network and host telemetry. That produces a recognisable set of behaviours worth studying. Beacons poll on a schedule, frequently with a randomised jitter component so that intervals do not look machine-generated. They blend into permitted traffic by using common application protocols over ports that are rarely blocked, or by tunnelling through domain name resolution, and they frequently use legitimate third-party services as relay points rather than bespoke infrastructure. They also tend to fail in characteristic ways: repeated connections to a hostname that resolves to a documentation-range address or a domain registered very recently, a steady byte-size profile in both directions, TLS sessions with unusual parameters, or a process that talks to the internet at regular intervals with no user interaction. Defenders have two complementary angles. Network analytics and flow metadata can surface beaconing periodicity and long-lived low-volume channels without any payload inspection, which is valuable when traffic is encrypted end to end. Host and identity telemetry can surface which process is making the connection, under which account, and what it did immediately before and after. Because the channel is what makes dwell time possible, disrupting or detecting it early is often the single most consequential intervention available to a defender, and it is also the point at which an adversary's operational security is most exposed.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a C2 beacon does on a victim host and why periodic check-in shapes its observable behaviour.
  • Identify beaconing characteristics in flow data, including interval regularity, jitter and consistent payload sizes.
  • Recognise abuse of legitimate services and protocol tunnelling as a means of blending into permitted traffic.
  • Build detections that combine network periodicity with host process and account attribution.
  • Assess domain-age, destination reputation and TLS metadata signals while accounting for false positives from legitimate software.
  • Decide when to disrupt a channel versus continue monitoring, and justify the tradeoff in a written recommendation.
