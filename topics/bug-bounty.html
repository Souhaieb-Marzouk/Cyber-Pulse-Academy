Bug Bounty Programme

Offensive SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A bug bounty programme pays outside researchers who find security problems in a company's systems. The company writes down what may be tested and what reward is offered. Researchers report valid problems instead of misusing them. In return the company fixes the issue and usually pays a reward.

Why should you care?

Many people can look at your systems for problems, and that finds issues your own team missed. Researchers are rewarded for reporting a problem instead of using it against you. They are told clearly what is allowed and what is not. This reduces legal risk for both sides. Without clear rules, a helpful report can still turn into a lawsuit or an angry public post.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The company decides which systems researchers may test and which ones they must avoid.
  2. A policy is published with clear rules, reward ranges, and a way to report findings.
  3. Researchers test only the systems inside that scope and look for security weaknesses.
  4. They send a report with clear steps that show how the problem can be repeated.
  5. The company or a triage team checks the report and rates how serious it is.
  6. The company fixes the problem and pays a reward before any public disclosure.

Real-world example

Imagine a library that asks locksmiths to test its doors. The library first says which doors are included in the test, because some rooms hold private papers. Locksmiths open those doors and report every weak lock they find. The library repairs the locks and pays for the useful reports. It invites the public to look only after the repairs are done.

Key takeaways

  • Always agree the scope and rules in writing before any testing begins.
  • A useful report shows clear steps, the real impact, and a suggested fix.
  • Keep findings private until the company has had time to repair them.

In more detail

A bug bounty programme is a formal arrangement in which an organisation invites independent security researchers to test defined systems and pays for valid, previously unknown vulnerabilities. It is governed first by scope, which names the domains, applications, and sometimes source repositories that may be tested, and by a set of rules covering prohibited techniques, data handling, and safe harbour language that protects good-faith research from legal action. Most programmes use a triage service to validate reports, reproduce the issue, and assign a severity from a published scoring method, with rewards scaled to impact. The reporting channel matters as much as the reward, because a clear reproduction, an impact statement, and a remediation suggestion shorten the time to a fix. The common mistake is treating a programme as a replacement for internal testing: it covers only what researchers choose to look at, gives no guaranteed coverage, and finds nothing if the scope is narrow, the rewards are unrealistic, or reports sit unanswered. Another frequent error is public disclosure before a fix ships, which converts a controlled process into an incident.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a bug bounty programme is and how it differs from a penetration test.
  • Describe the role of scope, rules of engagement, and safe harbour in a programme.
  • Explain how a vulnerability report is triaged and how severity affects a reward.
  • Outline the responsible disclosure process from report to public release.
  • Identify the limits of bounty programmes as a security assurance activity.
  • Describe what an organisation needs, such as triage capacity, to run a programme well.
