G0007 APT28

G0007Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

APT28 is a tracking name for a long-running spying group. Many governments and security companies link it to Russia's military intelligence service. The same activity appears under names such as Fancy Bear, Sofacy and Forest Blizzard. Those names overlap, so treat the link as an assessment, not a proven fact.

Why should you care?

The letters A P T stand for advanced persistent threat, meaning a patient attacker with money and time. This group guesses passwords on login pages that face the internet, such as webmail and remote access portals. Weak passwords and missing multi-factor authentication (a second proof, such as a code on your phone) are what let it in. Fix those two things and you remove its easiest path.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The group sends email with a harmful file or a link to a copy of a login page.
  2. People type their password into the fake page, and the attacker now has it.
  3. The group also tries one common password on many accounts, hoping one works.
  4. With a real account, the intruder looks like a normal worker on the network.
  5. They use tools already built into the system, so no strange program appears.
  6. Mail and documents are collected slowly, often over a very long period.

Real-world example

Think of a large office with a staff entrance. A stranger stands outside and politely asks people for their door code. He also tries the same simple code on fifty different doors, because someone always picks an easy one. Once inside, he wears the same uniform as everyone else, so nobody stops him. That is why a second proof of identity matters.

Key takeaways

  • Turn on a second proof of identity for every account that faces the internet.
  • Teach staff that a login page can be a careful copy of the real one.
  • Watch for tools already on the system used at unusual times or by odd accounts.

In more detail

APT28 is a long-running suspected Russian state-sponsored intrusion set that multiple government and vendor reporting tracks attribute with moderate to high confidence to a unit of Russia's military intelligence service, commonly identified in public reporting as Unit 26165 of the GRU. Vendors have published under many overlapping names, including Fancy Bear, Sofacy, STRONTIUM, Sednit, Pawn Storm, and Forest Blizzard, and the boundaries between those labels are not perfectly consistent across publishers, so defenders should treat alias convergence as approximate rather than exact set equality. Reporting has repeatedly described the group targeting government ministries, military and defence contractors, diplomatic missions, political organisations, election-related bodies, media outlets, think tanks, and, in some campaigns, hospitality, logistics, and aviation-sector supply chains. Observed regional focus clusters around Europe, North America, the Middle East, and Central Asia, with activity also reported against organisations in the Caucasus and Eastern Europe. The tradecraft described in open reporting is broad rather than narrowly specialised: spearphishing with malicious attachments and links, credential harvesting through lookalike authentication pages, password spraying against exposed services including webmail and VPN portals, exploitation of public-facing applications, and abuse of legitimate remote services and valid accounts for access. Post-compromise behaviour attributed to the group includes extensive use of command and scripting interpreters, living-off-the-land binaries to blend into administrative activity, credential theft from browsers and mail clients, deployment of custom implant families with modular downloaders, and interest in network infrastructure such as routers and mail servers as both access vectors and collection points. Several reporting streams also describe operational security mistakes, including reused infrastructure and registration artifacts, which have supported attribution. For defenders, the practical consequence is that detection investment should concentrate on identity and authentication telemetry, public-facing service exploitation, email-borne initial access, and outbound command-and-control patterns rather than on any single malware signature. Attribution is contested in some cases and assessed with only moderate confidence in others, and some activity bundled under the label may reflect distinct sub-units or copycat operations.

What you will practise

  • Compare the tradecraft attributed to APT28 in vendor and government reporting and explain which intrusion phases carry the strongest analytic confidence.
  • Explain how password spraying against webmail, VPN, and other exposed authentication services is detected using identity telemetry rather than endpoint signatures.
  • Build a detection hypothesis for credential phishing delivered through lookalike authentication pages and evaluate where such detections produce false positives.
  • Identify the living-off-the-land binaries and scripting interpreters most often associated with this group and explain how to baseline their legitimate administrative use.
  • Assess the risk that network edge devices and mail infrastructure become both initial access vectors and long-term collection points, and prioritise hardening accordingly.
  • Construct SIEM queries that surface anomalous authentication patterns consistent with state-sponsored credential access in a hybrid identity environment.
  • Explain the supply-chain and third-party access risk described in reporting on this group and how to scope vendor trust accordingly.
  • Evaluate the limits of attribution in public reporting and describe how to write a threat assessment that states confidence levels honestly.
