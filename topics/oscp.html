Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

OSCP stands for OffSec Certified Professional. It is a hands-on exam where you break into practice machines instead of answering multiple-choice questions. You must find weaknesses, get in, gain more rights, and write a clear report. It proves you can test real systems safely.

Why should you care?

Many security jobs ask for proof that you can actually test a system, not just talk about it. OSCP gives employers that proof, because you pass it by working against live targets. Studying for it also trains habits you need daily: careful notes, patient checking, and honest reporting. Without that practice, theory alone rarely helps.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You start with reconnaissance (careful looking around), scanning the practice network to find live hosts and open services.
  2. You check each service by hand, because automated scan results often miss the detail that matters most.
  3. You use a known weakness to get a first foothold, which means a basic level of access on the machine.
  4. You then raise your rights on that host, often by finding weak settings, stored passwords or bad permissions.
  5. Inside a practice domain, you move to other machines and collect proof that you reached the goal.
  6. Finally you write a report with clear steps, evidence and advice on how to fix each issue.

Real-world example

Think of a building inspector who is paid to test the locks. The owner hands over the keys and a written list of rooms, so the work is legal and agreed. The inspector walks the corridors, tries each door, and writes down which ones open too easily. A good inspector never breaks anything and never keeps a copy of a key. That is exactly what the OSCP exam checks: method, patience and a clear write-up.

Key takeaways

  • OSCP is practical: you pass by testing real targets, not by memorising answers.
  • Build one repeatable method for looking around, and take notes as you go.
  • Always work only on systems you are allowed to test, and write findings clearly.

In more detail

The OffSec Certified Professional (OSCP) is a fully hands-on penetration testing certification that requires candidates to compromise live, isolated target machines and an Active Directory set rather than answer multiple-choice questions. It is aimed at security practitioners who already hold solid networking, Linux and Windows administration fundamentals and who want to demonstrate that they can run an end-to-end assessment against realistic targets. For an employer, the credential is treated as evidence of practical offensive skill: a passing candidate has shown they can enumerate an unfamiliar host, identify and validate a vulnerability, obtain a foothold, escalate privileges, move laterally through a domain environment, and document the whole chain in a professional report. The examination is practical, proctored and time-boxed, delivered as a supervised remote session followed by a separate reporting period in which the candidate submits a structured penetration test report with evidence, reproduction steps and remediation advice. Scoring is point-based across independent target machines and the Active Directory set, and the pass mark is a fixed point total, so candidates who plan their hours can deliberately leave some targets untouched instead of treating every machine as equally valuable. Because there is no partial credit for guesses and no theoretical safety net, preparation that consists only of reading walkthroughs rarely converts into a pass, since the exam targets reward independent enumeration. Deliberate lab practice changes the outcome: building a repeatable enumeration methodology, taking notes as you work, re-solving machines without guidance, and rehearsing report writing under time pressure are what separate a candidate who stalls on the first foothold from one who turns limited hours into the required points.

Exam domains and objectives

  • Information Gathering: enumerating hosts, services, shares and users to build an accurate picture of an unfamiliar target network before any exploitation is attempted.
  • Vulnerability Scanning and Public Exploit Research: validating automated scan output by hand, locating relevant public exploits and adapting them when they do not run unmodified.
  • Web Application Attacks: identifying and exploiting common server-side and client-side web flaws to obtain an initial foothold on a target host.
  • Client-Side Attacks: assessing user-facing delivery vectors such as document and browser-based lures, and recognising which endpoint controls reduce that risk.
  • Password Attacks: recovering or reusing credentials through offline cracking, online guessing and captured authentication material, and explaining the defensive controls that limit each route.
  • Windows Privilege Escalation: moving from a low-privileged shell to administrative rights by abusing misconfigurations, weak service permissions and exposed credentials.
  • Linux Privilege Escalation: escalating through SUID binaries, scheduled tasks, misconfigured services and leaked secrets on Linux hosts.
  • Port Redirection and Tunnelling: pivoting through a compromised host so that otherwise unreachable internal services can be assessed without breaking scope.
  • Active Directory Attacks: enumerating a domain, abusing authentication and delegation weaknesses, and chaining them toward domain-level objectives.
  • Post-Exploitation and Evidence Handling: stabilising access, collecting the artefacts that prove impact, and avoiding destructive actions on production-like targets.
  • Reporting and Professional Communication: producing a penetration test report with clear reproduction steps, evidence, business impact and prioritised remediation advice.
