Passkeys

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A passkey is a way to sign in without typing a password. Instead of a secret you remember, your phone or laptop keeps a special key that only works for one website. You unlock it with your fingerprint, your face or a device code. The website keeps the matching public part and never receives your private key.

Why should you care?

Most account break-ins happen because someone is tricked into typing a password on a fake page. A passkey cannot be typed, so a fake page has nothing useful to steal. There is also nothing to reuse, so one stolen password cannot unlock your other accounts. This removes a huge amount of everyday risk for very little effort. The risk if you ignore it is that your password remains the single point of failure for everything you own.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. On a website you choose to create a passkey instead of a password.
  2. Your device creates two linked keys, one public and one private.
  3. The website stores the public key and your device keeps the private key safe.
  4. Next time you sign in, the website sends a random challenge to your device.
  5. You unlock the device, which signs the challenge and sends the answer back.
  6. The website checks the answer with the public key and lets you in.

Real-world example

Think of a bank safe deposit box with two keys. The bank keeps one key and you keep the other, and the box only opens when both are used. A thief who copies the bank's key still cannot open the box. If you lose your key, the bank can issue a new one. It checks who you are first, with extra care.

Key takeaways

  • A passkey proves you hold a private key, so there is no password to steal or reuse.
  • Register more than one passkey per account, so a lost device does not lock you out.
  • The account recovery process becomes the weakest point once passkeys replace passwords.

In more detail

A passkey is a cryptographic credential that replaces a password for a specific account, standardised through the Web Authentication specification that is maintained by the World Wide Web Consortium with input from the FIDO Alliance. When you create a passkey, the authenticator, which may be a phone, a laptop's security chip or a hardware key, generates a public and private key pair scoped to the website's origin; the public key is registered with the service and the private key never leaves the authenticator and is used only after local user verification such as a fingerprint, a face check or a device PIN. Because the private key is bound to one origin and never transmitted, a replica site cannot replay it, which removes the shared secret that phishing and credential stuffing depend on. Passkeys come in two broad shapes: device bound, where the key cannot leave one piece of hardware, and synchronised, where the key is copied through an encrypted provider keychain so it can be used on several devices. The mistakes that undermine them are allowing a password reset to become the weak link, registering only one passkey per account so a lost device locks the user out, and confusing the encryption of a synchronised keychain with the strength of the account protecting it.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a passkey is and how it differs from a password or a one-time code.
  • Describe how a public and private key pair is created and used during sign in.
  • Explain why a passkey is resistant to phishing and to password reuse.
  • Compare device bound and synchronised passkeys and the trade-offs of each.
  • Identify the account recovery and backup authenticator practices that keep passkeys usable.
