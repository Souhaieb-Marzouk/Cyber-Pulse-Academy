G0008 Carbanak

G0008Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Carbanak is the name of a criminal group that stole money from banks. Researchers also call it Anunak. The group broke into bank computers and watched how staff worked. Then they sent fake transfers or arranged large cash withdrawals from cash machines.

Why should you care?

This group did not depend on one clever trick. They watched real staff for weeks and copied their habits. That means fraud checks based only on unusual transfers can fail. Banks and payment firms need to watch who is signed in and what tools are installed, not only the money.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. A bank employee opens an email attachment that looks like an invoice or a customer file.
  2. The attachment installs a remote control program that lets outsiders use the computer.
  3. The attackers move slowly and look for the servers that handle payments and transfers.
  4. They watch how staff perform daily tasks, including which forms they fill in.
  5. They then copy that routine to send money or to order cash from machines.
  6. They also delete logs and reuse the tools that bank administrators already trust.

Real-world example

Imagine a new cashier at a supermarket who watches the supervisor for a month. They learn the refund code, the till routine and the shift times. On a busy Saturday they issue refunds to themselves, and every step looks exactly like normal work. The manager reviews the takings at month end and sees a normal trading day. Nothing in the numbers stands out, because the routine was copied perfectly from the people who write the rules.

Key takeaways

  • Watch for remote control tools that nobody approved.
  • Money movement that looks legitimate can still be fraud, so check the person behind it.
  • Protect and monitor log records, because attackers delete them.

In more detail

Carbanak, also reported as Anunak, is a financially motivated intrusion set that has targeted banks, payment processors and later hospitality, retail and healthcare organisations. Public reporting from 2015 described the full pattern: spear phishing to gain a foothold on an employee workstation, escalation to administrative access, and then weeks of quiet observation inside the bank's own systems before any money moved. The distinctive feature was that the operators learned internal procedures well enough to imitate them, using the same remote administration tools that bank staff trusted, recording screens, and then issuing transfers or ordering cash withdrawals at branches and cash machines through accomplices. Because the money movement looked like legitimate business activity, fraud controls that only watched for external attackers missed it. Evidence includes remote control software installed without a change request, new administrative accounts, transaction records that differ from the employee's normal pattern, and deleted or altered logs. Attribution here is criminal rather than state sponsored, and law enforcement statements have described arrests connected to the group.
