G0032 Lazarus Group

G0032Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Lazarus Group is an umbrella name for a wide mix of activity that governments and security companies link to North Korean state operators. The United States has publicly blamed North Korean actors for some campaigns. Vendors split the same activity into smaller clusters with their own names. Because the label covers so much, treat any single claim with care.

Why should you care?

This group steals money as well as information, so banks, payment platforms and cryptocurrency services are targets. It has also attacked software update systems, which means a trusted download can carry an intruder. It has used destructive tools that wipe data. That combination means you must protect your data, your money paths and your suppliers all at once.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers study a target and send a message that fits, such as a job offer.
  2. The person opens a file or follows a link, and hidden code starts running.
  3. The code contacts a server and waits for instructions from the operator.
  4. The group steals passwords, then moves to other systems with normal administration tools.
  5. Sometimes they hide harmful code inside a software update that people already trust.
  6. Finally they steal money, lock files for ransom, or wipe data completely.

Real-world example

Imagine an office that orders printer paper every month from the same supplier. One month the box contains a hidden device that copies every document. Nobody questions the delivery, because the van looks right and the paperwork is correct. Trust in a familiar supplier is exactly what makes this approach so effective.

Key takeaways

  • Trusted software updates are an attack path, so check what you install.
  • Be careful with unexpected job offers and social messages at work.
  • Keep backups that attackers cannot reach, and test that they restore.

In more detail

Lazarus Group is an umbrella designator applied by vendors and governments to a broad collection of intrusion activity that public reporting attributes, with varying confidence, to North Korean state-sponsored operators. The United States government has formally attributed specific activity and campaigns to North Korean state actors under the HIDDEN COBRA label, while the vendor ecosystem splits the wider cluster into sub-clusters such as Diamond Sleet, Zinc, Labyrinth Chollima, and Guardian of Peace, each with distinct tooling and mission focus. The scope of this grouping is therefore genuinely contested: analysts disagree about which campaigns share operators, infrastructure, and funding lines, and some activity labelled Lazarus in early reporting has since been reassigned. Reporting describes a uniquely wide target set driven by revenue generation as well as espionage, including financial institutions, cryptocurrency exchanges and payment platforms, defence contractors, aerospace and engineering firms, media and entertainment companies, healthcare and pharmaceutical organisations, and government agencies. Geographic reporting concentrates on South Korea, the United States, Japan, India, and Southeast Asia, with cryptocurrency-focused activity reported globally. Described tradecraft spans spearphishing of individuals with credible lures including job offers and social media contact, exploitation of public-facing applications and widely deployed software, supply-chain compromise of trusted update mechanisms, abuse of valid credentials, custom backdoors and loader families, destructive and disruptive operations including ransomware deployment and wiper activity, and monetisation through cryptocurrency theft and the use of front companies. The group's operational diversity means defenders cannot rely on a small set of behavioural rules; the defensible common threads are supply-chain trust, third-party developer tooling, and identity-based access to financial and administrative systems.

What you will practise

  • Distinguish the government-attributed HIDDEN COBRA activity from the wider vendor-tracked sub-clusters and explain why the umbrella label is contested.
  • Analyse supply-chain compromise of software update and build mechanisms and identify the controls that would detect or prevent it.
  • Explain how social-engineering lures such as fraudulent job offers are recognised and countered in a recruitment-heavy organisation.
  • Detect destructive or disruptive activity patterns early by identifying the precursors that precede them in the intrusion lifecycle.
  • Assess the risk of third-party developer tooling and dependency compromise when defining build pipeline trust boundaries.
  • Explain how cryptocurrency and payment fraud objectives change the assets a defender must protect and monitor.
  • Prioritise detection coverage for an organisation that holds both intellectual property and financial transaction capability.
  • Evaluate the reliability of public reporting when a tracking label spans multiple distinct operational clusters.
