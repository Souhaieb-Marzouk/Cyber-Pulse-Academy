T1029 Scheduled Transfer

T1029ExfiltrationExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Scheduled transfer means sending stolen data out of a network at set times instead of all at once. The attacker chooses certain hours or a repeated interval. This technique belongs to the exfiltration tactic, which covers stealing data from a victim network. The sending itself uses another method, such as a command channel. The schedule is the part that helps the attacker stay hidden.

Why should you care?

A large sudden upload is easy to notice, so attackers spread the theft over time. Quiet hours also mean fewer people watching the alarms. A steady pattern can look like normal automation, such as a nightly backup. If you only review traffic for a single day, the pattern may never appear. This delay means data can leave your network for weeks before anyone asks a question.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker decides on a time window, such as lunchtime, nights or weekends.
  2. They set the malware to send data only during that window, often with a small delay.
  3. They pair the schedule with a transport method, such as a normal looking web request.
  4. Some malware repeats the transfer on a fixed interval, for example every few hours.
  5. Others copy the timing of real business activity, such as the nightly backup job.
  6. The transfer keeps happening quietly until the attacker has all the data they want.

Real-world example

Think of a warehouse worker who takes one small item from the loading dock every night at the same hour. One missing envelope is noticed by nobody. Over three months, a whole shelf is gone. The cameras record every visit, but each clip looks like an ordinary evening shift. Only when someone compares the clips side by side does the pattern appear. The theft was always visible, spread thin enough to look normal.

Key takeaways

  • Review network traffic over weeks, because a repeating pattern is invisible in a single day.
  • Build a baseline of normal traffic by hour and day, including backups and software updates.
  • Ask why data left at a time when no one was working, even if the amount was small.

In more detail

Scheduled transfer is the timing half of exfiltration: rather than dumping data the moment it is collected, the adversary constrains when traffic leaves so that the pattern of activity resembles something the environment already produces. Two variants are common. Time of day scheduling places transfers inside busy business hours, where they hide among ordinary cloud and backup traffic, or deliberately inside quiet windows such as nights and weekends when analyst coverage is thin. Interval scheduling fires on a fixed period, a technique that produces metronomic regularity in network flow records and is easy to overlook in a single session but obvious in a week of data. The technique does not move data by itself, so it is always paired with a transport such as a command and control channel or an alternative protocol, and the scheduling logic usually lives in the implant. Detection therefore depends on baselining volume and destinations by hour and day, then looking for periodic flows that no scheduled job explains, transfers that start when no user is active, or a steady drip of uploads from a host that has no business sending data outward. Comparing transfer times with the organisation's own backup, patching and synchronisation windows separates routine automation from an adversary's clock.

What you will practise

  • Explain how restricting exfiltration to certain times or intervals helps an adversary avoid attention.
  • Describe both time of day scheduling and fixed interval scheduling and how each appears in network data.
  • Explain why this technique must be combined with a transport technique such as exfiltration over a command channel.
  • Identify the log sources and baselines, including flow records and proxy logs, needed to spot periodic transfers.
  • Distinguish an adversary's schedule from legitimate scheduled jobs such as backups and synchronisation.
