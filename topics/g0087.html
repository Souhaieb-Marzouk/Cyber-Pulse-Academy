G0087 APT39

G0087Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

APT39 is a tracking name for a spying group that security companies link to Iran's intelligence ministry. It is also called Chafer and Remexi. The group targets phone companies and travel businesses, because those hold call records and travel plans. The country link is a company assessment, not a proven fact.

Why should you care?

Telephone records and travel bookings reveal who talks to whom and who goes where. That is valuable intelligence. This group collects slowly and quietly, so alarms based on large data volumes often stay silent. It also reuses tools and servers shared with other groups. That makes simple blame unreliable, so focus on behaviour inside your own network.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. An email with a harmful attachment or a link starts the intrusion.
  2. A backdoor is installed and contacts its server a few times each day.
  3. The group then steals passwords kept by web browsers and mail programs.
  4. They add a scheduled task so their access returns after a restart.
  5. They read mail and copy records in small amounts, avoiding large transfers.
  6. Collection can continue for many months before anyone notices that anything is odd.

Real-world example

Imagine a librarian who wants the information in a private archive. He never carries out a big box of files. Each night he photocopies two or three pages and puts everything back exactly as it was. The shelves look untouched, and the daily totals look normal. Only a pattern over many months gives him away.

Key takeaways

  • Look for small, regular outbound connections rather than huge transfers.
  • Protect saved browser and mail passwords on every managed device.
  • Shared tools and servers make blame hard, so trust your own evidence.

In more detail

APT39 is a long-running espionage intrusion set that multiple commercial threat intelligence vendors have assessed as linked to the Iranian Ministry of Intelligence and Security, an assessment that rests on infrastructure overlap, targeting patterns, and shared tooling rather than on any public technical proof, so it should be treated as a vendor judgement of moderate to high confidence rather than an established fact. Reporting describes sustained targeting of telecommunications providers and travel-related industries, with secondary interest in aviation, hospitality, and IT service providers, and victim organisations identified across the Middle East, Europe, Asia, and North America. The operational logic attributed to the group is intelligence collection rather than disruption: telecommunications metadata and subscriber information, and travel itineraries and booking systems, have both been described as collection priorities in vendor reporting. Tradecraft commonly described includes spearphishing for initial access, use of custom backdoors alongside commercially available or commodity remote access tools, credential theft from browsers and mail clients, and persistence via scheduled tasks and autostart mechanisms. The group is also reported to reuse infrastructure and tooling associated with other Iran-nexus clusters, which complicates attribution and means indicators frequently overlap across multiple tracked actors. For defenders, the reliable signal is behavioural rather than infrastructural: unexpected outbound beaconing from endpoints in telecom or travel environments, mail client credential store access, and the presence of remote administration tooling that the organisation has no legitimate reason to deploy. Detection programmes should also account for the group's history of long dwell times and low-volume collection, which makes volume-based alerting weak.

What you will practise

  • Describe the collection priorities attributed to Iran-nexus espionage activity against telecommunications and travel sectors.
  • Recognise why attribution based on infrastructure overlap carries lower confidence than attribution based on unique tooling.
  • Build detections for credential theft from browser and mail client stores on managed endpoints.
  • Hunt for low-volume, long-dwell command and control beaconing in environments where volume thresholds are ineffective.
  • Explain how commodity remote access tools are abused for persistence and why allow-listing is fragile.
  • Assess detection coverage gaps that arise when an actor reuses infrastructure shared with other tracked clusters.
  • Prioritise logging sources for a telecommunications or travel-sector environment facing state-sponsored espionage.
