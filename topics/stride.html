STRIDE

Secure EngineeringHardKeyword

STRIDE is a threat enumeration mnemonic that groups threats into six categories: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege. It was developed so that designers without a deep attack background could still systematically ask the right questions about each element of a data-flow diagram. The categories map neatly onto security properties, which is what makes the framework teachable: spoofing attacks authenticity, tampering attacks integrity, repudiation attacks non-repudiation of actions, information disclosure attacks confidentiality, denial of service attacks availability and elevation of privilege attacks authorisation. In practice the mnemonic is applied per element and per flow, with a common convention that processes, data stores, data flows, external entities and trust boundaries each attract a characteristic subset of the six. For a practising engineer STRIDE is most useful as a completeness check rather than a rating scheme, because it deliberately says nothing about likelihood or impact and produces no prioritisation on its own. Teams normally pair it with a scoring method and with attack-library mapping to connect abstract categories to observed adversary behaviour. The known weaknesses are worth stating plainly. STRIDE was framed for classical component-and-network designs and fits cloud identity, serverless and machine-learning pipelines awkwardly. It has no privacy or supply-chain category. Its output is highly dependent on the facilitator's skill, and groups tend to over-report the categories they understand and under-report repudiation and availability. Reviewing a STRIDE session for concrete preconditions and observable failure modes is the cheapest way to raise its quality.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to name the six STRIDE categories and state which security property each one attacks.
  • You will be able to apply the mnemonic element by element to a data-flow diagram without missing entire categories.
  • You will be able to explain the conventional mapping between diagram element types and the STRIDE categories most likely to apply.
  • You will be able to convert a bare STRIDE label into a concrete threat statement with preconditions and impact.
  • You will be able to identify design situations where STRIDE fits poorly, such as cloud identity graphs and machine-learning pipelines.
  • You will be able to critique a completed STRIDE worksheet for over-reporting and under-reporting bias.
