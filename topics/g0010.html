G0010 Turla

G0010Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Turla is a tracking name for one of the oldest known spying groups. Several governments and security companies link it to Russian state security services. It is famous for hiding its secret communications inside normal network traffic. The same reporting describes very long, quiet stays inside victim networks. The link is an assessment, not a proven fact.

Why should you care?

Turla hides its control messages inside ordinary internet lookups and other normal traffic. A firewall (a guard that checks network traffic) may wave this through, because it looks harmless. The group also stays for years and collects slowly, so alarms based on large volumes stay silent. You need to understand normal traffic before you can spot the strange parts.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The intruders get a first foothold, often through email or a stolen password.
  2. They install a hidden program that hides deep in the operating system.
  3. That program wraps its secret messages inside requests that look completely normal.
  4. Because the traffic looks ordinary, simple filters and block lists miss it.
  5. The attackers may also take over servers owned by other people to hide their origin.
  6. They quietly collect documents for years, and they almost never rush their work.

Real-world example

Imagine a delivery van that arrives at a warehouse every day. On the outside it looks like every other van, with normal boxes and normal paperwork. Inside one box, hidden between layers of cardboard, is a small message. Guards who check only the outside of the van never see it. To catch this, you must open a few boxes.

Key takeaways

  • Learn what normal network traffic looks like, so quiet oddities stand out.
  • Watch for connections that last a very long time to unexpected places.
  • Spying campaigns can last years, so keep your logs for a long time.

In more detail

Turla is one of the longest-documented espionage intrusion sets, assessed by multiple governments and vendors as associated with Russian state security services, most commonly linked in public reporting to the Federal Security Service. That association rests on third-party analysis of infrastructure, tooling, and targeting, and although several national authorities have stated it with high confidence, it remains an attribution judgement rather than an observable technical fact. Reporting describes targeting of government and diplomatic entities, military organisations, research and defence-industrial bodies, and, in later campaigns, entities in Europe, the Middle East, Asia, and North America. The group is known in public reporting for technically distinctive tradecraft: satellite-based and hijacked-network command and control paths, including abuse of other actors' infrastructure; the Snake framework, a long-lived modular implant documented extensively in national advisories; DNS-based covert channels; custom rootkit components; and the use of compromised legitimate websites and cloud services to blend with normal traffic. It has also been reported to co-opt and operate implants on networks already compromised by other intrusion sets, which means victim telemetry may show two distinct actors and incident scope must be established carefully. For defenders, the group's emphasis on low-and-slow collection and network-layer concealment makes host-only monitoring insufficient. Detection effort should include DNS anomaly analysis, hunting for long-lived encrypted sessions to unexpected destinations, review of egress paths for unauthorised tunnels, and integrity monitoring of core system components. Attribution of this cluster should be presented with explicit confidence language, and the overlapping vendor naming schemes should be reconciled before comparing campaigns.

What you will practise

  • Describe how hijacked network infrastructure and DNS covert channels complicate network-layer attribution.
  • Build detections for DNS tunnelling indicators and long-lived anomalous encrypted egress sessions.
  • Explain the operational risk of acting on a network that a second, unrelated intrusion set has also compromised.
  • Assess endpoint integrity monitoring options for detecting kernel-level or boot-level persistence.
  • Evaluate egress filtering and outbound allow-listing as controls against covert channel abuse.
  • Interpret national advisory attribution language and translate confidence levels accurately into internal reporting.
  • Prioritise log sources for a low-and-slow espionage threat with multi-year dwell time.
  • Explain why decommissioning a legacy implant requires both host remediation and infrastructure-side action.
