TLS Inspection

Network SecurityVery hardKeyword

TLS inspection is the practice of terminating or intercepting encrypted sessions inside a controlled boundary so that security controls can examine the plaintext that would otherwise be opaque. Because most malicious traffic now uses the same transport encryption as legitimate traffic, signature-based network detection, data loss prevention and URL filtering are blind without decryption. Two broad techniques exist. An active proxy terminates the client connection, presents a certificate the client must trust, and opens a separate connection to the server, while passive decryption uses key material or session keys supplied out of band to decrypt copies of traffic without altering the flow. Both depend on trust: active interception requires a private certificate authority whose root is installed on every endpoint, which makes that authority a high-value target and a frequent source of operational breakage when applications use certificate pinning, mutual TLS or embedded trust stores. Inspection is not free. Decryption consumes substantial processor and memory capacity, so throughput, latency and sizing change materially, and uninspected encrypted traffic becomes a growing blind spot as the inspection ratio falls. Privacy and legal constraints are equally important, because intercepting employee banking, health or personal traffic carries regulatory exposure in many jurisdictions, and an internal certificate authority must be protected, logged and audited like any other signing authority. Bypass categories are necessary for pinned applications, software update channels and regulated third parties, and each bypass should have a documented owner. Monitoring must cover the inspection platform itself, since an expired root certificate, a failed cipher negotiation or a silent fail-open policy can disable decryption without anyone noticing.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to compare active interception with passive decryption and state the trust requirements of each.
  • You will be able to explain why certificate pinning and mutual TLS break inspection and how to handle the exceptions.
  • You will be able to size and monitor a decryption platform for capacity, latency and fail-mode behaviour.
  • You will be able to define and document bypass categories with owners and review dates.
  • You will be able to detect a silent drop in inspection coverage and treat it as a control failure.
  • You will be able to describe the privacy, legal and key-protection obligations created by internal interception.
