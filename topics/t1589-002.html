T1589.002 Gather Victim Identity Information: Email Addresses

T1589.002ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

An email address is how you reach a person at work. This technique is about attackers collecting those addresses for a target company. They then know exactly who to send harmful messages to.

Why should you care?

Confirmed addresses greatly improve the chance that a harmful message is opened. Attackers can also test thousands of guessed addresses against your sign in service. The failures look like noise, which makes them easy to miss unless you watch for the pattern.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker first looks for any public email address from your organisation.
  2. They work out the address format from that example, such as first name plus surname here.
  3. They then generate a large list of likely addresses for the real employees.
  4. They check the finished list against sign in or directory services to see which ones exist.
  5. The confirmed addresses are used for messages or repeated sign in attempts.
  6. Your logs show many requests and failed sign ins from one source in a short time.

Real-world example

Think of a hotel with rooms numbered by floor and position. Once you know that room 412 is on the fourth floor, you can guess every other room number in the building. You still have to try the doors to learn which rooms are occupied, and that knocking is what the front desk notices.

Key takeaways

  • One public address is enough to reveal your whole naming pattern.
  • Many failed sign ins across many accounts can mean address enumeration.
  • Avoid obvious shared formats for accounts that the internet can reach.

In more detail

This sub-technique covers collecting the email addresses of a target organisation's staff, which is the step that converts a company name into a working target list. Collection is usually passive first. Mail addresses appear in press releases, support pages, conference slide decks, professional networking profiles, code commit histories, and document metadata. Simple patterns can then be inferred: once one address is known, the format used for everyone else usually follows, whether that is first name, first initial plus surname, or a department prefix. Active collection is where the useful telemetry lives. Sign in and identity endpoints frequently behave differently for a valid account than for an invalid one, which allows an attacker to confirm whether a guessed address exists. Older authentication endpoints and directory lookup services are common choices for this probing because they respond in distinguishable ways. The output feeds two follow on behaviours: bulk phishing to confirmed live mailboxes, and password spraying against confirmed accounts, which generates a broad, shallow pattern of failures across many users that stands out clearly in identity logs. Defenders can reduce the guessing surface by avoiding predictable address formats for externally reachable services, and can detect the probing by looking at unusual volumes of requests to authentication endpoints from single sources or through anonymous network paths.
