G0133 Nomadic Octopus

G0133Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Nomadic Octopus is a name researchers use for a group that spies on government and phone company staff, mainly in Central Asia. Public reports link it to interests in the region. It often starts with a friendly message on social media, not with a technical attack.

Why should you care?

The target is often a person's phone, which holds messages, photos, calls and location. That is private life as well as work life. The fake app looks and works like a popular chat program, so it is hard to notice. Because the first step is a friendly conversation, technology alone cannot stop it; awareness and careful installation habits can.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker creates a believable profile on a social network or chat service.
  2. They start a friendly conversation and slowly build a relationship with the target.
  3. They suggest installing a chat app, claiming the usual one does not work well.
  4. The file is a copy of a real app with hidden collection features added.
  5. The victim grants permissions for messages, contacts and location without suspicion.
  6. The app sends private data to the attacker while the chat keeps working normally.

Real-world example

Imagine a stranger at a bus stop who becomes a friendly face over several weeks. One day they hand you a phone charger as a gift. It works perfectly and charges your phone, but it also copies your messages each night. You trusted the person, not the device. That is why attackers invest weeks in a relationship before they ever send a file.

Key takeaways

  • Install apps only from official stores, and never from a link sent in a chat.
  • Review app permissions often, because a chat app rarely needs your call logs.
  • A friendly online relationship can be the first stage of an attack on your device.

In more detail

Nomadic Octopus is a espionage cluster that public reporting associates with Central Asian interests and that has been tracked targeting government, diplomatic and telecommunications organisations, most notably in Afghanistan and neighbouring countries. Reporting describes the group using social engineering as its primary entry method: fake profiles on social networks and messaging platforms, often posing as young women or as employees of a relevant organisation, followed by delivery of a malicious application that impersonates a popular messaging client. On mobile devices the payload family has been reported to request broad permissions and to collect messages, contacts, call logs, location and audio, making the phone itself the collection platform rather than a stepping stone. Analysts have noted remote template injection in documents and the use of legitimate hosting for staging and control. Attribution is stated with limited confidence in public reporting, and the group's activity overlaps with other tracked clusters in the region, so which intrusion belongs to which operator is often unclear. Evidence is found in application installation records, unusual permission grants, messaging metadata and proxy logs, not in distinctive malware signatures.

What you will practise

  • Explain how fake social media profiles are used to deliver mobile malware.
  • Describe the permissions that reveal an application is collecting more than it needs.
  • Identify mobile device artefacts that show an unauthorised application was installed.
  • Explain why mobile collection is an end goal rather than only a stepping stone.
  • State how public reporting qualifies attribution for this cluster.
