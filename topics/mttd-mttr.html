MTTD and MTTR

SOC & Detection EngineeringHardKeyword

Mean Time to Detect and Mean Time to Respond are the two headline measures of security operations performance. Mean Time to Detect measures the elapsed interval between when malicious activity actually began and when it was first identified, while Mean Time to Respond measures the interval from identification to containment or resolution, depending on how the organisation defines the endpoint. Both are averages and both are therefore easily misread. A mean computed over a handful of incidents is dominated by outliers, so distributions, medians and percentiles communicate far more than a single number. The definitions themselves are the hardest part. Detection time cannot be measured from the alert timestamp unless you also determine when the activity started, which usually requires retrospective log analysis and often moves the start earlier once the incident is understood. Response end is equally ambiguous: is it containment of the immediate threat, eradication, restoration of service, or case closure. Without a written definition agreed with the business, teams optimise a metric they cannot compare over time. The measures are also gameable. Suppressing noisy detections lowers alert volume and can appear to improve response time while degrading detection. Closing cases quickly without corroboration improves the average and hides unresolved risk. The genuinely useful approach is to pair times with quality measures: the proportion of incidents found by internal detection rather than external notification, the fraction detected at each ATT&CK stage, dwell time distributions, and containment precision. Tracking MTTD and MTTR by incident class and detection source, and reviewing the specific incidents that set the extremes, converts an executive number into an engineering feedback loop.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to define detection start, detection point, containment and closure precisely enough that the metric is comparable over time.
  • You will be able to explain why means alone mislead and use medians, percentiles and distributions when reporting response performance.
  • You will be able to compute dwell time for an incident by reconstructing the true start of activity from retrospective log evidence.
  • You will be able to identify how suppressing alerts or closing cases prematurely can artificially improve these metrics.
  • You will be able to complement timing metrics with detection-source and coverage-quality measures such as internal versus external discovery.
  • You will be able to segment MTTD and MTTR by incident class and use the worst cases to drive specific detection or playbook improvements.
