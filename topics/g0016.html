G0016 APT29

G0016Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

APT29 is a long-running intrusion set that multiple government and vendor reporting streams attribute with moderate to high confidence to a Russian foreign intelligence service, commonly identified in public reporting as the SVR. Published under names including Cozy Bear, The Dukes, NOBELIUM, Midnight Blizzard, YTTRIUM, and Iron Hemlock, the aliases reflect overlapping but not identical scopes as different publishers refined their tracking. Reporting describes sustained targeting of government agencies, diplomatic and foreign-policy institutions, think tanks, healthcare and research organisations, technology and managed service providers, and, in several documented waves, supply chains where a trusted software vendor or cloud service provider becomes the means of reaching downstream victims. The geographic emphasis in reporting is North America, Europe, and multilateral diplomatic organisations, with activity also observed against organisations in Asia and the Middle East. The tradecraft emphasised in public reporting is patient and identity-centric: password spraying and credential stuffing against exposed authentication endpoints, abuse of valid accounts including service and application identities, heavy use of cloud and identity provider APIs for discovery and persistence, token and certificate theft in some described cases, and custom implant families known for long dwell and low-noise lateral movement. Reporting has repeatedly highlighted novel persistence paths added to cloud identity platforms and the use of trusted service providers for command and control, which means endpoint-centric detection alone is insufficient. Analysts have also documented the group's preference for operating within existing administrative tooling and protocols rather than deploying conspicuous malware, complicating triage for teams whose detection strategy is signature-driven. Attribution of any given intrusion is often assessed at moderate confidence, and some activity published under these aliases may involve different operators or be contested, so defenders should distinguish between the confidence in an attribution and the confidence in the observed behaviour when writing assessments and setting detection priorities.

What you will practise

  • Explain how supply-chain and managed-service-provider access changes the victim's detection perimeter and what telemetry must be collected to compensate.
  • Detect password spraying and credential stuffing against cloud identity providers using sign-in risk, failure distribution, and user-agent telemetry.
  • Analyse cloud and identity provider audit logs to identify persistence mechanisms created through APIs rather than endpoint malware.
  • Explain how token, certificate, and application credential theft is detected and what revocation steps limit blast radius.
  • Evaluate detection coverage for adversary activity that relies on legitimate administrative tooling and explain where gaps remain.
  • Compare public reporting on this group's cloud tradecraft and identify which observables are durable versus environment-specific.
  • Build a threat hunting hypothesis for trusted service provider command and control and describe the network evidence that would confirm or refute it.
  • Draft a confidence-qualified attribution statement that separates observed behaviour from assessed sponsorship.
