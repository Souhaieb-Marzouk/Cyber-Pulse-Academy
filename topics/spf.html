SPF

Email SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

SPF, the Sender Policy Framework, is a public list that says which computers may send email for your domain. Your domain is the part after the at sign in an address. Receivers can check that list before they trust a message.

Why should you care?

Anyone can type your domain into the sender field of an email. Without a list like SPF, receivers cannot tell your real mail from a forgery. SPF helps other mail systems notice messages sent from servers you never approved. It reduces spam and impersonation, although it cannot stop every fake message.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The domain owner writes a short text record listing the approved sending servers.
  2. That record is published in the domain name system for the whole world to read.
  3. When mail arrives, the receiving server reads the address in the envelope sender.
  4. It looks up that domain and finds the published list of approved senders.
  5. It compares the connecting address of the sending computer with the entries in the list.
  6. The result, such as pass or fail, is recorded and used by the receiver's own policy.

Real-world example

Think of a post office that stamps every parcel with the sender address. Imagine the town keeps a public noticeboard listing which shops may use the town name as a return address. When a parcel arrives, the clerk checks the noticeboard. If the shop is listed, the parcel passes. If a stranger used the town name, the clerk sees a mismatch and marks it. The noticeboard is the SPF record, and the clerk is the receiving mail server. The clerk still uses judgement about what to do next.

Key takeaways

  • SPF checks the sending server, so it is one signal and not a full guarantee.
  • Keep the number of lookups small, because a long record fails to load.
  • Combine SPF with other email checks, since forwarding can break its result.

In more detail

SPF, the Sender Policy Framework, is an email authentication method that lets a domain owner publish, in the domain name system, the list of hosts authorised to send mail using that domain in the envelope sender address. A receiving server reads the envelope sender, looks the domain up in DNS, retrieves the SPF record as a text record, and evaluates the connecting client address against the listed mechanisms. It returns one of several results: pass, fail, softfail, neutral, none, permerror or temperror. SPF constrains only the envelope sender and the sending host; it does not protect the visible From header, does not survive forwarding, and does not by itself tell the receiver what to do with a failure, since that depends on policy that the receiver chooses. It breaks when a domain exceeds the ten DNS lookup limit or when mail is relayed through a service the record does not list. The common mistake is believing an SPF pass proves the message is legitimate: an attacker can register a lookalike domain, publish a valid record for it, and pass every check.

What you will practise

  • Define SPF and explain what it authorises using a domain name.
  • Describe how a receiving server evaluates an SPF record and what results it returns.
  • Explain why SPF alone does not prove that a message is legitimate.
  • Identify common causes of SPF failure, such as forwarding and lookup limits.
  • Recognise why SPF is one part of a wider email authentication setup.
