G1035 Winter Vivern

G1035Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Winter Vivern is a name for a group that steals email. Its targets are government and military offices, mainly in Europe and Central Asia. Public reports link it to Russian-speaking interests. It focuses on webmail, which is email you read in a browser. The same group appears in other reports under different names, such as TA473.

Why should you care?

Email holds plans, contacts and passwords, so a stolen mailbox is very valuable. This group is patient and quietly returns many times. It often succeeds on systems that are not updated or not configured well, not on brand-new unknown flaws. That means ordinary maintenance work is a real defence, and it is one you can start today. Reporting on who runs the group is careful rather than certain, so you should judge the behaviour you see.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker scans for webmail systems that are open to the internet.
  2. They look for missing updates or settings that let small harmful programs run inside the page.
  3. Those small programs can read the session, so they collect login details as the user works.
  4. Sometimes the attacker instead copies a real inbox page to trick people into typing a password.
  5. They sign in, read mail, add forwarding rules, and stay quiet for a long time.
  6. They then email the victim's real contacts from a name those contacts already trust.

Real-world example

Think of a shared noticeboard in an office lobby. Someone pins a note onto the board that looks like an official announcement. People read it, trust it, and act on it. The board itself is fine, and no lock was broken. The attack worked because the board accepted whatever was pinned to it. Readers also assumed that anything on that board came from the company. That is how harmful content reaches users through a web page they already trust.

Key takeaways

  • Keep webmail and mail server software updated, because most successful attacks use known gaps.
  • Review mailbox rules and login locations often, and raise an alert for sign-ins from new countries.
  • A message from a real colleague can still be hostile if that account was taken over.

In more detail

Winter Vivern is an espionage cluster that public reporting associates with Russian-speaking interests and that has targeted government, military, diplomatic and telecommunications organisations in Europe and Central Asia, with a notable emphasis on webmail platforms. The group's reported signature is patient espionage against mail servers rather than noisy intrusion. Analysts have described exploitation of cross-site scripting weaknesses in webmail interfaces, credential harvesting through lookalike pages that mimic those interfaces, and the reuse of compromised mailboxes belonging to real contacts to send further messages. Researchers note that the tooling is comparatively lightweight and that the group succeeds through persistence, reconnaissance and the value of mail access itself rather than through advanced malware. Attribution is stated with moderate confidence in public reporting, and the group's name overlaps with designations used by other vendors, so defenders should compare behaviours instead of relying on a label. Evidence appears in mail server logs, authentication to webmail from unexpected locations, mailbox rules or forwarding entries created without a user request, and scripts or templates left in the mail platform. A common misunderstanding is that webmail exploitation requires unknown flaws; most reported cases involved patched-late or misconfigured systems, and ordinary maintenance removes most of the exposure.

What you will practise

  • Explain how cross-site scripting in a webmail interface can be turned against its own users.
  • Describe the evidence of mailbox compromise visible in mail server and authentication logs.
  • Explain why patching and secure configuration defeat most reported webmail exploitation.
  • Describe how a compromised mailbox becomes a trusted platform for further phishing.
  • State how public attribution for this cluster is qualified and why names overlap between vendors.
  • Outline an investigation checklist for a suspected mailbox takeover.
