G0059 Magic Hound

G0059Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Magic Hound is one of several names for a group that spies on governments and universities. Other companies call the group Charming Kitten, Phosphorus, or Mint Sandstorm. Public reports link this activity to Iranian state interests. They rely mostly on conversation and trickery, not on software flaws.

Why should you care?

This group attacks people, not machines. They may talk to you for weeks before sending a harmful link. A quick glance at one message will not help you. They also try to weaken your second login step, which protects a stolen password. Their patience is the main reason to take slow, odd contact seriously.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker chooses a person with useful access, such as a researcher or official.
  2. A fake profile or an impersonated colleague starts a friendly conversation.
  3. Trust grows over days or weeks, often through several messages.
  4. A link leads to a fake login page, or a file is sent as part of the chat.
  5. The attacker tries to register a new login method on the stolen account.
  6. With account access, they read mail, collect documents, and reach colleagues.

Real-world example

Picture a hotel reception desk. A caller says he is a guest in room 412 and has locked himself out. He is polite, knows the room number, and chats about the weather while the clerk checks the system. The clerk hands over a key card without seeing a face. The long friendly phone call is the attack, and the key card is your login.

Key takeaways

  • Long, friendly, unsolicited contact is a warning sign, not just politeness.
  • Review the login methods registered on your accounts from time to time.
  • Report unusual approaches, even when no file or link was sent.

In more detail

Magic Hound is a long-running espionage cluster that public reporting links with Iranian state interests and that vendors track as APT35, Charming Kitten, Phosphorus, Mint Sandstorm, or TA453. Reporting consistently describes social engineering as the primary access method rather than exploitation: prolonged correspondence with researchers, journalists, and government staff, fake personas and impersonated colleagues, credential harvesting pages, and multi factor authentication fatigue or enrolment trickery. Public analyses also describe the use of legitimate remote management and administration utilities for access and movement, alongside custom PowerShell-based tooling. The operational patience is the notable feature; some reported engagements involve weeks of harmless conversation before any malicious file appears. The misunderstanding to avoid is treating this as a purely email problem. Evidence appears across identity logs, mailbox rules, device enrolment records, and endpoint process ancestry, and the strongest early warning is often an unexpected sign-in or a new authentication method registered on an account.

What you will practise

  • Describe how long-term social engineering builds trust before a technical lure.
  • Explain authentication method registration abuse against multi factor authentication.
  • Identify identity log evidence of an account takeover in progress.
  • Explain why reporting suspicious contact matters before a link is clicked.
  • Attribute activity using public reporting language rather than firm assertions.
