TA0004 Privilege Escalation

TA0004EnterpriseExtremeTactic

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Privilege escalation, often shortened to priv esc, is how an attacker turns a normal user account into a much more powerful one. A standard account can read some files. An administrator or domain account can change almost anything. Escalation is the bridge between those two levels.

Why should you care?

One ordinary account is a small problem. One administrator account can undo most of your protections. Attackers need this step to disable security tools, reach servers and steal the data worth taking. Much of it is not clever hacking at all. It is simply a permission that was handed out too freely and never reviewed.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker holds a low-privilege account, perhaps one employee login or one compromised laptop.
  2. They look for weaknesses, such as an old unpatched program or a service running with high rights.
  3. They use that weakness to gain more control, or they simply use rights that were granted too generously.
  4. They may steal another user's session token, which is a temporary proof of identity.
  5. Now they can read more, change settings and prepare the next stage of the attack.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital. A visitor badge opens the front lobby and the coffee shop. A staff badge opens the wards and the medicine store. If someone finds a staff badge left on a desk, they do not need to break any lock. They simply walk through doors that now open for them. Nothing about them changed. Only their level of access changed, and that is exactly what privilege escalation is.

Key takeaways

  • Privilege escalation turns a small foothold on one machine into real power.
  • Most escalation simply uses permissions that were set far too generously.
  • Give people the fewest rights they need, and review them often.

In more detail

Privilege Escalation is the tactic covering the steps an adversary takes to move from a foothold with ordinary user rights to something far more powerful, whether that means local administrator on one host, domain-wide control, or an equivalent role in a cloud tenant. Behaviours include exploiting misconfigured services and file permissions, abusing elevation control mechanisms such as user account control bypasses, injecting code into higher-privileged processes, stealing tokens, taking advantage of overly broad sudo or role assignments, and exploiting vulnerable kernel or driver components. The defender's view differs sharply by platform. On endpoints, escalation generates process access events, token manipulation, unusual child processes of privileged services, new service or driver installation, and integrity-level changes. In identity and cloud environments it appears as role assumption, policy attachment, group membership change, or a sudden expansion of what a principal can do. This is one of the hardest tactics to detect in isolation because legitimate administration produces the same primitives, and because many escalations are simply the use of permissions that were granted too generously rather than an obvious exploitation event. Meaningful detection requires modelling what each principal should be able to do, alerting on capability changes rather than individual actions, and correlating an escalation with whatever access it enabled next, such as credential dumping or lateral movement.

What you will practise

  • Map the privilege escalation paths that matter most on Windows, Linux, and major cloud platforms, and explain how each is normally abused.
  • Explain what process access, token, and integrity-level telemetry reveals about escalation attempts and where it is blind.
  • Model expected privileges per principal so that capability expansion is detectable without relying on signature-style rules.
  • Correlate an escalation event with the credential access or lateral movement that follows it to establish intent and impact.
  • Identify the configuration weaknesses, excessive permissions, and unpatched components that make escalation possible in the first place.
  • Prioritise hardening actions by realistic exploitability rather than by scanner severity alone.
