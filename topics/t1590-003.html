T1590.003 Gather Victim Network Information: Network Trust Dependencies

T1590.003ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This sub-technique is about learning who your network trusts. It looks for partners, suppliers, and support companies that can connect to your systems. Those trusted links interest an attacker because the partner may already have the access they want.

Why should you care?

Your security is only as strong as the outside companies you let in. A supplier with a connection to your network is a path an attacker can borrow. Partner accounts are often watched less closely than your own staff accounts. If you do not know every trusted link, you cannot monitor or close them.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker reads public material, such as contract awards and news, to find your main suppliers.
  2. They look for support companies that advertise remote access or managed security services to you.
  3. They may send emails that pretend to be a supplier and ask staff about shared systems.
  4. They check whether partner systems share naming, certificates, or address ranges with yours.
  5. They test logins from partner address ranges against your remote access services.
  6. Any link that already trusts them becomes a route that avoids your outer defences.

Real-world example

Think of an office block where the front door is locked and guarded. The landlord also gives keys to a cleaner, a plumber, and an information technology (IT) support company. Those people come and go without signing in at the front desk. A stranger who wants to enter learns the names of these helpers, and may even borrow a cleaner's uniform. The guard at the front door sees nothing unusual, because the visitor looks expected. Trusted network links work the same way. Attackers map your suppliers so they can arrive looking like one of them. Your watchlist has to include those partners, not only your own staff.

Key takeaways

  • Keep a written list of every outside company with network access, and review it regularly.
  • Log and review partner connections, because they may bypass your normal front door.
  • Teach staff to confirm supplier requests using a known contact, not the contact given in the message.

In more detail

This sub-technique is about mapping the trust relationships that let outside organisations reach into a victim network, such as managed service providers, contractors, and support firms that hold connected and often privileged access. It is collection about relationships rather than about addressing, and much of it is satisfied by legally available material: partner and case study pages, contract awards, conference talks, job adverts naming a vendor, and social media posts about joint projects. Where public data is thin, adversaries move to elicitation through phishing for information, contacting staff while posing as an existing supplier to confirm which systems are shared. The victim side sees little host telemetry; the artefacts surface later as authentication attempts from partner-owned address ranges, vendor portal logins at unusual hours, or change requests that arrive outside normal support channels. The practical detection problem is that legitimate vendor access looks almost identical, so allow-listing, source network attribution, and ticket correlation carry the detection weight.

What you will practise

  • Explain what network trust dependencies are and why they differ from other network information.
  • Identify public sources that reveal an organisation's suppliers and support providers.
  • Describe how elicitation by email is used to confirm shared systems and access.
  • Recognise the telemetry that reveals misuse of trusted partner access.
  • Propose controls that keep third-party access visible and reviewed.
