True and False Positives

SOC & Detection EngineeringHardKeyword

A true positive is an alert that correctly identifies the behaviour the detection was written to find; a false positive is an alert raised on activity that does not match that behaviour. The pairs that matter operationally are richer than that binary. A true positive may still be benign in context, which is why analysts distinguish a detection that fired correctly on authorised administrative work from one that fired on genuine adversary activity. A false negative is the more dangerous case: malicious behaviour occurred and nothing alerted, either because the log source was absent, the rule logic did not cover the variant, or the telemetry was normalised into a shape the rule never matched. Detection engineers therefore evaluate content on two axes, precision, meaning what fraction of alerts are genuine, and recall, meaning what fraction of real occurrences are caught. Improving one usually costs the other, which is why tuning is an explicit trade-off documented with its rationale rather than an accumulating set of exclusions. Practical classification requires ground truth, and ground truth requires work: confirming with asset owners whether a process is sanctioned, checking change records for scheduled jobs, validating whether an account was legitimately used, or replaying controlled test activity to prove a rule fires. The vocabulary also covers near misses and true negatives, and it is worth stating plainly that a closed alert labelled false positive is a claim of fact, not a dismissal. Teams that keep a labelled corpus of past alerts, including the reasoning, build the empirical basis for tuning, for measuring detection coverage, and for demonstrating improvement over time.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to classify alerts into true positive, false positive and false negative with an evidence-based rationale rather than intuition.
  • You will be able to distinguish a detection that fired incorrectly from one that fired correctly on activity that is benign in context.
  • You will be able to explain the precision and recall trade-off and describe the operational cost of moving either measure.
  • You will be able to obtain ground truth for a disputed alert through asset ownership records, change tickets and controlled test activity.
  • You will be able to identify likely false negatives by reviewing which ATT&CK techniques your log sources and rules can actually observe.
  • You will be able to maintain a labelled alert corpus that supports tuning decisions and demonstrates detection improvement over time.
