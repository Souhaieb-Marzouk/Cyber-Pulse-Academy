T1496.001 Resource Hijacking: Compute Hijacking

T1496.001ImpactExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This is the part of resource hijacking that steals processing power. The attacker's program uses your computer chips to do heavy work, usually to earn cryptocurrency. Your own programs then run slowly because the chips are busy. This sub-technique is the compute focused part of the wider resource hijacking technique.

Why should you care?

Your servers may become so slow that customers notice before you do. Machines run hot and wear out faster, and cloud bills grow with every extra hour of use. In container environments the attacker can start many workers at once, so the cost multiplies quickly. An attacker may also delete your security tools or other miners processes to protect their income. What looks like a performance problem can be an active intrusion.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gets code running, often from a stolen credential for a container interface or an exposed service.
  2. The program joins a mining pool and receives blocks of work to calculate.
  3. It uses all spare processor or graphics capacity, sometimes leaving a small amount for the user.
  4. It registers itself to restart after reboots or when its process is stopped.
  5. Some miners look for and stop other miners processes so they keep all the profit.
  6. The attacker collects the earnings while your hardware and your bill absorb the cost.

Real-world example

Imagine a shared office with one powerful printer that everyone relies on. A visitor quietly sends thousands of their own print jobs through the night. In the morning your team finds the printer warm, the paper tray empty, and their own documents stuck at the back of a long queue. The visitor has not taken anything from the office, yet the printer was working for them the whole time.

Key takeaways

  • Compare processor use with your deployment calendar to spot capacity used by strangers.
  • In container environments, alert on workloads and images that do not match your naming rules.
  • Outbound connections to mining pools are a strong sign that a host has been hijacked.

In more detail

Compute hijacking is the processing power branch of resource hijacking. The attacker installs code that consumes processor or graphics cycles for their own benefit, most often to mine cryptocurrency, and it runs alongside legitimate workloads until saturation degrades service. Servers and cloud instances are favourite targets because they are powerful and lightly watched, while containers are attractive because a stolen interface credential lets an attacker start many short lived workers at once. A recurring behaviour is competition: some miners search for and terminate rival mining processes so that all available capacity is theirs. Defenders see sustained high utilisation that does not match the deployment calendar, worker or instance names that do not follow the naming convention, repeated image pulls from unfamiliar registries, and outbound connections to mining pools. Pairing utilisation data with workload inventory is what turns a vague performance complaint into a confirmed compromise.

What you will practise

  • Define compute hijacking and explain why attackers want processing power.
  • State how this sub-technique differs from the wider resource hijacking technique.
  • Describe why servers, cloud instances and containers are common targets.
  • Identify the telemetry that shows compute capacity being used by an unknown workload.
  • Explain how miners disable competing malicious processes on the same host.
  • Describe the business costs of hijacked compute and how to measure them.
