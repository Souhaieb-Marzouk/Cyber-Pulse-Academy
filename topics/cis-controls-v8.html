CIS Critical Security Controls

GRC & ComplianceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

The CIS Critical Security Controls are a prioritised list of things a company should do to defend itself. They start with basic steps and move to harder ones. Each control explains what to protect and how to check it. The order matters, because the first controls are the most useful.

Why should you care?

Security advice is often long, expensive, and confusing. A prioritised list tells you where to begin with a small team. It also reduces the biggest risks first instead of the easiest ones. Following the order avoids buying tools that fix a later problem while simple problems stay open. It gives managers a clear way to measure progress.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You start by listing every device, account, and program your company uses.
  2. You keep your software updated so that known weaknesses are closed quickly.
  3. You limit administrator rights and give people only the access they need.
  4. You set safe configurations, turn on logging, and keep backups that you test.
  5. You build skills, write rules, and practise how you respond to an incident.
  6. You review your coverage each year and close the biggest gaps you still have.

Real-world example

Think of keeping a large building safe. First you list every door and window, then you repair the broken locks. You give master keys to very few people. You write down what happens in the entrance hall each night. Only later do you add cameras in the car park. Skipping the first steps would leave the building open.

Key takeaways

  • Start with the early controls, such as knowing your assets and patching them.
  • The controls are prioritised, so follow the order rather than the easy wins.
  • Use the list to measure progress, not as a one-time audit to pass.

In more detail

The CIS Critical Security Controls are a prioritised set of defensive safeguards, published by the Center for Internet Security and maintained through a consensus process with input from government, industry, and practitioners. Their defining idea is ordering by defensive value rather than by category: the controls begin with knowing what you own and what runs on it, then move through continuous vulnerability management, controlled use of administrative privileges, secure configuration, audit logging, and recovery capability, because an organisation that cannot see its assets cannot defend them and an organisation that cannot restore data cannot recover from a destructive incident. The framework is grouped into control areas, each with safeguards that are further classed as foundational, organisational, or advanced, so a small team can start with the essential steps and extend coverage over time; the guidance also supports implementation groups that help organisations pick a realistic starting point based on size and risk. The common mistake is to treat the list as an audit checklist to satisfy once, or to jump straight to advanced safeguards such as full packet inspection while asset inventory and patching, the earlier and far cheaper controls, remain unfinished.

What you will practise

  • Explain what the CIS Critical Security Controls are and who they are for.
  • Describe why the controls are ordered by defensive priority rather than topic.
  • List the areas covered, from asset inventory through logging and recovery.
  • Explain how safeguards are grouped so a small team can start sensibly.
  • Describe how organisations measure and report progress against the controls.
  • Identify the mistake of chasing advanced safeguards before foundational ones.
