G1051 Medusa Group

G1051Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Medusa Group is a ransomware team that locks a company's files and demands money. Ransomware is software that makes files unreadable until the owner pays. This group is not the same as an older banking program that also used the name Medusa.

Why should you care?

This group has been active since around 2021 and works with partners, so attacks can arrive from many different people. They steal files first and then lock them. If you do not have copies kept somewhere else, a business can lose years of work in an afternoon. Preparation is far cheaper than paying.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. They break in with a stolen login or by exploiting an unpatched service.
  2. They look around the network and collect administrator passwords.
  3. They copy valuable files to their own storage.
  4. They turn off the recovery features that could restore your files.
  5. They run the locker program, which renames and scrambles many files.
  6. They leave a note and list your company on a public website.

Real-world example

Picture a library where every book is rewritten in a secret alphabet overnight. The reading rooms still work and the shelves are still full, but nothing can be read. The thief also took photographs of every page before he started, and he sends you a bill to teach you the alphabet again.

Key takeaways

  • Keep an offline backup and test that you can restore from it.
  • Deleting recovery features is a common step, so alert on those events.
  • Report and get help early, because the first hours shape the outcome.

In more detail

Medusa Group refers to a Medusa ransomware operation, distinct from the unrelated Medusa banking trojan tracked under the same family name, that first appears in public reporting around 2021 and has remained active under an affiliate model. Operators and affiliates breach a victim network, steal data, then deploy a locker that adds its own file extension, disables recovery features and drops a ransom note, while a leak site publishes victim names to force payment. Reported entry paths include exploitation of unpatched internet facing services, phishing and valid accounts, and affiliates have been described contacting victims directly, including by phone. Tradecraft includes use of remote monitoring and management tools, built in Windows utilities for discovery and backup destruction, and data exfiltration before encryption. Evidence is loud but distributed: service and shadow copy deletion events, mass file rename patterns, RMM installation logs, ransom notes and the leak site listing. Attribution of specific intrusions is contested in public reporting and names are assigned to the brand rather than to individuals.
