DNSSEC

Network SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

DNSSEC stands for Domain Name System Security Extensions. It adds a digital signature to the answers that translate website names into addresses. A computer that checks the signature knows the answer is genuine. It can then refuse fake answers.

Why should you care?

Without a signature check, a fake answer can send you to a copy of your bank or email page. The address bar may still look almost right, so people often do not notice. DNSSEC lets machines detect such tampering. It does not hide your browsing and it does not judge whether a site is honest. It only proves the answer was not changed.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Each domain owner creates a key pair and signs the records that describe its domain.
  2. The signed records and the public key are published so any resolver can fetch them.
  3. The parent domain also signs a small summary of the child's key.
  4. This creates a chain that starts from a trusted starting point in the resolver.
  5. A validation failure means the resolver discards the answer instead of using it.
  6. The keys must be renewed in time, because an expired signature also breaks the domain.

Real-world example

Think of a passport office that stamps a seal on every certificate it issues. A border officer checks the seal against a known sample and trusts the document only when the seal is correct. The seal proves the paper is real and unchanged. It says nothing about whether the traveller is a good person, and it is not a secret envelope.

Key takeaways

  • DNSSEC proves an answer is authentic and unchanged, nothing more.
  • It does not encrypt lookups and does not block malicious domains.
  • Key and signature management is the hard part, so plan renewals and rollovers.

In more detail

DNSSEC, the Domain Name System Security Extensions, adds cryptographic signatures to DNS answers so a resolver can verify that a record came from the zone that owns it and arrived unmodified. It works through a chain of trust: each zone signs its own records, and a parent zone signs a digest of the child's key, starting from a trust anchor configured in the validating resolver. A signed zone publishes its keys, a signature for each record set, and a denial of existence proof for names that do not exist, so an attacker cannot invent a convincing negative answer either. Crucially, DNSSEC provides authenticity and integrity only. It does not encrypt queries, so it does not hide which names are being looked up, and it does not make a domain legitimate; a criminal can sign their own zone perfectly well. Operational care matters because an expired signature or a key rollover done in the wrong order makes a domain unresolvable for validating clients. The mistake people usually make is assuming DNSSEC protects privacy or filters bad domains, when it only proves that the answer is the one the zone owner published.

What you will practise

  • Explain the purpose of DNSSEC and the properties it does and does not provide.
  • Describe how a chain of trust from a configured trust anchor validates an answer.
  • Explain why signed denial of existence matters for names that do not resolve.
  • Recognise operational risks such as expired signatures and poorly ordered key rollovers.
  • Correct the common misunderstanding that DNSSEC provides privacy or reputation filtering.
