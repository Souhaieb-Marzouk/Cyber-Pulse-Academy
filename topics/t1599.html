T1599 Network Boundary Bridging

T1599Defense ImpairmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A boundary device sits between two networks and decides what traffic may pass. This technique means an attacker takes control of that device. Then they change its rules to let blocked traffic through. The separation between the two networks is broken, even though both networks still work normally.

Why should you care?

Your network separation is only as strong as the device that enforces it. If that device is changed, an attacker can pass freely in both directions. Data can leave through a path you believe is closed. Command and control traffic can hide inside traffic you allow. Everything still looks normal, so you may not notice for a long time. Watching configuration changes is your best defence.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gains administrative access to a router, firewall, or segmentation device.
  2. They read the current rules to learn what traffic the device blocks and allows.
  3. They add a rule, a route, or a translation entry that permits the traffic they want.
  4. They save the configuration so the change survives a restart of the device.
  5. They route command and control or stolen data through the newly opened path.
  6. They may replace the device software to hide their new rules from review.

Real-world example

Imagine two office floors joined by one locked fire door. Staff on each floor assume that nobody crosses without permission. A cleaner copies the key and props the door open with a wedge. Both floors still look busy and normal, and the door is still in place. The attacker changed the rule rather than the building.

Key takeaways

  • Log and alert on every configuration change made to boundary devices.
  • Check for rules that were added outside a planned change window.
  • Segment internal networks so that one broken boundary is not the only one.

In more detail

Boundary bridging means an adversary takes control of the device that enforces separation between two networks and then changes its policy so traffic that should never cross now does. The target is usually a router or firewall at the perimeter, or an internal segmentation device such as a switch or a virtual firewall sitting between server tiers. The change is configuration rather than code: access control lists, routing entries, address translation rules, or virtual local area network assignments. Once the device forwards the forbidden traffic, the attacker can tunnel command and control through it or copy data out along a path defenders believe is closed. Detection is strongest in configuration management, where unexpected rule additions, configuration saves outside a change window, and administrative logins from unusual source addresses all stand out against a known baseline.
