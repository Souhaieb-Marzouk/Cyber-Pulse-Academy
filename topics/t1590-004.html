T1590.004 Gather Victim Network Information: Network Topology

T1590.004ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This sub-technique is about how your network is laid out. It covers the parts that face the internet and the parts inside your buildings. It includes routers, gateways, and the way systems are grouped into segments.

Why should you care?

If an attacker knows your layout, they know where the weak points sit. They can choose a path that avoids your strongest controls. They can also guess which systems sit close to valuable data. A layout that is easy to guess makes every later step of an attack easier.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker sends packets that expire after a few hops to trace the path to your network.
  2. They read how long replies take to estimate distance and rough network shape.
  3. They examine the naming of your systems for clues about buildings, floors, or functions.
  4. They read public documents, such as job adverts, that mention network equipment and designs.
  5. They identify gateways and routers by the way those devices answer probes.
  6. They combine everything into a simple map showing where a later attack should start.

Real-world example

Imagine a large hospital with many wings and corridors. A visitor at the front desk asks simple questions about where the pharmacy and the records office are. They study the floor signs in the lobby and the names printed on the doors. Nobody finds this odd, because visitors ask for directions all day. Slowly the visitor builds a mental map of the building. Later, that map tells them which corridor leads to the rooms worth entering. Network topology gathering is that map making. The attacker learns your gateways and how your systems are grouped. Each question looks harmless, yet the picture they build is not.

Key takeaways

  • Naming systems after buildings or floors gives attackers a free map, so use neutral names.
  • Watch for repeated short time to live packets that look like path tracing from the internet.
  • Check the documents you publish for network diagrams and equipment details before release.

In more detail

This sub-technique covers the physical and logical arrangement of a victim's networks, both the internet-facing edge and the internal segments behind it, including gateways, routers, and other infrastructure that defines how traffic flows. Adversaries assemble it from hop-limited probe traffic that reveals intermediate devices, from naming conventions that encode buildings or functions, from public documents such as tenders and job adverts that describe equipment and designs, and from diagrams that leak into supplier material or assessment reports. The tell-tale telemetry is at the perimeter: packets with deliberately short time to live values, repeated path-probing from the same source, and queries against device management interfaces. The detection challenge is that monitoring platforms, content delivery networks, and troubleshooting tools generate similar traffic, so consistent source ranges, probe cadence, and destination breadth matter more than any single packet, and internal segmentation detail should never be inferable from a public response.

What you will practise

  • Define network topology in the context of adversary reconnaissance and explain what it reveals.
  • Describe how path tracing and response timing are used to infer intermediate devices.
  • Explain why host naming conventions and public documents leak infrastructure detail.
  • Identify perimeter telemetry that indicates active topology mapping.
  • Recommend naming, publishing, and device response settings that reduce leakage.
