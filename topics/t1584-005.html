T1584.005 Compromise Infrastructure: Botnet

T1584.005Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A botnet is a group of compromised machines that one attacker controls together. This sub-technique is about building that group by breaking into many systems, rather than renting one. The parent technique covers compromising other people's infrastructure. Here the assembled asset is the network of victims.

Why should you care?

An attacker with thousands of machines can send spam or flood a service from all directions. Each infected device belongs to an ordinary home or office, so no single address looks wrong. Cleaning up means finding every device, and many owners never realise theirs was involved.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker scans the internet for devices with default passwords or unpatched software.
  2. Each successful intrusion installs a small program that starts automatically after restarts.
  3. The infected devices contact a control server at regular intervals for instructions.
  4. The attacker groups them by region or speed to use them for different jobs at once.
  5. For mass mailing or flooding, all devices receive the same command and act together.
  6. Some operators also seize another group's botnet by pointing the devices at new servers.

Real-world example

Picture a block of flats where every letterbox has the same simple lock. One person makes copies of the key and reads every letter. The postman still delivers normally, neighbours notice nothing, and the thief can act as one person or as a hundred houses at once.

Key takeaways

  • Regular outbound connections from a device to a new destination are a classic infection sign.
  • Changing default passwords and updating firmware removes most recruitment chances.
  • Botnet activity is best spotted in aggregate, across many hosts rather than one.

In more detail

In this sub-technique an adversary builds a botnet by compromising many third-party systems, or takes over an existing one, instead of renting capacity from a criminal service. The parent technique covers compromising third-party assets; here the assembled asset is a large, distributed set of controlled machines. Recruitment usually targets cheap and exposed equipment such as consumer routers, network appliances, and internet of things devices, where default credentials and missing updates make access easy, and the resulting network is then used for coordinated tasks like mass phishing, distributed denial of service, or proxying to obscure the operator's own systems. Takeover of another group's botnet is achieved by redirecting the compromised machines to new control servers, which means existing infections simply change masters. From a defender's viewpoint the infected host shows little that is unique: outbound connections on a regular schedule to a small set of destinations, commands executed by ordinary system processes, and resource use that rises gradually. Detection leans on egress baselining and on tracking shared infrastructure across many hosts rather than on file signatures alone.

What you will practise

  • Explain how this sub-technique differs from the parent Compromise Infrastructure technique.
  • Describe how third-party devices are recruited into a botnet and kept under control.
  • Identify host and network telemetry that indicates botnet membership.
  • Explain how taking over an existing botnet changes control without new infections.
  • Describe the containment steps for a device suspected of botnet membership.
