OT Network Monitoring

OT & ICS SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

OT means operational technology, the computers and devices that run machines, pumps and power grids. OT network monitoring means watching the traffic on those networks to spot problems. It is done by copying the traffic and looking at the copy, not by installing software on the machines. This matters because those machines often cannot run extra software safely.

Why should you care?

A problem in an office network slows down email. A problem in a plant network can stop water, power or production. Many industrial devices are old and were never built with security in mind. Staff also connect laptops for maintenance, which can bring in unwanted activity. If you cannot see the network, you cannot tell an accidental change from a deliberate one. Careful monitoring gives early warning without touching the machines themselves.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You copy network traffic from a special port on a switch or from a physical tap device.
  2. The copy goes to a monitoring system, so the real traffic continues untouched.
  3. The system reads the industrial protocols and records who talks to whom and how often.
  4. Over some weeks it builds a picture of what normal looks like for your plant.
  5. It then alerts when something new appears, such as a strange laptop or a programming command.
  6. Analysts check each alert with the engineering team before anyone changes the process.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital ward where patients must not be disturbed. The nurses do not wake each patient to ask how they feel. They watch monitors at the desk and note the readings on a chart. When a reading moves outside the usual range, a nurse walks over to check in person. The chart only works because the staff first learned what normal looks like for each patient.

Key takeaways

  • OT monitoring listens to a copy of the traffic, so it does not disturb fragile industrial devices.
  • Useful alerts come from learning a baseline and noticing changes, not from malware signatures alone.
  • Always confirm a suspected issue with the engineers who understand the physical process.

In more detail

OT network monitoring is the practice of gaining visibility into operational technology networks, the systems that control physical processes in factories, utilities and transport, by observing traffic rather than by installing agents on the controllers themselves. The constraint that shapes the whole discipline is that industrial devices such as programmable logic controllers are often old, single-purpose and unable to run endpoint software without risking the process they control, so monitoring has to be passive: traffic is copied from a span port or a network tap and analysed out of band. Analysis is usually protocol aware, because industrial protocols such as Modbus, DNP3, EtherNet/IP and PROFINET carry process values whose meaning and legitimate ranges depend on the engineering design, so a useful tool learns a baseline of which controller talks to which, on what protocol, at what rate and with what function codes, then flags deviations and unauthorised engineering activity. The typical mistake is importing an information technology mindset wholesale: monitoring hosts for malware signatures, or expecting deterministic alert thresholds, when the important signals are changes in the process, new devices appearing, unexpected programming commands and configuration downloads, and it is equally wrong to assume that a sensor in monitor-only mode can never affect the process, since some inline appliances and active probing of fragile devices can themselves cause outages.

What you will practise

  • Explain what operational technology is and why passive monitoring is preferred on those networks.
  • Describe how a span port or network tap provides a copy of traffic for analysis.
  • Explain how protocol aware baselining produces meaningful alerts in an industrial environment.
  • Recognise signs of concern such as new devices, unexpected engineering commands or changed rates.
  • Describe how analysts validate a monitoring alert with process engineers before acting.
