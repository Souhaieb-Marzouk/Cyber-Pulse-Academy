SOC 2

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

SOC 2 is an independent report about how a service company protects customer data. SOC stands for System and Organization Controls. An outside accounting firm checks the company's controls and writes an opinion. It is not a certificate, and there is no pass or fail result. Customers read the report to decide whether to trust you.

Why should you care?

Business customers often refuse to buy from a company that cannot show how it protects data. Filling in a long questionnaire for every deal wastes time and gives inconsistent answers. A SOC 2 report answers most of those questions once, in a form their auditors accept. Preparing for it also forces you to tidy processes you already run informally.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Define the clear boundary of the service that the report will cover.
  2. Choose which trust categories apply to you, such as security and availability.
  3. List your existing controls and give each one a clear named owner.
  4. Collect evidence on a schedule, such as access reviews and change tickets.
  5. Let the outside accounting firm test the controls over the chosen period.
  6. Share the finished report with your customers and fix whatever it flags.

Real-world example

Think of a restaurant hygiene rating shown in the window. The rating comes from an inspector who visited, not from the owner's own claims. A high rating helps customers decide quickly. SOC 2 works the same way for data. The report describes what the inspector saw during a set period, and it also lists the things you, the customer, must still do yourself.

Key takeaways

  • SOC 2 is an opinion from an auditor, not a certificate.
  • Type 1 looks at design; Type 2 covers real operation over time.
  • Collect evidence all year instead of scrambling before the audit.

In more detail

SOC 2 is an attestation report issued under the AICPA Trust Services Criteria that describes how a service organisation designs and, in a Type 2 report, operates controls relevant to the security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy of customer data. It is not a certification and there is no pass or fail: an independent CPA firm expresses an opinion on whether management's description of its system is accurate and whether the selected controls operated effectively over a defined review period. Two report types exist. A Type 1 report speaks to control design at a single point in time and is frequently used to unblock early enterprise deals; a Type 2 report covers design and operating effectiveness across a window, commonly three to twelve months, and is what mature buyers demand. The Common Criteria, organised around control environment, communication and information, risk assessment, monitoring activities and control activities, map closely to how engineering teams already work, which is why SOC 2 readiness often reduces to formalising change management, access review, vulnerability management, incident response and vendor risk processes that already exist informally. For practitioners the practical work is defining the system boundary, producing a control matrix with named owners, gathering evidence on a schedule rather than in a panic, and being precise in the description of the system. Bridge letters and complementary user entity controls matter because responsibilities split between the provider and the customer.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to distinguish a SOC 2 Type 1 design opinion from a Type 2 operating effectiveness opinion and explain the review period.
  • You will be able to define a service organisation's system boundary and the infrastructure, software, people and procedures inside it.
  • You will be able to map the Trust Services Criteria to existing engineering controls and assign named control owners.
  • You will be able to design an evidence cadence that keeps access reviews, change tickets and monitoring artefacts audit-ready year round.
  • You will be able to interpret a bridge letter and complementary user entity controls in a vendor due diligence review.
  • You will be able to spot the difference between a control that is documented and a control that actually operated.
