Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is the system of rules, people and services that issue and check digital certificates. A certificate is a signed file that binds a public key to a name, like a website address. Certificate authorities sign these files. Your device trusts a small set of root authorities, and everything else must chain back to them.

Why should you care?

Two strangers on the internet need a way to trust each other. PKI solves that by introducing trusted signers. Without it, you could not tell your bank's website from a copy made by an attacker. Most real PKI failures are not clever maths. They are messy operations: a certificate nobody renewed, a trusted root that should not be there, or a revocation list clients never check.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. A certificate authority creates a root key pair and publishes its root certificate.
  2. Anyone who wants a certificate sends a request with their name and public key.
  3. The authority checks the request, then signs a certificate with its private key.
  4. Your device already trusts the root, so it can verify that signature.
  5. The device also checks the dates, the allowed uses and the chain of issuers.
  6. It may also ask whether the certificate was cancelled before its expiry date.

Real-world example

Think of a passport office. The office checks your identity and issues a passport signed with an official stamp. Border guards do not know you, but they recognise the stamp and can verify it against a list of trusted offices. If a passport is reported stolen, the office adds it to a cancellation list. If a guard never checks that list, a stolen passport still works.

Key takeaways

  • A certificate is a signed link between a public key and a name.
  • Trust flows from a small set of root authorities down through a chain.
  • Expired certificates and unmanaged roots cause most real outages and trust gaps.

In more detail

Public key infrastructure is the combination of certificate authorities, registration authorities, certificate repositories, revocation services and the policies that bind public keys to identities so that parties who have never met can authenticate each other. A certificate is a signed data structure containing a subject public key, an issuer, a validity period, extensions that constrain usage, and the issuer's signature over all of it; a relying party validates that structure by building a chain to a trusted root, checking signatures, validity times, key usage and extended key usage, name constraints, and revocation status through CRL or OCSP. In practice most PKI incidents are not cryptanalytic. They are trust-store problems: an over-broad enterprise root installed on endpoints, a subordinate CA with no name constraints, an internal CA whose private key sits on a network-reachable server, a certificate issued to an unverified requester, a revocation path that clients never actually check, or a machine identity that outlives the employee who owned it. Lifecycle management is equally important, because expiring certificates cause outages while forgotten long-lived ones become stable footholds. Modern practice pushes towards short-lived certificates, automated issuance through ACME-style protocols, hardware-backed CA keys, certificate transparency monitoring for rogue issuance, and continuous inventory of everything presenting a certificate on the network.

What you will practise

  • Describe the roles of certificate authority, registration authority, repository and revocation service in an operational PKI.
  • Validate a certificate chain manually, including signature, validity, key usage, name constraints and revocation status.
  • Explain why clients frequently fail to enforce revocation and what design choices reduce that exposure.
  • Identify trust-store risks such as unnecessary enterprise roots, unconstrained subordinate CAs and stale root certificates.
  • Design a certificate lifecycle process using automation, short validity periods and hardware-protected CA keys.
  • Use certificate transparency and issuance monitoring to detect rogue or misissued certificates.
