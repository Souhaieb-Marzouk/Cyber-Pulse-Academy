Eradication

Incident Response & ForensicsVery hardKeyword

Eradication is the incident response phase in which the team removes the adversary's ability to continue operating in the environment: malware, tooling, persistence mechanisms, created or compromised accounts, malicious configuration, and the initial access vector that let them in. It is the phase most often declared too early. Deleting a dropped binary or rebuilding a single workstation addresses a symptom rather than the foothold; if the adversary planted a scheduled task, a rogue service, a federated identity token, a malicious OAuth application, an additional SSH key or a shadow administrator account, that access survives the cleanup and the incident simply restarts. Sound eradication begins with scoping evidence: which accounts authenticated where, which persistence locations exist across the estate, which systems share the compromised credential or the vulnerable component. Root cause then has to be closed rather than merely observed - patching the exploited service, rotating every credential the adversary could have touched, removing malicious identity objects, and confirming that the initial access path no longer works. Eradication actions are destructive to evidence by nature, so forensic collection precedes them, and each action is recorded with a timestamp, an operator and a verification result. Verification is the crux: after cleanup the team must re-hunt using the adversary's known tactics, techniques and procedures to demonstrate absence rather than assume it. Only when eradication is verified does recovery begin, and even then detection coverage and monitoring are raised rather than relaxed.

What you will practise

  • Describe what eradication must remove beyond malware, including persistence, identity objects and the initial access vector.
  • Scope persistence across endpoints, identity providers, cloud control planes and network infrastructure before cleanup begins.
  • Determine which credentials, tokens and secrets require rotation after a confirmed compromise and justify the scope.
  • Sequence forensic collection and eradication so that destructive cleanup does not destroy evidence.
  • Verify eradication with threat hunting driven by the adversary's observed tactics, techniques and procedures.
  • Explain why restoring a rebuilt host without closing the root cause usually produces a repeat incident.
  • Record eradication actions with timestamps, operators and verification results suitable for audit and legal review.
