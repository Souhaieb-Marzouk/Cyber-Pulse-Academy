Ransomware

Endpoint & MalwareExtremeKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Ransomware is malicious software, or a whole intrusion, that blocks an organization from using its own data and then demands payment to give it back. The usual method is encrypting files so they cannot be opened. Many groups also steal data first and threaten to publish it, which is called double extortion.

Why should you care?

A ransomware event can stop a business completely, and the encryption phase happens fast. The best chance to intervene comes earlier, while the attacker is still exploring the network. Good backups that are offline or cannot be altered are the single most useful preparation. Organizations should also decide in advance who calls which shots, because the middle of an incident is a bad time for those arguments.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gets in, often through a stolen password, phishing email, or exposed service.
  2. They explore the network, quietly raise their permissions, and reach more machines.
  3. Valuable data is quietly copied out of the network before anything is encrypted.
  4. Backup tools and recovery features are attacked so that restores become hard.
  5. The encryption program then runs across many machines at once, very quickly.
  6. A note appears demanding payment, sometimes with a threat to publish the stolen data.

Real-world example

Imagine a shop where the owner keeps the spare key under the mat and the safe combination in a drawer. A thief who gets inside first locks every door and changes every lock, then offers to sell the keys back. If the owner had kept a copy of the keys in a bank vault across town, the demand would mean nothing. That vault is an offline backup. Ransomware only works as a business when recovery is impossible.

Key takeaways

  • Ransomware is usually a long intrusion that ends in a fast encryption event.
  • Keep backups offline or unchangeable, and practice restoring from them.
  • Assume data was stolen even if no leak site is mentioned.

In more detail

Ransomware is malicious software or an intrusion campaign whose objective is to deny an organisation access to its own data or systems and then sell that access back, typically by encrypting files, locking virtualisation or backup infrastructure, or threatening to publish stolen data. Modern incidents are rarely a single executable. They are operations that begin with access brokering, phishing, exposed remote services or valid credentials, proceed through discovery, privilege escalation and lateral movement, often harvest data for double extortion before encryption starts, and only then deploy the payload across many hosts at once. The encryption phase is quick and loud, but the preceding dwell time is where defenders have the best chance to intervene, which is why detection strategy emphasises the precursors: mass file rename behaviour, deletion or disabling of shadow copies and recovery catalogs, unexpected use of backup administration interfaces, credential dumping, and the disabling of endpoint protection. Recovery depends far more on preparation than on any live response. Organisations need offline or immutable backups whose restore path has genuinely been rehearsed, a documented decision process for isolation versus shutdown, and pre-negotiated legal, regulatory and communications roles. Paying a demand is discouraged because it funds further crime, does not guarantee restoration, and in some jurisdictions carries sanctions or reporting implications. Because exfiltration frequently precedes encryption, incident scoping must assume data theft even when no leak site is mentioned, and breach notification timelines should be assessed from the moment of first unauthorised access rather than the moment files were encrypted.

What you will practise

  • Describe the typical ransomware intrusion lifecycle from initial access and discovery through exfiltration and encryption.
  • Detect pre-encryption precursors such as recovery-inhibition behaviour, credential access and mass file modification.
  • Define a backup architecture that resists destruction, including immutability, offline copies and restore rehearsal.
  • Lead a ransomware incident decision tree covering isolation, shutdown, escalation, legal and regulatory notification.
  • Explain double extortion and why breach assessment must assume data exfiltration even without proof of theft.
  • Justify a no-payment position and outline the sanctions, insurance and recovery consequences of paying a demand.
