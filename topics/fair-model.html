FAIR Risk Model

Risk & ResilienceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

The FAIR risk model is a way to describe risk using numbers instead of words like high or low. FAIR stands for Factor Analysis of Information Risk. It breaks a risk into smaller parts, such as how often something happens and how much it would cost. Teams use it to compare risks fairly.

Why should you care?

Managers often hear that a risk is high, without knowing what that means. Words cannot be compared or added up, and they rarely win budget. Numbers help leaders choose between two projects with a clear reason. FAIR also shows which part of a risk causes most of the cost. That tells you where to spend your effort.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You write the risk clearly: which asset, and which kind of attacker or event.
  2. You estimate how often that event is likely to reach the asset each year.
  3. You estimate how often it would succeed, given your current protections.
  4. You estimate the cost if it succeeded, including cleanup and lost business.
  5. You keep each estimate as a range, not one number, and say how confident you are.
  6. You combine the ranges to show expected loss, then test how it changes with new controls.

Real-world example

Think of a shop deciding whether to buy a better alarm. The owner does not say the risk is high. He estimates how often thieves try the door, how often they get in, and what stock and repairs would cost. Then he can compare a two thousand pound alarm with the yearly loss it would prevent.

Key takeaways

  • FAIR turns risk into estimated frequency and estimated money lost.
  • Use ranges and state your confidence, because honest uncertainty is more useful than false precision.
  • Quantified risk makes it possible to compare options and defend spending.

In more detail

The FAIR risk model, where FAIR stands for Factor Analysis of Information Risk, is an approach to expressing cyber and operational risk in measurable terms instead of qualitative labels. Its central claim is that risk is the probable frequency and probable magnitude of future loss, which means a risk statement should eventually resolve to something like a range of expected financial loss per year for a defined asset and threat community. To get there the model decomposes risk into factors: how often a threat actually makes contact with the asset, the probability that contact becomes an action, how resistant the asset is to that action, and how much loss follows, split into primary response costs and secondary costs such as fines, legal fees and lost business. Analysts estimate each factor with ranges and confidence rather than single invented numbers, which is why the method pairs well with workshop techniques such as modified Delphi estimation. It is used to compare competing investments, to defend a security budget in business language, and to quantify risks recorded in a register. The mistake people usually make is adopting the vocabulary without the discipline, producing impressive loss figures with no stated basis, which destroys trust in the whole exercise.

What you will practise

  • Explain what the FAIR model measures and how a quantified risk statement differs from a label.
  • Describe the main factor groups, including frequency of contact, resistance and loss magnitude.
  • Explain why estimates are expressed as ranges with stated confidence.
  • Apply the model to compare competing security investments or to prioritise register entries.
  • Recognise the failure of using FAIR terminology while inventing numbers without any basis.
