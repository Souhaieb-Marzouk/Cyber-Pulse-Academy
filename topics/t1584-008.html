T1584.008 Compromise Infrastructure: Network Devices

T1584.008Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This is when an attacker takes control of a network device such as a home router or a company firewall. The device sits between the internet and the computers behind it. The attacker may not care about the people using it. They want the device as a quiet place to work from. The device keeps working, so nobody notices.

Why should you care?

These devices are always switched on and connected to the internet. Many of them never get security updates and cannot run antivirus software. From one router an attacker can reach many victims and hide the real source of their traffic. If your organisation owns such a device, your address may appear in attack reports. Regular patching and a check of administrator logins are the main defences.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker scans the internet for devices with known weaknesses or unchanged default passwords.
  2. They log in with those credentials and change settings so their access survives.
  3. They may install altered firmware so the device hides their tools from the owner.
  4. The device then forwards their traffic, so victim connections appear to come from it.
  5. It can also host malicious files or inject content into pages passing through it.
  6. Owners often see only slow performance, or they may see no sign at all.

Real-world example

Think of a village post box that also acts as a sorting office. Letters pass through it every day and nobody watches it closely. Someone breaks in, copies the keys and starts posting their own letters from the same box. Every envelope carries the village postmark, so recipients trust it. The post box still delivers normal mail on time. Only a check of who holds keys, and of letters sent at strange hours, shows that the box is no longer just a post box.

Key takeaways

  • Edge devices are computers too, and they need updates and strong passwords.
  • Replace default administrator passwords before a device faces the internet.
  • Log and review administrator logins to routers and network appliances.

In more detail

Here the target is not the victim's data but the router, firewall, virtual private network appliance or other edge device the adversary needs as a staging post. Small office and home office routers are attractive because they are internet-facing, rarely patched and often cannot run endpoint security agents, so a foothold there survives workstation rebuilds. Once in control, the adversary can host phishing payloads, inject content into traffic that passes through, harvest credentials seen on the wire, and proxy command and control so victim connections terminate at a residential or business address instead of attacker infrastructure. Detection rests on firmware and configuration baselines, administrative login records, and flow data showing unexpected outbound sessions from the device itself.

What you will practise

  • Explain why internet-facing network devices are valuable to an adversary.
  • Describe how a compromised router can support phishing, proxying or credential theft.
  • Identify device telemetry that shows unauthorised configuration or firmware changes.
  • Distinguish normal administrative access from attacker access on an edge device.
  • Recommend basic hardening for small office and home office routers.
