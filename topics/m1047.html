M1047 Audit

M1047Enterprise MitigationVery hardMitigation

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Audit is the practice of recording security-relevant events so you can see what happened and prove it later. It covers sign-ins, account changes, program starts, service installs, and cloud activity. Records are collected in one place, kept long enough to be useful, and protected from tampering. Without these records, investigations become guesswork.

Why should you care?

When something goes wrong, the first question is what happened and when. Good records answer that in minutes instead of weeks. They also let you hunt for quiet activity that no alert covered. An attacker who knows the logs are thin can work slowly and stay unseen. Records must be protected too, because erasing them is often part of an attack.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Choose the events worth recording, such as logins, new accounts, and service installs.
  2. Turn on the same settings across servers, workstations, cloud accounts, and network devices.
  3. Send everything to one central store, with clocks synced so times line up.
  4. Keep the records long enough to cover an investigation that starts months later.
  5. Protect the store so local administrators cannot edit or delete their own traces.
  6. Review the records with searches and alerts, or the archive becomes a shelf nobody reads.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital keeping notes for every patient visit. If a patient returns with a fever, the notes show which ward they were in and who treated them. Without notes, the staff can only guess. The notes are also kept securely, because a record that anyone can rewrite is worth very little. Audit logs are the hospital notes for your computers and accounts.

Key takeaways

  • Decide what to record, then check that it is actually arriving.
  • Sync clocks and centralise logs, or you cannot build a timeline.
  • Protect audit records from the administrators they are meant to observe.

In more detail

Audit is the control that makes an environment observable: the deliberate collection, retention, and review of security-relevant events so that unauthorised activity can be detected, reconstructed, and attributed. Implementation in a real estate spans several layers that must be joined together. Operating system audit policy is tuned to capture process creation with command-line arguments, authentication outcomes, account and group changes, service installation, scheduled task creation, and privilege use. Directory and identity services capture sign-in events, token issuance, and administrative role assignment. Network infrastructure contributes flow records, proxy and DNS logs, and firewall decisions. Cloud control planes contribute API activity trails for every subscription, account, and project. Endpoint agents contribute process lineage, driver and module loads, and file integrity information. All of it converges into centralised storage with normalised time, retained long enough to support an investigation that begins months after initial access, and protected from the very accounts it is meant to observe. Audit is the foundational control behind every other detection capability: without it, threat hunting, incident response, and forensics are guesswork. It directly supports detection of remote service use, account creation and discovery, autostart persistence, defence impairment, and data staging or exfiltration behaviour, because those actions leave configuration and authentication traces. The honest limitations are volume and quality. Default audit policy is either too sparse to be useful or so verbose that signal drowns in noise and licensing cost, and collecting events without a review process produces archives nobody reads. Failure modes include logging only on servers and not on workstations or build agents, leaving audit records in a writable location for any local administrator, failing to synchronise clocks so timelines cannot be correlated, expiring retention before an intrusion is discovered, and disabling a noisy audit subcategory after a false alarm instead of tuning the detection.

What you will practise

  • Explain how to design a tiered audit policy covering endpoint, identity, network, and cloud control-plane telemetry.
  • Describe how to normalise timestamps, field names, and identifiers across heterogeneous log sources for correlation.
  • Explain how log retention, immutability, and segregation of duties protect audit records from the accounts they observe.
  • Identify the audit gaps that most often make an incident timeline unreconstructable and prioritise closing them.
  • Describe the audit subcategories or event sources that support detection of persistence, account manipulation, and defence impairment.
  • Explain how to tune noisy audit sources without discarding the events that matter for investigation.
  • Evaluate an existing logging estate against detection and forensic requirements and produce a prioritised remediation plan.
