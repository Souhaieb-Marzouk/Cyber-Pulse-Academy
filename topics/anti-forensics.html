Anti Forensics

Incident Response & ForensicsExtremeKeyword

Anti-forensics is the set of techniques an adversary uses to frustrate, mislead or delay forensic analysis, either by destroying evidence, by making analysis uneconomic, or by planting artefacts that send investigators down the wrong path. The category spans data destruction such as secure wiping, free-space overwriting and the deletion of logs; artefact manipulation including timestamp alteration, log tampering and the removal of entries from event or audit trails; concealment through encryption, steganography, alternate data streams, hidden partitions and packed or obfuscated binaries; tool subversion, where malicious code interferes with the very utilities a responder would run, including kernel-mode rootkits and the disabling of security logging; and attribution attacks such as the deliberate use of another organisation's naming conventions or living-off-the-land binaries to blend into normal activity. For defenders, anti-forensics matters less as a list of tools than as an interpretive warning: the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and the failure of a standard tool to find something may itself be a finding worth pursuing. Investigations respond by collecting volatile data before artefacts can be altered, corroborating every conclusion across independent sources, validating acquisition and analysis tools against known-good behaviour, and treating inconsistencies in timestamps or log continuity as investigative leads. Reporting anti-forensic indicators, including gaps, tampering and tool anomalies, alongside substantive findings protects the credibility of the case and informs detection engineering, because these techniques often require behaviour that can be detected even when the artefacts themselves cannot be recovered.

What you will practise

  • Categorise anti-forensic techniques by whether they destroy, manipulate, conceal or misattribute evidence.
  • Interpret missing, discontinuous or inconsistent artefacts as investigative findings rather than as proof of innocence.
  • Adapt collection order and tooling when an environment may contain kernel-level or tool-subverting implants.
  • Corroborate conclusions across independent evidence sources so they withstand deliberate artefact manipulation.
  • Map observed anti-forensic behaviour to adversary tactics and identify the detection opportunities it creates.
  • Document and communicate confidence limits when evidence integrity or completeness is in question.
  • Brief stakeholders on how anti-forensics affects scope estimates, investigation timelines and legal defensibility.
