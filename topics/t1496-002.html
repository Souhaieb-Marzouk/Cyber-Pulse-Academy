T1496.002 Resource Hijacking: Bandwidth Hijacking

T1496.002ImpactExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Bandwidth hijacking means an attacker uses the internet connection of a computer they have taken over. The victim pays for the traffic and the attacker gets the benefit. The owner of the computer often has no idea that it is happening.

Why should you care?

Your monthly network bill may rise with no clear reason. You may also hit data limits you never expected to reach. Ordinary users share the same connection, so internet access becomes slow for real work. Illegal traffic is then blamed on your internet address, so other providers may block your email or block your website. If you ignore this, the hidden cost keeps growing while the attacker keeps earning.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Malware on a victim computer calls out to a control server and asks for instructions.
  2. The control server either sends flood commands or tells the host to act as a relay.
  3. For flooding, the host sends traffic at chosen targets, often for denial of service.
  4. For proxy work, outside users route their internet requests through the victim connection.
  5. The attacker is paid by proxyware buyers while the victim pays the network bill.
  6. Malware also scans the internet for more weak hosts, which costs more capacity.

Real-world example

Picture a shared flat where the internet bill is split between four friends. One friend secretly sells the flat's connection to strangers, who use it all evening for their own traffic. The connection becomes slow for everyone else, and the bill doubles at the end of the month. Nobody can say who caused it, because the extra traffic looked like normal browsing. That is bandwidth hijacking: your connection is borrowed and resold without your knowledge.

Key takeaways

  • Bandwidth hijacking uses your connection for someone else's profit or for attacks.
  • It costs money and damages your reputation because your address is used.
  • Watch for steady outbound traffic from processes you do not recognise.

In more detail

Bandwidth hijacking turns a compromised endpoint into a network resource that the adversary sells or spends elsewhere. The implant does not need to steal files; it only needs outbound capacity, so the visible effect is sustained egress traffic that competes with legitimate business flows and with the connection limits of local networking equipment. Three distinct businesses run on this capacity: botnet traffic used for denial of service and for seeding torrents, proxyjacking where the victim's address is resold to a proxyware provider, and internet-wide scanning that hunts for more victims. The artifact defenders care about is volume plus destination shape rather than payload: high upload relative to download, many distinct destinations, long-lived sessions to a small set of relay endpoints, and proxyware processes or service registrations appearing on hosts. Legitimate bulk traffic exists, but it usually has a recognisable owner and destination set, such as backup, patching, or content distribution, so baselining egress per host and per process is what separates the two.

What you will practise

  • Describe how bandwidth hijacking differs from ordinary data theft in its goals.
  • Explain what proxyjacking is and why a victim may never notice it.
  • Identify the network flow features that reveal unusual outbound capacity use.
  • Explain how hijacked bandwidth supports denial of service and malicious torrents.
  • Distinguish legitimate bulk egress such as backup from hijacked traffic.
  • List the host artefacts that suggest proxyware is installed on a system.
