Confidence Level

Threat IntelligenceExtremeKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A confidence level is a label on a piece of threat intelligence. It says how much weight that claim should carry. It describes how strong the evidence is, not how likely an attack is. Teams often use words like low, moderate and high, and they explain their reasons.

Why should you care?

Threat reports arrive from many places, and they often disagree with each other. Without a confidence level, you cannot rank them. You might block a harmless address because of one weak rumour. Or you might ignore a serious warning that came from a strong source. A shared confidence vocabulary lets a whole team act in proportion. High confidence can justify immediate blocking, while low confidence should start a hunt instead.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Ask two separate questions. How trustworthy is the source, and how credible is this specific piece of information?
  2. Rate the source. A well-known research team is stronger than an anonymous forum post.
  3. Rate the evidence itself. Was it seen directly, or is it only a rumour passed along?
  4. Choose a confidence word from a small fixed list, such as low, moderate or high.
  5. Write down your reason, and name the evidence that would change your mind.
  6. Match the action to the level. Block on high confidence, and hunt quietly on low confidence.

Real-world example

Think about a weather forecast. A presenter might say there is a high chance of rain tomorrow. That is a probability. Separately, you trust the forecast more when several models agree and the sky already looks grey. That is confidence. The two ideas are different. A forecast can be very likely and still be low confidence if the data is thin. Threat intelligence works exactly the same way, so you should report both.

Key takeaways

  • Confidence describes the strength of your evidence, not the chance an attack will happen.
  • Use a small fixed set of confidence words, and always write down your reason.
  • Let the confidence level decide the response, from quiet hunting up to immediate blocking.

In more detail

Confidence is the explicit, calibrated expression of how much weight an analytic judgement should carry, and it is distinct from the likelihood that an event will occur. A useful threat intelligence product states both: how reliable the sourcing is, and how strongly the analysis supports the conclusion. It then uses consistent estimative wording so consumers can rank competing claims against each other. Source reliability and information credibility are frequently assessed with a two-axis scheme that rates the source from completely reliable to unreliable and the information from confirmed to improbable, which produces a compact code an analyst can attach to each statement. Analytic confidence is a different judgement. It reflects the quality and quantity of evidence, the coherence of the argument, the soundness of the methods used, and the extent to which alternatives were considered. Established intelligence community standards for analytic writing require that judgements be sourced, that uncertainty be expressed in words with defined meanings, and that probability be distinguished from confidence. In practice, teams define a small vocabulary and keep to it, for example low, moderate and high confidence paired with stated reasons, and they avoid vague hedges such as possibly or could be, which carry no ranking information. Common failure modes include overconfidence driven by a single vivid source, anchoring on an initial vendor attribution, and treating absence of evidence as evidence of absence when collection was never attempted. Calibration improves by recording judgements and later scoring them against outcomes, by peer review that challenges the reasoning rather than the conclusion, and by writing estimative statements a reader can falsify. For defenders, confidence determines action: high-confidence judgements may justify immediate blocking or escalation, while low-confidence reporting should generate hunting, monitoring or collection requirements rather than disruptive change.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to distinguish source reliability, information credibility, analytic confidence and likelihood within one product.
  • You will be able to apply a defined confidence vocabulary consistently and state the reasoning behind each rating.
  • You will be able to replace vague hedging with ranked estimative wording that a reader can falsify.
  • You will be able to identify overconfidence, anchoring and absence-of-evidence fallacies in a draft assessment.
  • You will be able to map confidence levels to proportionate defensive action, from monitoring through to blocking.
  • You will be able to design a simple calibration record that scores past judgements against observed outcomes.
