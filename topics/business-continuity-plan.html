Business Continuity Plan

Risk & ResilienceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A business continuity plan explains how a company keeps working when something goes badly wrong. It covers what to do, who is in charge, and how to reach people. It also describes how the company returns to normal later. The aim is to keep important services running for customers.

Why should you care?

Problems such as fire, flood, or a long power cut can stop a business completely. If nobody planned ahead, staff waste hours deciding what to do first. Customers then leave and money is lost. Regulators may also ask how you would keep essential services running. A written and practised plan turns panic into clear steps.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You list the services your organisation must keep running for customers and the law.
  2. A business impact analysis shows how quickly each service must return after a stop.
  3. You choose ways to keep working, such as another site or a cloud service.
  4. You write clear roles, contact details, and step-by-step actions in one document.
  5. You train your staff and practise the plan with exercises on a regular schedule.
  6. After any real event or test, you update the plan and fix what did not work.

Real-world example

Think of a bakery that loses power in the middle of the morning rush. A shop without a plan closes and sends everyone home. A shop with a plan has a printed price list, a manual card machine, and a second oven at a partner shop. One person calls the staff and one calls the regular customers. Bread still leaves the door, and the owner knows who decides when to reopen.

Key takeaways

  • Keep the plan short, clear, and usable when systems and networks are down.
  • Practise it regularly, because a plan nobody has tested is only a document.
  • Review it after every change, incident, and test, then update the contacts.

In more detail

A business continuity plan is the documented set of arrangements that keeps an organisation's critical products and services available during and after a disruption, and returns them to normal in a controlled way. It is broader than disaster recovery: recovery planning concentrates on technology such as servers, data, and networks, while continuity also covers people, premises, suppliers, communications, and the manual workarounds that bridge the gap while systems are restored. The plan is built from a business impact analysis, which ranks processes by the harm caused when they stop and sets recovery time and recovery point objectives, and from risk assessment, which identifies the events most likely to interrupt them. A workable plan names who may declare an incident, where staff will work if a site is unavailable, how the organisation will communicate with staff, customers, and regulators, and the order in which services come back. The common mistake is measuring the plan by its size rather than by evidence that it works: an untested plan, an out-of-date contact list, or a plan that assumes the same event will not also remove the people who are supposed to execute it.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a business continuity plan is and what it is meant to achieve.
  • Describe the difference between business continuity and disaster recovery planning.
  • Explain how a business impact analysis sets recovery time and recovery point objectives.
  • Identify the main parts of a plan, such as roles, communications, and workarounds.
  • Describe how exercises and post-incident reviews keep a plan current.
  • State why staff, suppliers, and premises belong in continuity planning, not only technology.
