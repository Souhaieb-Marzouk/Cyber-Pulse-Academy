SSO

Identity & AccessVery hardKeyword

Single sign-on (SSO) is an authentication architecture in which one identity provider authenticates a principal once and then issues assertions or tokens that multiple relying-party applications accept in place of their own credential stores. Instead of every application holding a password database, each application trusts a federated identity provider such as Entra ID, Okta, Ping or a Keycloak deployment, and receives a signed statement about who the user is and, increasingly, what context the authentication occurred in. The security benefit is concentrated and easy to understate: credentials live in one hardened location with one MFA policy, one lockout policy, one session lifetime and one revocation path, so deprovisioning a leaver becomes a single disable action rather than a hunt through dozens of local account stores. The corresponding risk is that the identity provider becomes a tier-zero system whose compromise grants access to every federated application, and that the session cookie or refresh token becomes the real credential that adversaries pursue. Most real-world SSO incidents therefore involve token theft, consent phishing against the authorisation endpoint, misconfigured assertion validation on the relying-party side, or stale accounts that survive offboarding because a shadow application was never federated. Practitioners must be able to reason about session lifetime, token binding, sign-out propagation, identifier normalisation and the difference between an application that validates a signature correctly and one that merely decodes it. Logging quality matters here: the identity provider's sign-in and audit logs, not the application logs, are usually the authoritative record of who authenticated and from where.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to explain the roles of identity provider, service provider and relying party in a federated sign-in flow.
  • You will be able to describe why the SSO session token, not the password, is the primary credential an adversary targets.
  • You will be able to evaluate session lifetime, token binding and global sign-out settings against a stated risk appetite.
  • You will be able to identify common SSO misconfigurations such as unvalidated assertions, wildcard redirect URIs and orphaned application accounts.
  • You will be able to use identity provider sign-in logs to reconstruct an authentication timeline for incident response.
  • You will be able to plan deprovisioning so that disabling one identity revokes access to all federated applications.
