G0022 APT3

G0022Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

APT3 is a research name for a skilled cyber spying group that public reports link to China. It has attacked defence, aerospace, and university networks. It has used both unknown and already-fixed software flaws to get in.

Why should you care?

Universities and defence suppliers hold research that is valuable to spies. The group has used flaws that no patch existed for at the time. That means patching alone cannot stop it. Some known flaws were fixed years ago but still worked, because systems were never updated. So measure your patch coverage, not just your patch policy.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers find a website that their targets trust and visit every day.
  2. They modify the pages so they silently attack visitors using a known browser flaw.
  3. A single visit to that page is enough, with no download and no warning shown.
  4. A loader then installs backdoors that restart automatically and survive a system reboot.
  5. The operators steal passwords and cryptographic keys so they can move between systems.
  6. They log in through remote services, which keeps their activity quiet and hard to notice.

Real-world example

Picture an airport queue where staff check passports by eye. A forger studies which document features the staff look at, then copies exactly those features. The queue keeps moving and the check still happens, but it no longer catches the forgery. Watering hole attacks work the same way. The website is the check, and the visitor trusts it. When the site itself is altered, every visitor walks through the same queue. Each one carries something the guard has been taught to accept.

Key takeaways

  • Patching is necessary but never sufficient on its own.
  • Attackers target the websites your staff already trust and visit.
  • A browser or plugin flaw can compromise a machine with no download.

In more detail

APT3 is a cluster name for espionage activity that public reporting links to China-nexus operators and describes as mature and selective, with campaigns against aerospace, defence, government, technology, and higher-education targets. Notably for defenders, its tradecraft has included both undisclosed and publicly disclosed browser vulnerabilities, so patching closed one path while the operator moved to another; publicly reported activity also used watering hole compromises, where a legitimate site its targets already visit is modified to serve an exploit. Post-compromise behaviour has been documented as methodical, with custom loaders, multiple backdoors, credential theft, and use of stolen keys and remote services to hold access. Some public analysis describes the group as disciplined in operational security, which means indicators expire quickly. The recurring misunderstanding is that full patching ends the risk. Reporting shows that patching removes known vectors, but the operator adapts, and detection must cover user-facing applications, endpoint process behaviour, and authentication anomalies together.

What you will practise

  • Explain how watering hole compromise turns a trusted website into a delivery mechanism.
  • Distinguish undisclosed vulnerabilities from publicly known but unpatched ones.
  • Describe the post-compromise chain from loader to multiple backdoors.
  • Identify why credential and key theft accelerates movement across a network.
  • Explain why fast indicator expiry forces behavioural rather than signature detection.
  • Describe how to measure real patch coverage across browsers, plugins, and operating systems.
