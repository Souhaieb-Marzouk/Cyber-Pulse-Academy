T1588.007 Obtain Capabilities: Artificial Intelligence

T1588.007Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Artificial intelligence tools, such as chatbots, can write text and code when asked. This technique is about attackers using those public tools to help with their work. The tool itself is a normal service that anyone can use.

Why should you care?

These tools remove language and writing barriers, so attacks arrive in perfect wording and in your own language. Fake voices and video make a phone call seem real. Spellings and odd phrasing used to be warning signs, and they are much weaker signs now.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker opens a public artificial intelligence tool and explains what they need.
  2. The tool writes the messages, scripts, or research summaries in a few seconds.
  3. The attacker then reviews and edits that output before using it in the operation.
  4. They use the finished text in emails or phone calls to make a request sound believable.
  5. They may also create fake images, voices, or video of a real person.
  6. Defenders must then verify an unusual money or access request through another trusted channel.

Real-world example

Imagine a shared flat where a note appears on the fridge asking everyone to leave the spare key under the mat for a repair worker. The note is neat and polite and the handwriting is perfect. That neatness no longer proves anything, so you call the landlord before you move the key.

Key takeaways

  • Poor spelling and awkward wording are no longer reliable warning signs.
  • Verify unexpected money, access, or password requests using a known phone number.
  • Attackers can produce convincing messages in many languages at very low cost.

In more detail

This sub-technique covers an adversary using publicly accessible generative artificial intelligence tools, typically large language models, to support an operation instead of building capability in house. The model is treated as rented capability: the adversary outsources text generation, code drafting, translation, and summarisation to a service anyone can reach. That enables several distinct behaviours, including faster reconnaissance summarisation, fluent phishing text in languages the operator may not speak, and draft scripts or payload fragments that the operator then refines. It also lowers the barrier to offensive research, since a model can explain a vulnerability class or suggest how a parser might be attacked. A further behaviour is content generation beyond text: synthetic images, audio, and video built to impersonate a real person in a voice or video call. On the attacker side this activity leaves no victim telemetry at all, because it happens before contact. Detection therefore shifts to the effects and to the marks of machine generation: unusually fluent and grammatically consistent messages, generic phrasing that does not match a known colleague, and generated media that shows artefacts or fails a live challenge. Organisations should expect convincing lures at scale during peak business periods, and should rely on verification of requests out of band rather than on the quality of the text.

What you will practise

  • Explain how adversaries use publicly available artificial intelligence tools during operations.
  • Describe the tasks that artificial intelligence can automate for an attacker.
  • Explain why generated text and media weaken old warning signs such as poor spelling.
  • Describe verification habits that defend against impersonation by generated content.
  • Explain the main limitation of detecting this activity from victim telemetry.
