G1038 TA578

G1038Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

TA578 is a label used by security researchers for a criminal group that sells access to hacked companies. Its special trick is to make the victim phone a call centre. A person on the phone then pretends to help and asks the victim to install remote support software. That software is a legitimate tool that lets someone control a computer from far away.

Why should you care?

This attack avoids email attachments, so many filters see nothing wrong. The victim volunteers access because they believe they are talking to real support staff. Once remote control is given, the attacker can work openly, move around the network, and prepare a later ransomware attack. A person is part of the chain here. Training and help desk procedures stop these campaigns better than file scanning alone.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers send fake invoices or subscription notices through web contact forms.
  2. The message asks the reader to phone a number if the charge looks wrong.
  3. A friendly operator answers and offers to fix the problem remotely.
  4. The operator asks the victim to install a normal remote support tool.
  5. Once connected, the attacker looks around, steals data and installs more tools.
  6. The stolen access is then sold or passed to another criminal group.

Real-world example

Imagine a letter that says your electricity bill will rise unless you ring a helpline. You ring, a calm voice answers, and they offer to check your meter from their office. You unlock the door for them. Nothing was forced and nothing looked broken. The visitor simply used your own politeness and worry. Callback campaigns work the same way, and the phone call is the real door being opened.

Key takeaways

  • Never install remote support software because someone who phoned you asked you to.
  • Confirm a support request through a number or portal you already know and trust.
  • Report unexpected invoices and subscription notices to your security team.

In more detail

TA578 is a separate Proofpoint-assigned identifier for a financially motivated access broker whose reported signature is callback phishing, also called telephone-oriented attack delivery. Instead of asking the victim to click a link or open a document, the campaign convinces the recipient to telephone a number, where an operator walks them through installing remote support software or a loader. Public reporting describes the group using contact forms on legitimate websites and fabricated subscription renewal or invoice notices as the opening move, and distributing loaders that later pull additional tooling; the same cluster has been linked in vendor reporting to the delivery of BazarLoader. The technique is notable because the first stage carries almost no malicious file, which makes attachment sandboxing and static content inspection unproductive on its own. Evidence in a real environment lives in the mail and voice records and in the endpoint: messages routed through website contact forms, inbound mail threads with an unexpected support number, remote access tools installed outside the change process, and help desk tickets that reference a vendor nobody procured. The common misunderstanding is that remote support software is inherently suspicious; it is legitimate and widely used, so the signal is the context, the approval path and the person who initiated it. Because the human conversation is the exploit, user reporting and help desk scripts are more valuable here than signature content. Vendor naming for this cluster overlaps with other tracked sets and should not be treated as a fixed organisational identity.

What you will practise

  • Explain how callback phishing turns a telephone conversation into a technical intrusion.
  • Describe why campaigns without attachments evade content-based email inspection.
  • Identify endpoint and help desk signals that indicate unauthorised remote support software.
  • Explain the legitimate uses of remote administration tools and why context determines risk.
  • Design a user reporting and help desk verification process for suspected callback campaigns.
  • Describe the criminal economy that connects access brokers with ransomware operators.
