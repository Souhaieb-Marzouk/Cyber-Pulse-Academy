G0004 Ke3chang

G0004Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Ke3chang is the name researchers use for a long-running spying group. Several security companies believe it works for a state, and they link it to China. The group is known less for clever tricks and more for patience. It keeps attacking similar targets for many years.

Why should you care?

Spying groups like this one do not disappear. They wait, learn and return with new tools. If your team only blocks known file names, the next version slips past you. Understanding this group teaches you to watch behaviour, not just names. It also reminds you that reports from security companies are opinions, not proven facts.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The group sends emails with a harmful document or a link to a fake login page.
  2. A person opens the file or types a password, and the attacker gets a way in.
  3. Small helper programs, called backdoors, are placed on the computer to keep access.
  4. The backdoor starts again after a reboot by hiding in a startup setting or a scheduled task.
  5. The attackers copy passwords saved in browsers and mail programs so they can reuse them.
  6. They quietly collect email and documents for a long time before anyone notices.

Real-world example

Think of a person who wants to read the post in a large office. The guards once learned his face, so he grew a beard and changed his coat. The guards had memorised the old coat. The visitor still walks the same corridor, at the same quiet hour, and still asks for the same room. Watch the behaviour, because the coat will change again.

Key takeaways

  • Watch how intruders behave, because file names and hashes change quickly.
  • Treat company reports about who is behind an attack as opinions, not proof.
  • Protect saved browser and mail passwords, since they let attackers log in as staff.

In more detail

Ke3chang is a long-running espionage intrusion set that multiple commercial vendors assess as China-based and state-aligned, with public reporting tracing activity back more than a decade and describing sustained targeting of government ministries, diplomatic organisations, military-adjacent entities, and, more recently, non-governmental organisations and media outlets. Victim concentrations reported by vendors include the Middle East, Europe, and North America, with a stable interest in diplomatic and governmental communications. The group is most often described in reporting for its malware families rather than for a distinctive initial access trick: researchers have documented layered backdoors and downloaders that have been rewritten and recompiled repeatedly over the years, with components reported under names such as TidePool and RoyalCli, alongside credential theft from browsers and mail clients. Initial access is commonly described as spearphishing with malicious documents or links, sometimes combined with credential phishing pages that mimic legitimate webmail or file-sharing services, and persistence is reported through registry-based autostart mechanisms and scheduled tasks. The defender-relevant characteristic is longevity and iteration: the same intrusion set recurs against similar targets with refreshed tooling, so detections tied to specific file names or hashes decay quickly while behavioural detections remain useful. Detection effort is best spent on document-to-interpreter process chains, unusual autostart registry modification, anomalous outbound sessions from office and mail applications, and credential access patterns on endpoints belonging to diplomatic or executive staff. Attribution statements are third-party assessments and should be presented with their confidence level rather than as established fact.

What you will practise

  • Describe how iterative malware rewriting defeats indicator-based detection and what detection strategy survives it.
  • Build detections for registry autostart persistence and scheduled task creation on managed endpoints.
  • Recognise credential phishing pages that impersonate legitimate webmail and file-sharing services.
  • Hunt for office and mail applications initiating network connections inconsistent with baseline behaviour.
  • Prioritise monitoring for high-value diplomatic and executive accounts given the group's targeting pattern.
  • Assess the value of long-horizon retro-hunting when a recurring actor refreshes tooling with shared code.
  • Explain why vendor naming conventions for this cluster vary and how to reconcile them in reporting.
