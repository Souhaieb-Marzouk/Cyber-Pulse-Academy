T1590 Gather Victim Network Information

T1590ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Gathering network information means collecting facts about the networks a target organisation uses. The attacker looks at address ranges, domain names, and the way the network is built. This work happens before any break-in, during the planning stage.

Why should you care?

Everything an attacker learns here makes the next step easier and quieter. If your address ranges and public services are well documented online, an attacker can aim straight at them. That avoids the wide scanning which usually trips alarms. Good public records can therefore help an attacker stay below your detection. Knowing what is exposed also helps you decide what to reduce.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker collects your registered domain names and address ranges from public internet registry records.
  2. They study historical records and certificate logs to find hosts and subdomains that still exist.
  3. They may query your name servers directly to learn how your Domain Name System (DNS) records are arranged.
  4. Next they scan the ranges to see which addresses answer and which network services listen on them.
  5. Finally they link what they found to your business, guessing which systems matter most.
  6. All of this is saved and reused later, so the same details support many attacks over time.

Real-world example

Think of someone planning a first visit to a large office building. Before arriving, they study public maps to learn the street address and the bus routes. They read the company website to see which floor each department sits on. They look at older photographs to spot side doors that were added later. None of this involves touching the building, and it uses only information anyone can find. The guards at the front desk see nothing strange, because nothing has happened yet. Only when the person finally walks up and tries the handles does anyone notice. Network information gathering works the same way. The planning leaves almost no trace in your logs, and the first real signal usually arrives when the probing begins.

Key takeaways

  • Most network facts an attacker needs are already public, so exposure is about what you publish, not only what you block.
  • Your first visible clue is usually active probing in firewall and flow logs, so tune those logs to spot patterns, not single packets.
  • Keep a written list of your own approved scanners so you can ignore their traffic with confidence.

In more detail

Gathering victim network information is the broad collection of network-level facts an adversary assembles before touching a target: allocated address ranges, owned domain names, routing structure, and the services exposed to the internet. Collection usually starts passively, drawing on public registry records, historical Domain Name System datasets, and certificate transparency logs, and only later becomes active when the attacker probes the discovered ranges directly. The passive phase leaves no telemetry on the victim side at all, so the observable evidence is normally the active phase: repeated connection attempts from a small set of source addresses, sequential port sweeps, or half-open TCP handshakes recorded in firewall and flow logs. Separating that from ordinary internet background noise is the real difficulty, because vulnerability scanners, research crawlers, and search engine bots produce similar patterns. Defenders therefore get more value from tracking scan cadence, source address diversity, and known approved scanner ranges than from blocking individual addresses, and from knowing how much of their estate is documented in public sources.

What you will practise

  • Describe what network information gathering collects and why it happens before an intrusion.
  • Explain how public sources such as registry records and certificate logs expose network details.
  • Identify which log sources record the active part of this activity.
  • Distinguish hostile probing from authorised vulnerability scanning and normal internet background noise.
  • List practical steps that reduce the network footprint you expose publicly.
