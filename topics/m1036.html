M1036 Account Use Policies

M1036Enterprise MitigationVery hardMitigation

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Account use policies control how and when an account may sign in, not what it can reach. They can demand a managed device, a trusted network, or a second factor. They can restrict where logins come from and end idle sessions. A valid password is far less useful when it only works under the right conditions.

Why should you care?

When an attacker buys a password from a breach, the password alone should not be enough. Use policies make that true. They block sign-ins from strange countries or unmanaged laptops, and they stop accounts being used for jobs they were never meant for. Together with permission rules, they shrink what a stolen credential can do. They also expose logins that happen at odd hours or from odd places.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Decide the conditions that make a sign-in acceptable, such as a known device or location.
  2. Write the rule so it checks those conditions when the person signs in.
  3. Add session limits, so an idle login closes instead of staying open all night.
  4. Restrict service and shared accounts to the systems they actually need to use.
  5. Test the rules against real work, and fix anything that blocks a legitimate task.
  6. Review the exceptions often, because a temporary rule tends to become permanent.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital wristband. It proves who you are, but it does not let you into every ward. Your badge opens the rooms for your job, during your shift, at your hospital. If someone finds a lost badge, it will not open the pharmacy door at midnight in another building. Account use policies work like that. The credential is only the start; the conditions decide whether the door opens.

Key takeaways

  • Combine use policies with permission rules; neither is enough alone.
  • Make a stolen password useless when it comes from an unknown device.
  • Review the exceptions often, since every permanent one is a hole.

In more detail

Account use policies constrain how, when, from where, and through which session type an identity may authenticate, rather than what that identity is permitted to read or change. Typical controls include conditional access that demands a compliant device, a trusted network location, an acceptable risk score, or a phishing-resistant authenticator; logon-hour and source-address restrictions; session lifetime and idle timeouts; limits on concurrent sessions; denial of interactive logon for service and shared accounts; and automatic disconnection of dormant or orphaned identities. In practice the difficult part is not writing the rule but keeping it true. Hybrid estates evaluate policy in several places at once, so a rule that is strict in the cloud can be undermined by an on-premises path that still accepts the same credential, and legacy protocols plus emergency break-glass accounts routinely sit outside enforcement. Vendor and batch service accounts resist restriction because their scheduled work cannot satisfy interactive conditions, so teams either carve out permanent exceptions or move the workload onto a managed identity. Understanding what an account is actually used for normally has to precede meaningful restriction, which is why sign-in and privileged-role reporting carry so much weight. The control attacks the value of stolen credentials directly: a valid account is far less useful when the credential cannot be used from an unmanaged host, at an unusual hour, or into a remote desktop service from the internet. It reduces the yield of brute-force and spraying campaigns by removing the paths they target and it damages lateral movement by denying interactive logon where it is not required. Its failure modes are common and honest: exclusions that outlive their justification, session tokens stolen after a correct policy decision, legacy authentication paths, and misconfigured rules that either lock out operations or quietly apply to nobody.

What you will practise

  • Explain how account use policy differs from permission policy and why both are required for a defensible posture.
  • Design conditional access conditions that make a stolen credential unusable from an unmanaged or untrusted host.
  • Identify legacy authentication paths, service accounts, and break-glass identities that bypass enforcement.
  • Apply logon restrictions to shared and service accounts without breaking scheduled workloads.
  • Triage sign-in and privileged-role reports to find dormant, orphaned, and over-privileged identities.
  • Assess how stolen session tokens can defeat policy that was evaluated correctly at authentication time.
