Privilege Escalation

Offensive SecurityExtremeKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Privilege escalation is when someone turns ordinary access into stronger access they were never meant to have. It can happen on one machine, or across a whole company directory or cloud account. Vertical escalation raises the level of permission. Horizontal escalation moves to a different account with similar rights. Horizontal moves are often just a step towards a bigger goal.

Why should you care?

Most intruders start with a normal user account. That account alone cannot usually read the important data or change system settings. Escalation is the step that changes this. It often abuses ordinary features rather than a software bug: loose file permissions, saved passwords, or roles that are too broad. Reducing standing admin rights and patching quickly removes many of these easy paths before anyone uses them.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The intruder arrives with a low-level account, perhaps a normal user or service.
  2. They check what that account can read, run and change on the system.
  3. They look for weak spots, such as loose file rights or saved passwords.
  4. They use one weak spot to gain more rights, often through a normal feature.
  5. With higher rights they can create accounts, change settings, or reach data.

Real-world example

Picture a visitor in a hospital. Their badge opens the front lobby and the cafe. Then they notice a supply cupboard left unlocked. Inside is a staff badge on a hook. Now they can open the medicine store and the records room. Nobody hacked anything. They simply used a door that should have been shut. Privilege escalation in computer systems is the same: small lapses in permission add up to full authority.

Key takeaways

  • Escalation often uses normal features, not clever software bugs.
  • Give people only the rights their job needs, and no more.
  • Patch quickly, because old flaws are still a common path upward.

In more detail

Privilege escalation is the set of actions that convert ordinary user or service access into authority the account was never intended to hold, whether that means higher rights on the same host or rights that reach across a domain or cloud tenant. Vertical escalation raises the level of permission an identity holds, while horizontal escalation moves to a different identity with comparable rights and is often a stepping stone rather than an end in itself. The underlying causes are consistent across platforms: excessive standing privilege, misconfigured permissions on files, services or container runtimes, unpatched local software, credential material left in memory, configuration files or environment variables, and trust relationships that let a low-trust principal assume a high-trust role. Cloud and container estates add their own variants, including over-broad role assumption policies, instance metadata reachable from a workload, and container configurations that expose the host. Because escalation usually abuses legitimate functionality, signature-based detection alone is inadequate. Useful signals include process trees in which a user-facing application spawns a shell or administrative utility, changes to local or directory group membership, newly granted roles or permissions, and access to privileged interfaces outside a change window. Effective mitigation is architectural rather than detective: least privilege, removal of standing administrative rights in favour of just-in-time elevation, unique local administrator credentials, timely patching, and application control that limits what an unprivileged user can execute. Detection then concentrates on the residual cases where those controls fail or are bypassed.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to distinguish vertical from horizontal privilege escalation and give a realistic example of each.
  • You will be able to identify the misconfiguration classes that most often enable escalation on hosts, directories and cloud tenants.
  • You will be able to read process ancestry and permission-change telemetry to recognise escalation behaviour.
  • You will be able to prioritise privilege reduction using standing rights, administrative tiering and just-in-time access.
  • You will be able to explain how container configuration and instance metadata exposure change the escalation picture.
