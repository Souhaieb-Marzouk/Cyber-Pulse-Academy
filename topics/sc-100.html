Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

SC-100 is a Microsoft exam for experienced people who design security plans rather than run single tools. It asks how you would protect a whole company across its own data centres and several clouds. You must already hold another Microsoft security certification before you can book it.

Why should you care?

Tools alone do not make a company safe. Someone must decide which controls to buy, where to place them, and what to give up. Bad design wastes money and still leaves gaps. This exam checks that you can choose a plan and explain the trade-offs to managers. That skill keeps security projects realistic and affordable.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You begin with the business needs. You ask what data matters, what rules apply, and what the company can spend.
  2. Then you list the risks in plain terms, such as stolen logins, locked files or leaked customer records.
  3. Next you choose controls for each risk. You compare options by cost, effort and how much they annoy users.
  4. You draw the design. It shows how identity, devices, networks, data and monitoring fit together across environments.
  5. After that you plan the rollout in stages, so old systems keep working while new protections arrive.
  6. Finally you set measures, such as how fast you detect and fix problems, and you review the plan often.

Real-world example

Imagine you are the architect for a family home, not the person fitting the locks. The family tells you they run a business from the kitchen and keep valuables in the study. You decide where walls go, which doors need alarms, and how people escape a fire. You also warn them that a cheaper design saves money now but costs more later. SC-100 tests that kind of planning, not the fitting of a single lock.

Key takeaways

  • Architects choose between options, and every choice has a cost and a downside.
  • Read the whole requirement first, because one hidden constraint can rule out a good answer.
  • A plan is only useful if ordinary teams can build and run it.

In more detail

Microsoft SC-100 Cybersecurity Architect is an expert-level certification for professionals who design and evolve security strategy rather than operate individual controls. Candidates are typically senior security engineers, solution architects, consultants or security leads who must translate business risk and regulatory obligation into an architecture spanning on-premises, Azure, multicloud and hybrid estates. Microsoft requires an active qualifying prerequisite certification before the exam can be booked, so this credential sits above the associate-level security exams rather than alongside them. For an employer the credential signals that the holder can choose between competing control designs, defend the trade-offs in cost, resilience and user friction, and communicate a Zero Trust roadmap to executives without over-engineering it. The exam is proctored and case-study heavy: most items present a scenario with stated requirements and ask which architecture, control set or sequence best satisfies them, with some drag-and-drop and multi-part design tasks. Because there is no lab component, the assessment is almost entirely about judgement under constraint. Practice changes results because the most common failure mode is selecting a technically sound control that quietly violates a stated business constraint such as data residency, existing licence investment, migration downtime or backward compatibility. Working repeatedly through reference architectures, design reviews and migration patterns turns constraint-reading into a habit, which is what separates a passing design answer from a defensible one.

Exam domains and objectives

  • Zero Trust strategy and architecture: Translate business requirements into a Zero Trust blueprint, define posture targets, and select identity-first controls across hybrid, Azure and multicloud estates.
  • Resilience and recovery design: Design segmentation, backup, immutability and recovery sequencing that keeps ransomware from becoming an existential event.
  • Identity and access design: Specify identity governance, privileged access, conditional access, passwordless authentication and workload identity patterns for human and non-human identities.
  • Security operations strategy: Define SOC structure, SIEM and SOAR architecture, threat intelligence integration, and the metrics such as mean time to detect and respond that steer the programme.
  • Governance, risk and compliance: Map regulatory and contractual obligations to technical controls, design for data residency and sovereignty, and align to frameworks such as ISO 27001 and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework.
  • Infrastructure security: Design protection for servers, endpoints, OT and IoT, containers, Kubernetes and hybrid workloads, including patching and configuration baselines at scale.
  • Data and application security: Specify classification, encryption and key management, secrets handling, and secure development lifecycle controls for applications, APIs and data stores.
  • Evaluation and recommendation: Compare competing technical options, produce architecture recommendations for executive stakeholders, and justify cost, risk and operational trade-offs.
  • Emerging and third-party risk: Incorporate cloud-native services, AI-enabled workloads and supply chain dependencies into architecture decisions and assurance activities.
