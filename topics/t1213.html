T1213 Data from Information Repositories

T1213CollectionExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Information repositories are shared places where a company stores documents and messages. Examples are team wikis, file sharing sites, chat tools and databases. This technique means an attacker searches those places for useful information. They often find passwords, network maps and private customer data.

Why should you care?

Companies keep much of their useful knowledge in shared tools. A single login may open hundreds of documents. If an attacker gets one valid account, they can read a great deal without breaking anything. They may find test passwords, network diagrams or plans for the future. That information then helps them reach other systems or pressure the company.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gets a valid account, often through a fake login page or a stolen session.
  2. They search the shared tools for words such as password, key, diagram or confidential.
  3. They read pages, download files and follow links to other internal systems.
  4. They look for test credentials, pieces of source code and customer records.
  5. They may change sharing settings so that private documents become open to everyone.
  6. Finally they copy the useful data out, sometimes by sending it to cloud storage.

Real-world example

Think of an office with one large filing room. Every employee has a key to the door, because people need documents to do their jobs. A visitor who copies one key can walk in and read anything, from phone lists to the alarm code. Nothing is broken and no door is forced. The room simply trusted everyone who came in, and nobody counted how many files left the room.

Key takeaways

  • Shared document tools are a rich target, because one login can open a huge amount.
  • Search your own wikis, chat and code for secrets before an attacker does it for you.
  • Review who can open each repository, and remove public or overly broad access.

In more detail

Data from information repositories is the collection stage in which an adversary mines the collaboration and storage systems a business already uses, instead of hunting for files on a single endpoint. These platforms hold policies, network and architecture diagrams, technical documentation, test credentials left in notes or code, project schedules, source snippets and customer records, and most of them are reachable from any authenticated account. The behaviour is largely ordinary search and read activity: queries, page views, file downloads, exports, permission changes and external sharing links. That is exactly why it is difficult to separate from daily work, and why detection leans on volume, breadth, timing and the identity involved. Reliable signals include one account touching far more repositories than its role requires, bulk exports of previously untouched content, new external share links, and access shortly after a credential is first used. Adversaries also abuse over-broad or public permissions that the organisation never intended to grant.

What you will practise

  • Define information repositories and give three common examples of them.
  • Explain why one valid account can expose a very large amount of stored data.
  • Describe the search, download and sharing behaviours that indicate data collection.
  • Identify access patterns, such as unusual breadth or volume, that point to mining.
  • Outline steps that reduce public and overly broad access to repositories.
