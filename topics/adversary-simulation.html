Adversary Simulation

Offensive SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Adversary simulation is a test where your own team copies the behaviour of a real attacker. The team uses the same methods that a known attacker uses. The goal is to check whether your defences notice and respond. It is a planned exercise, not a real break in.

Why should you care?

A security tool that is installed is not the same as a tool that works. Reports and dashboards can look healthy while real attacks pass unseen. Simulation shows where detection and response actually fail. It also gives defenders a safe chance to practise together. The findings usually change where the team spends its time.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You start by learning which attackers really do target organisations like yours today.
  2. The team picks a small set of attacker behaviours that are worth testing.
  3. You agree the rules of engagement, including timing and what is off limits.
  4. The team performs the agreed steps and records what it did and when.
  5. Defenders work normally during the whole exercise, so their response stays realistic.
  6. Afterwards everyone compares the actions with the alerts and closes the gaps.

Real-world example

Think of a fire drill in an office. The alarm is not a real fire, but the staff still walk to the exits. The building manager watches how long it takes and where people get stuck. Nobody is blamed for the drill itself, only for ignoring the alarm. Adversary simulation is the fire drill for security alerts, and the written report is the real point.

Key takeaways

  • Start from intelligence about real threats, not from a list of favourite tools.
  • Agree the rules of engagement in writing before anything runs.
  • Measure detection and response, and turn every gap into a tracked fix.

In more detail

Adversary simulation is the practice of emulating a specific threat actor's known tactics, techniques and procedures against your own environment in order to test whether detection and response actually work. It is narrower and more deliberate than a penetration test, which normally optimises for finding and chaining exploitable vulnerabilities, and more targeted than automated breach and attack simulation, which replays a fixed technique library across many controls. A sound exercise starts from threat intelligence about who realistically targets your sector and which behaviours matter, defines those behaviours explicitly, and agrees rules of engagement with defenders in advance, including timing, out-of-scope systems and a stop condition. The valuable output is not a count of shells but evidence about which steps generated telemetry, which produced alerts, and how quickly the team contained the activity. The mistake organisations make is running the exercise as a covert contest scored against the blue team, so the detection gaps it exposes are never closed and the next test rediscovers them.

What you will practise

  • Explain what adversary simulation is and how it differs from a penetration test.
  • Describe how threat intelligence shapes the behaviours chosen for an exercise.
  • List the elements of rules of engagement that protect the environment during a test.
  • Interpret simulation results in terms of detection coverage and response time.
  • Explain why defensive teams should be involved before, during and after the exercise.
