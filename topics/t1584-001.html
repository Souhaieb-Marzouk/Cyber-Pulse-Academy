T1584.001 Compromise Infrastructure: Domains

T1584.001Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A domain is the name of a website, and a subdomain is a smaller name inside it, such as mail.example[.]com. This sub-technique is about an attacker taking control of a name rather than a computer. The parent technique covers compromising other people's infrastructure. Here the stolen asset is the domain itself.

Why should you care?

If an attacker controls your name, they can point it anywhere. Users type the correct address and still arrive at a criminal site. Mail may be intercepted or forged. Because existing services keep working, nobody notices for weeks. Reputation earned over years is handed straight to the attacker.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker targets the email account or help desk that controls the domain's registration.
  2. With that access they change the name server records or the contact details for the domain.
  3. Alternatively they find a subdomain pointing at a resource the owner has already deleted.
  4. They register that abandoned resource themselves, so the trusted name serves their content.
  5. They add new subdomains while leaving old records untouched, so normal service continues.
  6. They obtain certificates for the stolen names, which makes the fake sites appear trustworthy.

Real-world example

Imagine a shop that moves premises but forgets to remove its old sign. A stranger rents the empty unit, keeps the sign, and greets customers inside. The address is correct and the sign is genuine, so customers hand over money to the wrong business.

Key takeaways

  • Every DNS record should point at something that still exists and is still owned by you.
  • Registrar account security and renewal reminders protect your name, not just your servers.
  • Certificate and DNS change alerts give you warning before users are affected.

In more detail

This sub-technique covers taking control of a domain or subdomain rather than a server. The parent technique describes compromising third-party assets generally; here the asset is name resolution and registration itself. Registration hijacking means changing a domain's records without the owner's consent, typically by taking over the registrant's email account, persuading a registrar help desk, exploiting a lapse in the renewal process, or abusing a cloud service that manages DNS. Subdomain takeover is different and often easier: an organisation leaves a DNS record pointing at a resource that no longer exists, and the attacker claims that resource so the trusted name resolves to their system. Domain shadowing keeps all existing records intact while adding malicious subdomains, so services are not disrupted and the additions can sit unnoticed for a long time. The evidence is name based, so certificate transparency logs, registrar change alerts, passive DNS history, and unexpected new host records matter more than endpoint telemetry.

What you will practise

  • Explain why stolen domains bypass reputation-based filtering.
  • Describe how DNS record hygiene prevents abandoned subdomains.
