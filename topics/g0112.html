G0112 Windshift

G0112Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Windshift is the name for a spying group that has been active since about 2017. Public reports also call it Bahamut. It targets chosen people, not whole networks. Its victims work in government and important services in the Middle East.

Why should you care?

This group talks to its targets first. It builds trust on social media before sending anything harmful. That means a firewall never sees the first contact. It also attacks phones, which often carry no company security software. It can listen to calls, read messages and follow location. A single trusted conversation can expose a person's whole working life. If your security plan covers only office computers, this kind of attack passes underneath it.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The intruders create a false online identity, such as a journalist or an activist.
  2. They contact the chosen person and build a friendly relationship over days or weeks.
  3. They send a link or a file that looks like part of that conversation.
  4. The file runs hidden software that watches the computer and reports back.
  5. On phones, they add a hidden app or a device profile that grants wide access.
  6. The software films, records sound, reads messages, and follows the person's location.

Real-world example

Think of a thief who does not break windows. He joins the same evening class as you, helps you carry your bags, and learns which door you use at night. Weeks later, when he asks to borrow your spare key for an emergency, you hand it over gladly. Nothing was forced and nothing looked suspicious, because the trust came first. Spying that starts with a friendly message works the same way. The attacker invests time in the relationship, and the harmful file arrives as a natural part of a conversation you believe is genuine.

Key takeaways

  • A friendly online contact can be an intruder building trust before an attack.
  • Phones need protection too, because they carry messages, calls and location.
  • Check who really signed a program before you trust it.

In more detail

Windshift, also tracked in public reporting as Bahamut, is described in MITRE ATT&CK as a threat group active since at least 2017 that targets specific individuals for surveillance in government departments and critical infrastructure across the Middle East. The distinguishing feature is the blend of social and technical tradecraft rather than any single implant. Reported activity includes fake personas on social media and messaging platforms used to build trust with a named individual before a link or attachment is delivered, which means the delivery channel is often a personal conversation that no corporate mail gateway ever sees. Reporting covers implants for both desktop and mobile platforms, including capabilities to capture audio and video, track location, read messages and contacts, and enumerate installed software, with an additional step of installing a management profile on mobile devices so that the attacker gains a durable, trusted position on the device rather than relying on a single app. On the desktop side reporting describes custom URL schemes registered through compromised websites, visual disguises such as document icons and screensaver style file extensions, and code signing certificates that had already been revoked, which suggests a deliberate attempt to borrow the appearance of legitimacy while accepting that validation will fail. Some campaigns reported in the source material were region locked, so a sample collected outside the target area may be inert and tell an analyst nothing. Detection therefore leans on social platform awareness, mobile device management review, certificate revocation checking, and monitoring for unusual registered URL handlers rather than on file signatures alone.

What you will practise

  • Explain why targeting named individuals changes the detection surface compared with mass campaigns.
  • Describe how a fake persona on a social platform becomes a malware delivery channel.
  • Explain what a mobile device management profile is and why an attacker would install one.
  • Explain why a revoked code signing certificate is still a useful clue for defenders.
  • Describe how region locking in a campaign can mislead an analyst who collects a sample elsewhere.
