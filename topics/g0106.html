G0106 Rocke

G0106Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Rocke is the name for a group that steals computer power instead of documents. Public reporting calls this cryptojacking. The group breaks into servers and runs hidden software that mines digital coins. This slows the servers down and costs the owner money for power.

Why should you care?

People often treat hidden mining as a small problem, but the intruder still has full control of your server. The same access could later be sold or used to steal data. Mining also uses electricity, processor time and cloud budget that you pay for. The group works hard to stay hidden, so it removes protective software and deletes logs. If you ignore a mining alert, you are leaving a real break in unrepaired.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The group scans the internet for servers that run old or unprotected services.
  2. It breaks in through a known weakness or a service that needs no password.
  3. A short script installs hidden mining software deep inside the server. The miner then runs for months while the server appears to work normally.
  4. The script adds scheduled jobs and services so mining starts again after a restart.
  5. It removes competing mining programs and blocks their network addresses with firewall rules.
  6. The mining program hides itself, and the group deletes logs and protective tools.

Real-world example

Imagine a neighbour who plugs an electric heater into your outside socket and runs it all winter. Nothing is stolen from your house, and no door is broken. Your bill goes up, and the socket is now in use by someone else all day. If a real burglar visits later, they will find that socket ready for them. Hidden mining works the same way. The intruder quietly borrows your servers for profit, and your costs quietly rise. The important part is not the coins. It is that a stranger already has a working key to your building.

Key takeaways

  • Hidden mining is still a real intrusion, not just a performance problem.
  • Unexpected processor load on a server deserves a proper investigation.
  • Missing log files and disabled protective tools are serious warning signs.

In more detail

Rocke is described in MITRE ATT&CK as an alleged Chinese speaking adversary whose main objective appears to be cryptojacking, meaning the theft of victim computing power to mine cryptocurrency rather than the theft of data. The entry notes that the name comes from an email address used to create the wallet that collected the proceeds, and that researchers have observed overlaps with activity tracked as the Iron Cybercrime Group, an overlap the entry states has not been confirmed. That unconfirmed link is worth keeping in mind, because it means the cluster boundary is drawn around shared tooling and hosting rather than around a verified owner. The behaviour described is distinctive. Initial access is reported through exploitation of public facing application servers and through scanning for exposed services such as remote administration and data stores that are reachable without a password. From there the activity is script heavy: shell and Python based tooling installs a miner, sets it to restart through scheduled jobs and system services, then works to remove rivals by killing competing mining processes and blocking their network ranges with host firewall rules. Evasion is a core part of the described behaviour, including uninstalling protective software, clearing system logs, hiding processes by intercepting shared library loading, and saving mining binaries under names borrowed from legitimate software. The defensive lesson is that cryptojacking is not a harmless nuisance: it consumes capacity, it requires a real intrusion with real persistence, and the same access can be resold or repurposed. Evidence to look for includes sustained processor load without a matching business reason, unexpected autostart entries, new rules in the host firewall, and missing log files on affected servers.

What you will practise

  • Explain what cryptojacking is and why it is treated as an intrusion rather than a nuisance.
  • Describe how exposed or unauthenticated services become initial access for this activity.
  • Identify persistence artefacts such as scheduled jobs, startup scripts and system services on servers.
  • Recognise evidence that an adversary removed protective tools or cleared log files.
  • Explain how unconfirmed attribution to a related criminal group should change confidence in reporting.
