T1589.003 Gather Victim Identity Information: Employee Names

T1589.003ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Employee names are the names of the people who work at a company. This technique is about attackers collecting those names. They use them to guess email addresses and to make their messages sound real.

Why should you care?

A message that names your manager and your project is much harder to doubt. Names also let attackers build a list of who to target, especially staff who can approve payments or access. Most of this information is already public, so there is nothing to block.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker identifies the target company and the teams they care about.
  2. They read the company pages, the press mentions, and the professional networking profiles.
  3. They note down each person's name, job title, and the team they belong to.
  4. They then infer how the company builds its email addresses from real names.
  5. They use those names to write messages that sound personal and urgent to the reader.
  6. Defenders should treat a correct name as a clue, never as proof of identity.

Real-world example

Imagine a hotel guest calls the front desk and asks for an envelope held for a specific guest. The receptionist hears the correct surname and hands it over. The name was printed on the conference list in the lobby, so the caller only had to read the board first.

Key takeaways

  • A correct name in a message is not proof that the sender is genuine.
  • Limit public detail about staff who can approve access or payments.
  • Verify unexpected requests through a second, trusted contact channel.

In more detail

This sub-technique covers collecting the names of a target organisation's employees, which is the raw material for everything that follows in a social engineering campaign. Names arrive from public sources: corporate team pages and press releases, professional networking profiles, conference speaker lists, regulatory filings, and older breach datasets that are still traded. The information is usually gathered in layers rather than in one pass, because a name becomes far more useful when it can be paired with a role, a team, and a manager. Names support two distinct follow on behaviours. They allow the reconstruction of email addresses from an observed format, and they allow the construction of credible lures that reference real people, real projects, and real reporting lines. That second use is what makes a message hard to dismiss, since a request that names your own manager and a plausible deadline does not look generic. Detection on the collection side is essentially impossible, because reading a public website is not visible to the site owner in any useful way. The practical defensive response is exposure management and habit. Publish less about staff who handle payments, access requests, or infrastructure, and train people to verify requests through a second channel rather than treating a correct name as proof of identity.
