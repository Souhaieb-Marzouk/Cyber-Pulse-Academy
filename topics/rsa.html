RSA

CryptographyVery hardKeyword

RSA is a public-key cryptosystem whose security rests on the presumed hardness of factoring the product of two large primes, combined with modular exponentiation over that product. A key pair consists of a modulus derived from two secret primes, a public exponent usually 65537, and a private exponent derived through the extended Euclidean algorithm. RSA supports both encryption and digital signatures, and it is normally used inside a padding or encoding scheme rather than on raw data, because textbook RSA is deterministic and malleable. Encryption uses OAEP padding; signatures use PSS or the older PKCS#1 v1.5 scheme, and the padding choice is where most real-world breaks have occurred. Practical risks include small key sizes that are within reach of well-resourced factoring efforts, shared or non-random primes generated by faulty entropy sources, Bleichenbacher-style padding oracle attacks against v1.5 encryption, and signature verification code that ignores algorithm identifiers or accepts the wrong hash. Signing is also much slower than verifying, which shapes how RSA is deployed in certificate chains and protocol handshakes. Throughout their lifecycle, RSA keys must be protected against exfiltration, rotated before weakness accumulates, and retired with their certificates, which is why hardware modules and inventory of key material are operational controls rather than cryptographic footnotes.

What you will practise

  • Explain the mathematical structure of an RSA key pair and why factoring the modulus compromises both confidentiality and authenticity.
  • Distinguish OAEP encryption padding from PSS and PKCS#1 v1.5 signature schemes, and state when each is acceptable.
  • Describe how a padding oracle attack against RSA encryption is exploited and how constant-time error handling mitigates it.
  • Identify weak key generation in an asset inventory, including undersized moduli and duplicated or non-random primes.
  • Interpret certificate chain and signature algorithm fields during a review of TLS or code-signing infrastructure.
  • Assess the migration path away from RSA towards elliptic curve and post-quantum signature schemes in a live environment.
