M1051 Update Software

M1051Enterprise MitigationVery hardMitigation

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Updating software means replacing old, flawed code with the fixed version the maker has released. It covers operating systems, applications, network devices, firmware, and the libraries your own code depends on. The hard part is rarely the update itself. It is knowing what you own, testing the change, and reaching every device, including the ones nobody is watching.

Why should you care?

Attackers scan the internet for systems with known, unpatched flaws. Once a fix is published, the flaw becomes easier to find and use, so delay is dangerous. Updating closes the most common way in. It also removes the flaws that let an attacker grow from one machine to many. Where an update is impossible, you need another control and a written record of the risk.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Keep an inventory of every device and piece of software, including cloud services.
  2. Watch for new fixes and rank them by severity, exposure, and reports of active abuse.
  3. Fix internet-facing systems first, often within days instead of waiting for the monthly cycle.
  4. Roll updates out in rings, starting with a small group, then the rest.
  5. Restart the service and then confirm the new version is really running.
  6. For anything you cannot update, add a compensating control and set a review date.

Real-world example

Think of a leaking roof. Fixing it while the weather is calm is cheap and quick. Waiting until a storm arrives means water damage in every room. Some buildings also cannot be closed for repairs, so you put a bucket down and write a note to fix it properly. Software updates work the same way, and the storm is an attacker scanning for the flaw you did not fix.

Key takeaways

  • You cannot patch what you have not inventoried, so start with a full list.
  • Fix internet-facing systems first, and always confirm the restart happened.
  • When patching is impossible, record the risk and add another control.

In more detail

Updating software is the systematic replacement of vulnerable code with vendor-fixed versions across every asset class an organisation operates, from operating systems and hypervisors through network appliances, container base images, firmware, and third-party libraries. The operational reality is dominated by inventory and scheduling rather than by the patch action itself, because you cannot update what you do not know exists. The control therefore rests on authoritative asset discovery, software composition analysis, a bill of materials for everything the organisation builds, and a change process capable of reaching production servers, field devices, remote laptops, and appliances that are only manageable through a vendor console. Effective programmes rank risk instead of chasing one score: vendor severity, evidence of exploitation in the wild, and whether the asset is internet-facing jointly decide what enters an emergency channel with a short deadline, while lower-risk internal findings wait for a scheduled ring. Where patching is genuinely impossible, teams apply compensating controls and record time-boxed risk acceptance with a named owner. The control breaks the most common initial-access path, exploitation of unpatched public-facing applications and remote-access gateways, and it removes the client-side and local privilege-escalation flaws that follow user execution. Its limitations are well understood: firmware and industrial systems rarely tolerate a reboot, shadow IT and embedded appliances escape inventory, vendors ship delayed or partial fixes, zero-days exist before patches do, and the update channel is itself a supply-chain target. Updating also fails quietly when installation reports success but the service is never restarted, or when an old container image is rescheduled from a registry that still holds it.

What you will practise

  • Explain how asset inventory, composition analysis, and risk ranking combine to produce a defensible patch cadence.
  • Distinguish emergency remediation of internet-facing systems from routine monthly rings and define the trigger for each.
  • Identify the asset classes that routinely escape inventory and describe how to bring them under management.
  • Apply compensating controls and time-boxed risk acceptance where patching genuinely cannot occur.
  • Assess the supply-chain risk introduced by the software update channel itself.
  • Verify that a patch has taken effect at runtime rather than trusting successful installation output.
  • Prioritise remediation using vendor severity, exploitation evidence, and exposure together.
