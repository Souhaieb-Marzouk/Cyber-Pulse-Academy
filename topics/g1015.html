G1015 Scattered Spider

G1015Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Scattered Spider is the name for a group of attackers who speak English well. They break into companies mainly by talking to people. Public reports say they have been active since about 2022. They target telephone companies, call centres, hotels, and gaming firms, and they often work with ransomware groups. They are known for being fast, persistent, and very convincing on the phone.

Why should you care?

This group attacks the help desk instead of the login page. It calls support, pretends to be an employee, and asks for a password reset or a new phone number link. Your strongest password does not help if someone else resets it for them. One time codes sent by text message can also be intercepted if the phone number itself is moved. These attacks look like ordinary support calls. Staff may not realise they are being targeted until the damage is done.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers gather personal details about an employee from public sources and social networks.
  2. They call the help desk and pretend to be that employee, using the details they collected.
  3. They ask for a password reset or for a new authentication device to be enrolled.
  4. They may also move the victim's phone number to a device they control.
  5. With the account open, they add their own devices and create backup login paths.
  6. They move into cloud systems, steal data, and sometimes hand access to a ransomware crew.

Real-world example

Picture a bank with a very strong vault door. The clerk at the counter will open it for anyone who knows the account holder's birthday and mother's name. The thieves never touch the vault door. They queue at the counter with a friendly smile and a paper full of correct facts. In this kind of attack the birthday facts are public profiles. The speed and confidence of the caller does the rest.

Key takeaways

  • Identity resets are a security control, so treat help desk requests like a locked door, not paperwork.
  • Move away from text message codes where you can and use app based or hardware sign-in instead.
  • Require strong verification for password resets, new device enrolment, and phone number changes.

In more detail

Scattered Spider, tracked under names including Octo Tempest, UNC3944, Storm-0875, and Roasted 0ktapus, is a financially motivated, English speaking intrusion set that public reporting describes as active since around 2022. It is unusual because its strongest skills are social rather than technical. Documented behaviour includes help desk impersonation to obtain password resets, subscriber identity module swapping to intercept one time codes, push notification bombing to wear a user down, and recruiting insiders. Telephone carriers, business process outsourcing firms, gaming, and hospitality organisations have featured prominently, and intrusions have progressed into cloud and identity platforms, virtual infrastructure, and in several cases ransomware deployment through partnerships with established crews. The operational pattern is identity-first: the attackers aim at the recovery process rather than the password itself, because that process is often weaker than the login. Multi-factor authentication is necessary but not sufficient, since help desk verification and number porting are frequently the path of least resistance. Attribution to a specific cluster is contested in places, so public reporting should be cited as reporting.

What you will practise

  • Explain how help desk impersonation defeats controls even when strong passwords are in use.
  • Describe subscriber identity module swapping and why text message codes are a weak factor.
  • Recognise multi-factor authentication fatigue and push bombing as social, not technical, attacks.
  • List the identity platform changes an attacker makes to keep access after a reset.
  • Design identity verification steps for support staff that survive a well researched caller.
  • Explain how identity access is handed to ransomware operators and what that means for impact.
