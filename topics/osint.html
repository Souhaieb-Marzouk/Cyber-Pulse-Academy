Open-Source Intelligence

Threat IntelligenceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Open source intelligence means finding and studying information that is already public, in order to answer a question. Open source here means openly available, not open source software. The work is mostly careful reading, comparing sources and writing down where each fact came from. It is used by security teams to understand what an outsider can already learn about them.

Why should you care?

Attackers often begin by reading public information, so your team should read it first and fix what it exposes. A public document can reveal software versions, staff names and internal project names. If you do not know your own public footprint, you cannot protect it. The other risk is believing weak information too quickly, which sends an investigation in the wrong direction. Writing down the source and the date lets a colleague check your work.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Start with a clear question, such as what an outsider can learn about our company.
  2. Collect from public places like registries, websites, job posts and code hosting sites.
  3. Record the exact source and the date you looked at it, every single time.
  4. Compare several independent sources before you treat something as true.
  5. Grade each item by how reliable the source is and how directly it supports the claim.
  6. Write the finding separately from your conclusion and state how confident you are.

Real-world example

Think of a journalist checking a rumour about a company. One person says the factory is closing. A good journalist does not publish that alone. They look for a public notice, ask a second independent source and check the date. Then they write what was confirmed, what is still unclear and where each detail came from. A careless journalist repeats the rumour as fact, and the mistake spreads to other papers.

Key takeaways

  • Open source means publicly available, and the analysis matters as much as the collecting.
  • Always note the source and the date, so anyone can check and revisit your finding.
  • Keep what you observed separate from what you concluded, and state your confidence.

In more detail

Open-source intelligence is the collection and analysis of information from publicly available sources to answer a specific intelligence question, together with the discipline of judging how reliable each piece is. The term refers to the sourcing, not to how secret the conclusion is: a report built entirely from public material can still be classified once it is combined and assessed. Sources include corporate registries, job advertisements, certificate transparency logs, domain registration records, code repositories, satellite and mapping imagery, social media, court filings and technical documentation. In security work it supports attack surface discovery, attribution and context building during investigations, and pre-engagement scoping for authorised testing. The characteristic failure is not finding too little but trusting too much: a single unattributed post is repeated until it hardens into fact, gaps are filled by inference, and the analyst never records the collection date, so the assessment cannot be revisited when the source changes. Rigour looks like source grading, corroboration across independent sources, date stamping, and separating what was observed from what was concluded, plus staying inside legal and ethical limits such as terms of service, privacy law and the rules of engagement for the target in question.

What you will practise

  • Define open-source intelligence and explain what makes a source open and public.
  • Describe how public information such as registries and job posts supports attack surface discovery.
  • Apply source grading and corroboration before accepting a claim as established.
  • Distinguish observed facts from analytical conclusions when writing an intelligence product.
  • Explain the legal and ethical limits that apply to collecting information about a target.
