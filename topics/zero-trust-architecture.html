Zero Trust Architecture

Identity & AccessHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Zero trust architecture is a way of designing systems that checks every request. Being inside the office network no longer makes you trusted. Each request must prove who is asking and why.

Why should you care?

Attackers who get past the network edge used to move freely inside. With zero trust, one stolen laptop opens far fewer doors. Remote and cloud work also blur the old idea of an inside network. So checking each request fits how people work today.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Every user signs in with strong proof, such as a second factor.
  2. The system also checks the device, like patch level and disk encryption.
  3. Each request is judged on its own by a central policy decision point.
  4. Only the smallest amount of access needed for that request is granted.
  5. Resources stay hidden behind a broker instead of being openly reachable.
  6. Every decision is logged so odd patterns can be reviewed later.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital pharmacy. Staff used to enter the building once and then walk anywhere. Now every cabinet needs a separate badge check, a fingerprint and a reason. Being inside the building gives you nothing. Each request is judged on who you are and what you need right then.

Key takeaways

  • Network location is not proof of trust in this design.
  • Verify identity and device before granting the least access needed.
  • Expect a gradual migration, not a single product you switch on.

In more detail

Zero trust architecture is a design approach in which no request is trusted because of where it comes from, and every access decision is made per request from identity, device state, context and policy. The mechanism replaces the implicit trust of a network perimeter with an explicit policy engine: a subject authenticates strongly, is authorised against the resource it wants, and is granted the narrowest access that works, often through a broker that hides the resource itself. Its distinguishing constraint is that the network location of a device stops being a credential, so a laptop on the internal subnet has no more standing than one connecting from a cafe. In real environments it appears as strong multi-factor authentication, device compliance checks, least-privilege authorisation, microsegmentation, and logging of every access decision for later review. The mistake people usually make is treating it as a product to buy or as simply deleting the internal network: it is an incremental migration of policy, and most organisations run it alongside existing controls for years. Poorly planned, it produces constant prompts and blocked work, which is why the user experience is part of the design.

What you will practise

  • Explain the core idea of zero trust architecture in plain language.
  • Describe how per-request policy decisions replace perimeter trust.
  • Explain the roles of identity, device state and context in an access decision.
  • Recognise how microsegmentation limits movement after a compromise.
  • Describe the practical migration steps and user-experience trade-offs.
