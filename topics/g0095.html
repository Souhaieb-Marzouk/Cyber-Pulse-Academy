G0095 Machete

G0095Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Machete is the name given to a group of attackers who mostly target organisations in Latin America. Public reports link them to spying on governments, armies, embassies, and energy companies. They use fake documents in emails, and the documents look related to the victim's real work. Security companies also call them El Machete or APT-C-43.

Why should you care?

Their messages are written in the local language and fit the target's daily work, so they look normal. A worker who opens one attachment can give away years of internal documents. The attackers also quietly record what is typed and what is on screen. Small organisations with no security team are common targets, and they often have no way to notice until much later.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers pick a target, often a government office, embassy, or energy company.
  2. They write an email in the local language with a work related subject line.
  3. The attached document or archive hides a program inside it.
  4. When the victim opens it, the hidden program starts and stays on the computer.
  5. The program records keystrokes, takes screenshots, and copies documents.
  6. Stolen files leave the network slowly, mixed with normal web traffic.

Real-world example

Think of a courier who brings a parcel to an office reception desk. The parcel looks exactly like the supplier deliveries that arrive every morning, and the label is in the local language. The receptionist signs for it and puts it on the desk inside. Nobody checks the sender, because the parcel matches a pattern they see daily. The contents then sit quietly in the office for months. That is how this group works: the delivery looks routine, and the routine is what makes it work.

Key takeaways

  • An attachment in your own language that fits your work can still be hostile.
  • Never open unexpected documents from outside senders on a work device.
  • Slow, small uploads to a few strange hosts are worth investigating.

In more detail

Machete, also reported as El Machete and APT-C-43, is a Spanish-language espionage cluster that public reporting links to activity against government, military, embassy and energy-related organisations in Latin America and, less often, in Europe. The intrusion set is best known for a long-lived custom backdoor family delivered through spearphishing, where the lure is a document or an archive that appears to relate to the target's own work and the attached file is a self-extracting archive or a document that drops an executable. Once running, the tooling in public analyses supports keystroke capture, screenshot collection, file theft and command execution, with exfiltration over common web protocols so the traffic blends with normal browsing. Infection chains have been documented that use removable media and shared drives as a secondary spread path, which matters in environments where those channels are not controlled. Reporting on this group is comparatively sparse and mostly vendor-driven, so several details, including the exact sponsoring state and the full scope of victims, are assessed with only low to moderate confidence; analysts should treat named campaigns as attributed observations rather than settled fact. Defensively, Machete is interesting because the intrusion depends on user interaction with a document rather than on a remote exploit. The durable evidence tends to be in mail gateway records, endpoint process trees that show a document reader spawning a script or executable, persistence entries in user-level autorun locations, and proxy or firewall logs showing repeated small uploads to a small set of external hosts.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a document lure replaces a remote exploit in this intrusion set.
  • Describe the collection behaviour of a backdoor that records keystrokes and screens.
  • Recognise the endpoint evidence left when a document reader starts a script.
  • Identify why local-language lures reduce the value of language-based filtering.
  • Summarise the uncertainty around attribution and sponsorship in public reporting.
  • Explain how removable media and shared drives extend an intrusion.
