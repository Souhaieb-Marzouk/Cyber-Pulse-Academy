M1053 Data Backup

M1053Enterprise MitigationVery hardMitigation

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A data backup is a copy of your important information kept somewhere else. It lets you put things back after an accident, a failure or an attack. Good backups are stored so that an attacker cannot find them or delete them. They are also tested, because a copy you cannot restore is not really a backup.

Why should you care?

Ransomware attacks lock or destroy the data you rely on and then demand payment to release it. If your only copy sits on the same network, the attacker simply encrypts or deletes it too. Backups turn a disaster into an inconvenience and remove the pressure to pay. They also cover ordinary problems, such as a failed disk, a mistake by a user, or a bad software release. Without them, a small incident can stop the whole organisation.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Decide what must be protected by listing the systems and data the business truly needs to run.
  2. Copy that data on a schedule to separate storage, keeping several versions over time.
  3. Keep at least one copy offline or immutable, so it cannot be altered or erased remotely.
  4. Give the backup system its own accounts, so a normal user or workstation cannot reach it.
  5. Restore real systems on a test schedule and time how long recovery actually takes.
  6. Watch job reports and storage health, because silent backup failures are the common trap.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital records office that photographs every paper file at the end of each day. The photographs go into a fireproof safe in a different building, and only two named people hold that key. If someone destroys the original files, the day is still recoverable. If the safe key were left on the front desk, the whole plan would fall apart.

Key takeaways

  • Keep at least one backup copy offline or immutable, away from normal network credentials.
  • Test restores on a schedule, since an untested backup often fails when you need it.
  • Protect backup accounts, because attackers look for the recovery system first.

In more detail

Data Backup is the practice of copying important data and system state to storage that an attacker cannot reach or destroy, so recovery does not depend on paying, negotiating or rebuilding by hand. The mechanism matters more than the slogan: backups taken onto a domain joined server with shared credentials are encrypted alongside production during ransomware incidents, because that server is just another target. Modern programmes use the three, two, one pattern of three copies on two media with one held elsewhere, plus immutable or offline repositories, versioned snapshots, and separate credentials for the backup tier that ordinary user accounts cannot use. Evidence includes backup job success and failure reports, restore test records, snapshot retention settings, and the audit trail showing who deleted a recovery point.

What you will practise

  • Explain why backups must be isolated or immutable to survive a ransomware incident.
  • Describe the three, two, one copy pattern and when to apply it.
  • Outline a restore test programme with realistic recovery time measurement.
  • Identify the credentials and trust relationships that put a backup tier at risk.
  • Recognise the evidence that shows whether a backup programme is healthy.
