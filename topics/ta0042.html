TA0042 Resource Development

TA0042EnterpriseExtremeTactic

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Resource development is the preparation an attacker does before the attack starts. They buy or rent servers, register lookalike website names, and gather tools. Nearly all of this happens on systems you do not own. You cannot watch it directly, so you rely on outside clues.

Why should you care?

Most attacks are cheap only because someone prepared in advance. Attackers can rent a server in minutes and copy your brand in a website name that looks almost right. If you spot that preparation, you can warn staff and block the fake site before any message arrives. Ignoring it means the first warning is usually a successful phishing click.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker decides who to target and what kind of access would be useful.
  2. They register website names that look similar to a trusted company's name.
  3. They rent servers or cloud accounts to host fake pages and send email.
  4. They build or buy tools, sometimes turning off security features on their own machines.
  5. Defenders watch for new lookalike names and for mail sent from unknown servers.

Real-world example

Imagine someone planning to burgle a bank. Weeks before, they rent a van, print a fake uniform, and copy the company logo onto a clipboard. None of that is a crime on its own, and none of it happens near the bank. Staff inside see nothing at all. The preparation is visible only through small outside clues, such as a van hire and a printing order. Resource development is that quiet preparation stage.

Key takeaways

  • Resource development is the attacker getting ready, usually out of sight.
  • Watch for website names that imitate yours and for new sending servers.
  • Warn your staff early about fake websites and fake messages.

In more detail

Resource Development is the tactic covering the infrastructure, identities, and tooling an adversary establishes or acquires before and during a campaign, almost all of which sits on systems the victim does not own and therefore cannot monitor directly. Typical behaviour includes registering lookalike or typosquatted domains, obtaining free or stolen cloud and virtual private server capacity, acquiring code-signing certificates through fraudulent registrations, staging payloads on legitimate file-sharing or code-hosting services, purchasing or renting access from initial-access brokers, and compiling or buying tooling that matches the target's defensive stack. From the defender's side the observable surface is indirect and mostly external: newly registered domains that resemble the brand, certificate transparency entries, DNS resolutions pointing at commodity hosting, mail authentication failures from previously unseen sending infrastructure, and abuse reports from third parties. Threat intelligence platforms, domain monitoring, and collaboration with registrars or hosting providers carry much of the detection weight, while internal telemetry usually only shows the consequences of this preparation, such as phishing sent from aged accounts or payloads downloaded from reputable content networks. The tactic is hard to detect in isolation because none of the preparatory actions are inherently malicious, because infrastructure is often recycled and shared between unrelated customers, and because attribution depends on correlating weak signals over time rather than on any single high-fidelity event.

What you will practise

  • Describe the adversary goal behind Resource Development and why most of its activity is invisible to internal monitoring.
  • Enumerate the observable external artifacts of campaign preparation: new domains, certificates, hosting changes, staged payloads, and broker activity.
  • Explain how domain and certificate monitoring can be tuned to a specific brand without generating unusable alert volume.
  • Correlate external infrastructure indicators with inbound email and authentication telemetry to identify a campaign before payload delivery.
  • Evaluate the limits of attribution when adversaries reuse commodity hosting, compromised legitimate sites, and shared tooling.
  • Draft an escalation path that turns an external infrastructure observation into a monitored defensive action.
