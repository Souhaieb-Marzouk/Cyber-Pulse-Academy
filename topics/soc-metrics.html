SOC Metrics

SOC & Detection EngineeringHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

SOC metrics are numbers that describe how well a security operations centre is working. A security operations centre is the team that watches for attacks. The numbers cover how fast the team reacts and how good its alerts are.

Why should you care?

Without numbers, a team cannot tell whether it is improving or just busy. Numbers also help leaders decide where to spend money. However, a single number can mislead you, because people can make it look good without fixing anything. Read several measures together over time.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The team agrees on a small set of questions it wants the numbers to answer.
  2. Each measure is given a clear definition that stays the same over time.
  3. Tools record the raw facts, such as the time an alert arrived and was closed.
  4. The team reviews the measures together each month, never as one number alone.
  5. They look for a cause behind the pattern, not for someone to blame.
  6. Changes are made to rules, staffing or tools, and the measures are watched again.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital dashboard that shows how long patients wait. If the only measure is waiting time, a clever manager can empty the queue quickly. They simply send people home without treatment. The number improves and the patients get worse. Hospitals therefore watch several measures together. They look at waiting time, correct diagnosis and patients who return. A security team needs the same balance. Speed on its own can be faked by closing alerts without reading them.

Key takeaways

  • Choose measures that answer a real question, not just measures you can collect.
  • Read several numbers together, because any single one can be gamed.
  • Never use a team metric as a personal target for one analyst.

In more detail

SOC metrics are the quantitative measures a security operations centre uses to understand whether its detection and response work is effective. Useful measurements fall into a few families: performance measures such as time from event to detection and from detection to containment, quality measures such as escalation accuracy and the proportion of alerts closed as benign, coverage measures such as the fraction of the estate sending logs to the platform and the adversary behaviours the rules claim to detect, and outcome measures such as confirmed incidents and their business impact. The reason this is harder than it looks is that the measures trade against each other. Closing tickets faster is easy if analysts dismiss alerts, and a falling false positive rate is easy if rules are silenced. A single number therefore says very little on its own; the value comes from reading a small set together over time and from keeping their definitions stable. In real environments the data lives in the ticketing system, the detection platform, an asset inventory and the post-incident review. The common mistake is turning a metric into an individual target, which teaches people to optimise the number rather than the security outcome.

What you will practise

  • Explain the purpose of metrics in a security operations centre.
  • Distinguish performance, quality, coverage and outcome measures.
  • Describe why measures such as detection time and false positive rate trade off.
  • Identify the data sources that provide trustworthy metric values.
  • Recognise how a metric used as an individual target distorts behaviour.
