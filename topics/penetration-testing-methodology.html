Penetration Testing Methodology

Offensive SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A penetration testing methodology is the plan a tester follows during an authorised security test. It says what will be tested, how far the tester may go, and how results are recorded. The purpose is to make the test fair, legal and repeatable.

Why should you care?

Testing a system without written permission can be a crime and can break live services. A plan protects both sides. It also makes results trustworthy. Two testers following the same plan should find the same kinds of problems. Without it, a test becomes a random hunt and the report cannot be used for decisions.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Agree the scope in writing, listing the systems in range and the systems that are out of range.
  2. Set the rules of engagement, including testing hours, source addresses and who to call in an emergency.
  3. Gather public information about the target, such as its domains and known technologies.
  4. Look for weaknesses, then exploit them only as far as the agreed rules allow.
  5. Record every step with enough detail that another tester could repeat it.
  6. Report each finding with its impact and a fix, then retest after the fix is applied.

Real-world example

Think of an airport security inspection. The inspectors do not wander onto any runway they like. They have a written authorisation, a schedule, a list of areas, and a contact who can stop the exercise. They walk the route a passenger could take and note each weak point. Afterwards the airport gets a report, fixes the weak points, and invites the inspectors back to confirm the fix worked.

Key takeaways

  • Written authorisation and clear scope come before any technical work.
  • A test proves impact by chaining findings, which a scanner cannot do.
  • A finding is closed only when a retest confirms the fix.

In more detail

A penetration testing methodology is the documented, repeatable process a tester follows so that an assessment is scoped, authorised, evidenced and reproducible. Public frameworks such as the Penetration Testing Execution Standard and NIST SP 800-115 describe the shape of that work, and most providers add their own internal standard. The phases are broadly consistent: scoping and rules of engagement, reconnaissance, threat modelling, vulnerability analysis, exploitation within agreed limits, post exploitation to demonstrate impact, and reporting with retesting. What the methodology actually constrains is not the tools but the boundaries and the record keeping. Rules of engagement fix which systems may be touched, from which source addresses, during which hours, what to do if a real compromise is discovered, and how any captured data is handled. Because a penetration test reasons about chaining weak findings and business impact, it answers questions that a vulnerability scan cannot. The mistake people make is treating the report as the finish line; a finding is only closed when the fix is verified by a retest, and a test performed once says nothing about a system changed since.

What you will practise

  • Describe the phases of a typical penetration testing methodology and what each one produces.
  • Explain the purpose of rules of engagement and list the items they should define.
  • Distinguish penetration testing from automated vulnerability scanning in terms of method and outcome.
  • Explain why testing must be authorised in writing and limited to an agreed scope.
  • Describe how findings should be documented so another tester could reproduce them.
  • Explain why retesting is required before a finding is considered resolved.
