Network Access Control

Network SecurityVery hardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Network access control, or NAC, decides whether a device may join your network and what it may reach once connected. It checks who or what is connecting, then checks the device's health, such as patches and antivirus. Healthy devices get normal access, unhealthy ones go to a repair network, and unknown ones are blocked or registered.

Why should you care?

Anyone who plugs a laptop into a wall socket or joins the wireless network is on your network, unless something checks first. One unpatched or infected machine can then spread trouble to everything else. NAC makes joining a decision with a record, instead of a physical fact. It also tells you which device is on which port, which helps you find and isolate a compromised machine quickly.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. A device connects to a switch port or a wireless network and asks to join.
  2. It proves its identity with a certificate, a password, or, for simple devices, its hardware address.
  3. The system then checks the device's health, such as updates, antivirus and disk encryption.
  4. A device that passes goes into a network zone that matches its role.
  5. A device that fails goes into a quarantine area where it can be fixed but not cause harm.
  6. Devices that cannot authenticate, like printers, get a documented exception and limited access.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital front desk. Every visitor must state who they are and who they are visiting. Staff receive a badge that opens only the doors they need. A visitor who looks unwell is sent to a separate room before entering the wards. A delivery driver without an appointment waits at reception. The building still has corridors, but each door checks a different list.

Key takeaways

  • NAC checks identity and device health before letting a device on the network.
  • Failed checks should quarantine, not simply lock people out of work.
  • Roll it out in monitor mode first so you discover odd devices safely.

In more detail

Network access control is the set of technologies that decides whether a device may join a network and what it may reach once connected. A typical deployment authenticates the device or its user before granting access, commonly through 802.1X with a RADIUS server, MAC authentication bypass for devices that cannot run a supplicant, or a captive portal for guests and unmanaged endpoints. After authentication the platform evaluates posture, covering operating system version, patch level, endpoint protection state, disk encryption and configuration compliance, and then places the device into a role-based segment or access control list. Devices that fail posture checks are quarantined to a remediation network where they can update but cannot reach production systems, and devices that cannot authenticate at all are denied or routed into a registration workflow. The result is that network admission becomes a policy decision with an audit trail rather than a physical fact. Projects fail more often for organisational than technical reasons. Discovery is the first hurdle, because most estates contain printers, cameras, badge readers, medical devices and building systems that predate any authentication requirement and may not support 802.1X, and each needs a documented exception with a compensating control. Rollout must be staged in monitor mode first, where the platform classifies devices and reports what would have happened, before enforcement is enabled, otherwise a misconfiguration locks out staff or takes critical systems offline. Link design also matters, because authentication on switch-to-switch and switch-to-controller uplinks requires careful configuration to avoid authenticating infrastructure. Once operating, network access control supplies valuable telemetry: it knows which device is on which port at which time, which helps incident response locate and isolate a compromised host quickly. Its limitation is that it secures the moment of connection, not the behaviour afterwards, so it complements segmentation and monitoring rather than replacing them.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to explain 802.1X authentication flows and where MAC authentication bypass or portals are needed.
  • You will be able to design posture checks and a quarantine path that does not break remediation.
  • You will be able to plan a monitor-mode rollout that discovers devices before enforcement begins.
  • You will be able to identify infrastructure links and headless devices that require documented exceptions.
  • You will be able to use admission telemetry for rapid host location and isolation during an incident.
  • You will be able to state the limits of admission control and the complementary controls it requires.
