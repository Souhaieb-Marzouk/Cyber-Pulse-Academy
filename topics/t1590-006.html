T1590.006 Gather Victim Network Information: Network Security Appliances

T1590.006ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This sub-technique is about finding the security devices that protect your network. It looks for firewalls, web filters, proxy servers, and intrusion detection systems (IDS). An intrusion detection system is a tool that watches network traffic for attacks. The goal is to learn what defences stand in the way.

Why should you care?

If an attacker knows your defences, they can choose methods that slip past them. A firewall that drops packets silently is much harder to map than one that sends back error messages. Finding a proxy or a bastion host tells the attacker where remote workers connect. That is a useful place to aim.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker sends probes to many ports and watches how your devices answer or stay silent.
  2. Different firewalls reply, reset, or drop traffic in different ways, which hints at the brand.
  3. They read error pages and banners that proxies and filters often leave in responses.
  4. They check public job adverts and supplier pages for the products your teams support.
  5. They test whether a bastion host or remote access gateway is reachable from the internet.
  6. They record which defences exist so they can later choose methods those devices may miss.

Real-world example

Imagine arriving at an airport and wanting to know how strict the security checks are. You watch the queue instead of joining it. You notice one lane where bags pass through a scanner and another where staff open every case by hand. You count how many officers stand at each gate. Nobody stops you, because watching is allowed, and later you choose the lane that looks easiest. Attackers study your security appliances the same way. They probe and observe how your firewalls and filters respond. The devices meant to protect you become the thing being mapped.

Key takeaways

  • Configure firewalls to drop unwanted traffic quietly, so probes cannot tell your rules apart.
  • Keep banners, error pages, and management interfaces from announcing product names and versions.
  • Job adverts and supplier pages often name your security products, so treat them as public information.

In more detail

This sub-technique covers reconnaissance aimed specifically at the defensive network devices guarding a victim: firewalls, content filters, proxies and bastion hosts, network-based intrusion detection systems (NIDS), and related security appliances. Adversaries infer their presence and vendor family from how probes are answered, since filtering devices differ in whether they silently drop packets, return administrative unreachables, or reset connections, and from banners and error pages that leak product identity. Public sources such as support contracts, job adverts, and supplier case studies frequently name the products in use, which is often cheaper than probing. The telemetry is concentrated at the perimeter and in management planes: probe traffic against filtered ports, inconsistent responses across adjacent ports, and access attempts against device interfaces. Legitimate health checks and monitoring agents produce similar traffic, so source stability and the breadth of targeted devices separate routine monitoring from deliberate appliance fingerprinting, and hardening should aim to make responses uniform and uninformative.

What you will practise

  • List the network security appliances that adversaries try to identify during reconnaissance.
  • Explain how differing responses to probes reveal the presence and vendor of a filtering device.
  • Describe how public documents expose which security products an organisation runs.
  • Identify perimeter telemetry that indicates deliberate appliance fingerprinting.
  • Recommend hardening that makes device responses uniform and uninformative.
