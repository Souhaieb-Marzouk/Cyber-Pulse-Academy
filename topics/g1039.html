G1039 RedCurl

G1039Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

RedCurl is a name for a group that steals business documents from companies. Public reports describe attacks in several countries since about 2018. The group is unusual because it uses normal, legal administration tools instead of writing its own harmful software. Researchers describe it as corporate espionage, which means stealing information from one company to help another.

Why should you care?

Most people expect an intruder to bring obvious bad files. This group brings none, so file scanners often see nothing to block. It simply signs in with stolen credentials and uses the same tools your own administrators use every day. Because its goal is quiet copying rather than damage, it can work for a long time without anyone noticing. That makes behaviour monitoring and identity checks your most useful defence.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers study a company and pick employees who handle contracts or finance.
  2. They send a personal email with a file that installs a small hidden program.
  3. The program opens a way back, and the attackers sign in with stolen or created accounts.
  4. They explore the network slowly and learn where valuable documents are kept.
  5. They copy the files, pack them into archives, and remove them through normal services.
  6. They delete their traces and often wait a long time before returning.

Real-world example

Picture a cleaner with a valid staff pass who never breaks anything. He empties the bins, walks every corridor, and photographs documents left on desks. He works the same hours as everyone else and greets people politely. Security cameras show nothing unusual because he is doing normal things in normal places. That is how an intrusion without malware looks. It is why who did what matters more than which file was bad.

Key takeaways

  • An intrusion can happen without any malware, using only stolen accounts and normal tools.
  • Watch administrator tools, large outbound transfers and access outside working hours.
  • Keep records of which documents left the network, because quiet theft rarely triggers an alarm.

In more detail

RedCurl is a threat cluster tracked by several vendors since around 2018 and described in public reporting as conducting corporate espionage for hire, with intrusions concentrated in Russia, Ukraine, Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany and Norway. What distinguishes the cluster is not custom malware but discipline with legitimate tooling: reporting describes the abuse of signed remote administration and remote execution utilities, living-off-the-land binaries such as archive and scripting tools, and careful staging of documents for exfiltration, all with relatively little destructive activity. Initial access is typically reported as targeted spear phishing with an attachment that leads to a loader, after which the operators quietly map the environment, collect business documents such as contracts, financial records and legal material, and remove them in staged archives. Public analyses also note an unusual level of operational security for a financially motivated set, including checks for sandbox and analysis environments and long dormancy between intrusions, and some researchers have argued the targeting and document selection suggest a state or intelligence customer rather than ordinary crime. Attribution of the cluster to any government remains unproven and is stated cautiously in open reporting. Evidence in a defended estate is behavioural: remote management tools installed outside change control, long-lived sessions from unfamiliar address ranges, compression of document shares, and large outbound transfers to file hosting or mail services. The common misunderstanding is that absence of malware means absence of an intrusion.

What you will practise

  • Explain how an intrusion can succeed using only legitimate administration tools and valid accounts.
  • Describe the document collection and staged exfiltration pattern typical of corporate espionage.
  • Identify behavioural indicators such as out-of-hours remote sessions and bulk archive creation.
  • Explain why anti-malware alone provides limited coverage against living-off-the-land activity.
  • Discuss public reporting on possible state interest without asserting unproven attribution.
  • Recommend data-centric controls that limit the value of copied business documents.
