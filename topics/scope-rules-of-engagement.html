Scope and Rules of Engagement

Offensive SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Scope and rules of engagement are the written rules for a security test. They say what may be tested, when, how, and by whom. They also say what to do when something goes wrong. Without written permission from someone allowed to give it, the same testing becomes unauthorised access. This document protects the tester, the client and the defenders.

Why should you care?

Testing without permission is not testing, it is intrusion. Clear scope keeps everyone safe and legal. It also prevents wasted work on systems nobody cares about. When defenders know a test is booked, they do not treat it as a real attack, which saves hours of false alarms. A good document also names emergency contacts and the conditions that mean stop now.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. List every system that is inside the test, using exact names and addresses.
  2. List what is out of bounds, including systems owned by other companies.
  3. State the allowed hours, the time zone, and any speed limits.
  4. Say which techniques are banned, such as service disruption or social tricks.
  5. Agree on emergency contacts, stop conditions, and exactly how findings will be reported.

Real-world example

Imagine hiring builders to fix your kitchen. You would not let them knock down walls in the flat next door. You agree the working hours so the neighbours can sleep. You say clearly that the gas pipe stays untouched. You also swap phone numbers for emergencies. A rules of engagement document is that agreement for a security test. It sets the boundaries before work starts, so nobody is surprised later.

Key takeaways

  • No written permission means the testing is simply not allowed.
  • Name what is out of scope as clearly as what is in scope.
  • Defenders should know about a planned test before it starts.

In more detail

Scope and rules of engagement are the written boundary that defines what may be tested, when, how, by whom, and what happens when something goes wrong; without documented authorisation from someone entitled to give it, the same activity is simply unauthorised access. A workable document names in-scope assets precisely enough to be unambiguous, lists excluded systems and data categories, and states testing windows with time zones. It defines permitted and prohibited techniques, for example whether denial-of-service testing, social engineering, physical entry or third-party hosted services are allowed, and it records the source addresses that defenders may allow-list. It sets escalation rules: emergency contacts on both sides, stop conditions such as discovery of an unrelated active intrusion or unexpected service degradation, and the obligation to report critical findings immediately rather than at the final readout. It also covers handling of sensitive data encountered during testing, evidence retention and destruction, deconfliction with the security operations centre, and the treatment of cloud or outsourced services where the provider may require separate notification under a shared responsibility model. Practically, the document protects the tester, the client and the defenders: it reduces false positives because the security operations centre knows when legitimate testing is scheduled, gives a tested route for stopping work, and provides the audit trail that regulators and insurers request. Scope should be reviewed whenever the environment changes materially.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to draft a scope section that names in-scope and excluded assets unambiguously.
  • You will be able to list the technique categories that must be explicitly permitted or prohibited in an engagement.
  • You will be able to define escalation paths, stop conditions and emergency contacts for a test.
  • You will be able to explain how deconfliction with a security operations centre reduces false positives.
  • You will be able to describe the authorisation needed for cloud and third-party hosted services.
  • You will be able to specify evidence handling, retention and data destruction requirements.
