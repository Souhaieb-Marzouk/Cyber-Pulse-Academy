G1034 Daggerfly

G1034Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Daggerfly is a name that researchers use for a group of attackers who break into networks to steal information. Other companies call the same group Evasive Panda or BRONZE HIGHLAND. Public reports link this group to long campaigns against telephone companies and government offices, mostly in Africa and Asia. It is not a program you download. It is a label for attacks that share tools and habits.

Why should you care?

This group is patient. It stays quiet inside a network for a long time and copies documents, email and login details. Because the attackers use normal business services for their hidden communications, simple blocklists often miss them. If you ignore unusual logins and quiet outbound traffic, the theft can continue for months. Knowing the pattern helps you spot small clues early, while you can still remove the intruder.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers send a message that looks like normal business email to someone in the target company.
  2. That person opens the attached or linked file, and small hidden software starts on the computer.
  3. The software contacts a normal cloud or web service, so the traffic looks harmless to a basic filter.
  4. The attackers use stolen passwords to read mail and documents, then set up a way to return later.
  5. They move slowly to other computers and collect the files that look valuable to them.
  6. Defenders find the trail in login records, new scheduled tasks and strange outbound connections.

Real-world example

Think of a large office building where the visitor desk only checks the name on a badge. An intruder prints a badge with a normal company name and walks in during the busy morning. He then sits in an empty meeting room for weeks. He breaks no locks. He copies papers, photographs whiteboards, and uses the staff kitchen like everyone else. The only clues are small. A badge is used at strange hours. A meeting room is booked for a person nobody recognises. That is how a quiet intrusion works, and why small records matter more than a dramatic alarm.

Key takeaways

  • A group name describes shared behaviour, not one virus you can simply delete.
  • Quiet intruders hide in normal services, so watch logins and outbound traffic, not only known bad files.
  • Report strange mailbox access and new scheduled tasks early, before company data leaves.

In more detail

Daggerfly is the tracking name used for a long-running intrusion set that public reporting attributes to China-nexus espionage activity, with vendor aliases including Evasive Panda and BRONZE HIGHLAND. The cluster is best known for targeting telecommunications operators, government bodies and, in later reporting, organisations in Africa and Asia, where operators combined custom and commodity malware rather than relying on a single signature tool. Reported tooling includes the MgBot family used for collection and remote control, loaders that abuse legitimate or signed executables so activity blends in, and browser credential theft used to re-enter mailboxes and web consoles. A distinguishing behaviour described in public analyses is the use of network redirectors and cloud services for command and control, which keeps malware callbacks pointing at trusted hosting instead of hostile-looking infrastructure. Evidence in a real environment is therefore rarely a loud alert: analysts see unusual outbound sessions to cloud endpoints, scheduled tasks or services created shortly after a phishing email, and mailbox access from new locations. The common misunderstanding is treating this as one malware family to block, when the durable detection surface is identity, persistence and outbound network behaviour. Attribution remains an assessment drawn from overlapping reporting and cluster boundaries have shifted as vendors merged and split names.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a threat group tracking name represents and why vendors use different names for the same cluster.
  • Describe the typical intrusion path from a business email lure to mailbox access and document theft.
  • Identify the log sources that reveal quiet espionage activity, such as authentication records and service creation events.
  • Distinguish between malware-centric detection and identity-centric detection in long-running intrusions.
  • Summarise public reporting on this cluster without overstating confidence in the attribution.
  • Recommend containment priorities when a low-noise intruder is suspected inside a network.
