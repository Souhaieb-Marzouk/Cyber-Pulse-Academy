Purdue Model

OT & ICS SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

The Purdue Model is a way to draw a factory or power plant network as a set of levels. The lowest levels are the machines and sensors. Higher levels are the computers that manage the plant and then the normal office network. Each level should talk only to the level next to it.

Why should you care?

Plant machines can cause real physical harm if they are changed. Office networks are full of everyday risks, such as email and web browsing. If the two are joined together, an office problem can reach a machine that controls valves or motors. Careful layers keep the dangerous side away from the risky side.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Group the devices in the plant by what they do, from sensors up to plant management.
  2. Write down which systems genuinely need to talk to each other across each group.
  3. Place a firewall on each boundary so only the agreed traffic passes through.
  4. Put a small brokered zone between the plant and the office network for shared data.
  5. Send plant data upward through that zone instead of allowing direct connections.
  6. Review the design when remote access or cloud services are added, as these weaken the layers.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital with an intensive care ward and an office block. Staff pass between them through one controlled door with a badge reader. The office gets summary reports, not live control of the ventilators. If someone in the office has a cold, the ward is not affected. If the ward needs something, it asks through the controlled door.

Key takeaways

  • Think in zones and boundaries, not in one flat plant network.
  • Shared data should pass through a brokered zone, never a direct link.
  • Remote access and cloud services need the same boundary checks as physical links.

In more detail

The Purdue Model, more precisely the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture, is a hierarchical reference model for industrial control networks that is widely reused in operational technology security as a way to describe zones and levels. The levels are usually numbered zero to five: level zero is the physical process itself, such as sensors and actuators; level one is the basic controllers and safety systems that read and drive them; level two is supervisory control, covering human machine interfaces and engineering workstations; level three is plant wide operations, including historians and scheduling; level three point five is the industrial demilitarised zone that brokers all traffic between plant and enterprise; level four is enterprise information technology; and level five is the wider external network. Its security value is that it constrains connectivity: each level boundary is meant to be a real control point, so an infection on a business network cannot reach a controller directly, and data leaving the plant passes through a brokered zone rather than a direct route. Treating it as a product architecture or a standard is the common mistake. It is a reference model, real plants flatten or extend it, and remote access, cloud analytics and wireless instruments blur it further, so the defensible principle is segmentation with enforced conduits rather than a diagram that matches the drawing.

What you will practise

  • Describe the levels of the Purdue Model and what kind of system sits at each one.
  • Explain why segmentation between plant and enterprise networks reduces risk.
  • Describe the purpose of an industrial demilitarised zone between operational and business systems.
  • Explain why the model is a reference architecture rather than a standard or a product.
  • Identify modern practices, such as remote access and cloud analytics, that blur the defined levels.
  • Recognise the danger of a flat network in which business systems can reach controllers.
