G1036 Moonstone Sleet

G1036Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Moonstone Sleet is the name used in public reports for a group of attackers linked to North Korea. The same activity is also tracked as Storm-1789. This group does not only steal, it also tries to earn money. It builds fake companies and fake job offers so that people trust it before any attack begins.

Why should you care?

A fake job offer or a fake business partner is hard to spot, because nothing technical looks wrong. The attacker spends weeks talking to the target and behaves like a normal recruiter. When trust exists, one file or one meeting link is enough. This means your hiring checks and your supplier checks protect you just as much as your antivirus. It also means your staff need to know that a friendly recruiter can be an intruder.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers create a fake company and a professional looking website for it.
  2. They contact people in technology and finance firms with job offers or partnership ideas.
  3. They chat for a while, so the target feels comfortable with them.
  4. They send a project file, a test task or a game that carries hidden software.
  5. That software runs on the work computer and gives the attackers a quiet way in.
  6. They then look for money, secrets and other accounts they can reach from there.

Real-world example

Imagine a stranger who opens a small shop next door to your office. They smile, remember your name, and offer you a discount card. After a month they ask to borrow your office keys for a delivery. Nothing they did was technical or frightening. They simply built trust first. That is how this group works. The attack starts with a friendly relationship, and the technical step at the end is small.

Key takeaways

  • Verify who you are dealing with before you open files or share access, even if the person is friendly.
  • Treat unsolicited job offers and partnership requests as a security event, not only a career opportunity.
  • Separate work and personal devices, and never test unknown code on a company machine.

In more detail

Moonstone Sleet is a Microsoft-assigned designation for a North Korean-attributed cluster that blends traditional espionage with revenue generation, and it is tracked elsewhere as Storm-1789. Public reporting describes the group creating fake companies, fake job advertisements and fabricated software or game projects to build rapport with targets in technology and cryptocurrency sectors, then delivering custom loaders and backdoors through those relationships rather than through mass phishing alone. Analysts have also described the group operating its own infrastructure, including interactive services and a bespoke game used as a delivery lure, which distinguishes it from clusters that only rent commodity tooling. The cluster is assessed as related to but distinct from other North Korean sets, and vendors have warned that it overlaps in behaviour with information technology worker schemes, where individuals obtain remote employment under false identities and then use that access for intrusion. Evidence in a defended environment is mostly identity and endpoint behavioural: recruitment-themed messages arriving at personal and corporate accounts, development tools or game executables launching from user directories, unusual remote-work sessions, and cloud or code repository access that does not match an employee's role. The common misunderstanding is that such campaigns are only a phishing problem, when the harder signal is a fake employee or supplier relationship that grants legitimate-looking access. Attribution rests on vendor assessment and is reported with moderate confidence.

What you will practise

  • Explain how fabricated companies and recruitment lures create access without exploiting a technical flaw.
  • Describe the difference between revenue-driven and pure espionage activity within one attributed cluster.
  • Identify endpoint and identity signals that reveal a fake employee or fake supplier relationship.
  • Explain why vendor-assigned cluster names differ and how to compare behaviour across them.
  • Recommend verification steps for unsolicited job offers, code tests and partnership requests.
  • Describe how cloud and code repository access patterns help detect misuse of legitimate accounts.
