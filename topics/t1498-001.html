T1498.001 Network Denial of Service: Direct Network Flood

T1498.001ImpactExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A direct network flood is a denial of service attack. Attackers send a huge amount of data straight at a target. One computer can do it, but many computers together are far more effective. There is no clever trick, only overwhelming volume.

Why should you care?

This is the easiest attack to launch, so almost any organisation can be a target. Because many machines send small amounts each, the traffic looks similar to real visitors, which makes filtering hard. Your internet provider may also slow down or block traffic to protect its own network. Being ready matters more than being clever.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gathers many controlled machines so the flood comes from many places at once.
  2. Each machine sends a stream of small packets toward the target address.
  3. Simple protocols like UDP are common because they need no setup at all.
  4. For TCP targets, many connection requests may be sent and left unfinished.
  5. The combined volume exceeds the target connection, so real traffic is dropped.
  6. Because every machine sends only a little, no single source looks unusual by itself.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital night desk with one receptionist. A crowd of people walks in and out of the entrance asking nothing and going nowhere, over and over. The receptionist cannot help the real patient waiting behind them. No single person in the crowd behaved strangely. Together they made the desk unusable, which is how a direct flood works.

Key takeaways

  • This sub-technique floods directly, with no reflector in the middle.
  • Botnets spread the traffic so it resembles real demand.
  • Capacity planning and provider side scrubbing are the main defences.

In more detail

A direct network flood is the simplest shape of denial of service: one or more systems send high volumes of packets straight at the victim's network, with no reflector or third party in the path. Almost any protocol can carry the flood, and the choice matters for the attacker because stateless protocols such as UDP and ICMP need no handshake, while stateful protocols such as TCP can be driven with floods of synchronise or acknowledgement packets that also consume connection state on hosts and filtering devices. Volume is usually supplied by a botnet so that each participant contributes a small amount, which is the practical reason this sub-technique is so hard to separate from genuine demand: at sufficient scale, every individual source looks like an ordinary client. Telemetry lives in flow records and edge counters. The distinguishing pattern is a sharp, uncorrelated rise in packets and bytes toward one address or service, high packet rates with short average packet size, a small number of source networks contributing most of the volume, and flows that never complete a session or request a response. Legitimate traffic spikes track a known event and finish their transactions, while flood traffic keeps arriving without any matching application activity. Mitigation is mostly upstream: capacity, scrubbing, and rate limiting performed by the provider on the organisation's behalf.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a direct network flood differs from reflection and amplification.
  • Describe why botnets make direct floods hard to distinguish from real traffic.
  • Explain how stateless and stateful protocols change the flooding approach.
  • Identify flow and packet level signals of a direct flood in progress.
  • Describe the role of upstream providers in absorbing flood traffic.
  • Explain what a sustained outage costs the targeted organisation.
