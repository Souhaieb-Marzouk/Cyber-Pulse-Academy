T1195.003 Supply Chain Compromise: Compromise Hardware Supply Chain

T1195.003Initial AccessExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This means an attacker changes a device before it reaches you. They might add a hidden chip or alter the software built into the device. The device then works normally, but it can also be controlled by the attacker. This is different from tricking software you download, because the problem is inside the physical equipment.

Why should you care?

You cannot fix this by reinstalling the operating system, because the change sits below it. It can survive disk wipes and factory resets. The device may pass normal security checks, because it behaves exactly as expected. The attacker may hold access for years and you may never see the first break in. Trusting your equipment without any checks leaves a gap you cannot close later.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Someone with access to the product before delivery changes a component or the firmware inside it.
  2. The change hides a small function, such as a hidden login, a beacon or a way in for the attacker.
  3. The device is packed and shipped normally, so the buyer sees an ordinary sealed box.
  4. The device starts up and works as advertised, so routine checks find nothing unusual.
  5. The hidden function reports out or waits for a signal from the attacker.
  6. After the attacker is inside, the tampering may be removed or left in place in case it is needed again.

Real-world example

Think of a door lock you order from a shop. Before it reaches you, a locksmith adds a second keyhole on the inside, where nobody looks. The lock still opens with your key and passes every test at the front door. Weeks later, someone lets themselves in with the hidden key, and the door shows no sign of force. The only clue would come from checking the lock against the maker's own design.

Key takeaways

  • A hardware backdoor can survive reinstalling the operating system, so cleaning the disk is not enough.
  • Compare firmware versions and boot measurements against the vendor values you recorded.
  • Buy critical equipment from known sellers and keep bills, serial numbers and packing records.

In more detail

Hardware supply chain compromise means an adversary alters a device, or the firmware that runs on it, before the buyer ever takes delivery. The tampering is performed at a manufacturer, an integrator, a reseller, a warehouse or a courier, so the product arrives with its packaging intact and its documentation correct. Because the implant lives below the operating system, in boot firmware, a baseboard management controller, a network interface or an added component, reinstalling the operating system does not remove it and ordinary endpoint agents have nothing to inspect. Defenders rarely see the modification itself. They see its consequences: a device that beacons to an unexpected destination, firmware versions that do not match the vendor baseline, a management controller answering on a network segment where it should be silent, or boot measurements that differ from the recorded good values. The parent technique covers any vendor or supplier stage; this sub-technique is specifically the physical component or firmware layer.

What you will practise

  • Explain how hardware or firmware can be modified before delivery to create a backdoor.
  • Describe why reinstalling an operating system does not remove this kind of implant.
  • Distinguish this sub-technique from other supply chain stages in the parent technique.
  • Identify telemetry such as firmware versions, boot measurements and unexpected outbound beacons.
  • Describe verification steps such as checking serial numbers, seals and vendor firmware baselines.
