PICERL

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

PICERL is a six-step map for handling a security incident. The letters stand for Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, and Lessons Learned. It gives a team one shared language, from the first alert to the final review. The steps guide decisions, but you can move back and forth between them.

Why should you care?

During a real incident, pressure makes people skip steps. They wipe a machine before saving evidence, or they restore service before the attacker is gone. A shared lifecycle keeps the team calm and ordered. Everyone knows what happens next, who decides, and what must be documented before moving on.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Preparation: get ready before anything happens, with logs, contacts, tools, and clear authority.
  2. Identification: work out what actually happened, which systems are involved, and how sure you are.
  3. Containment: stop the spread quickly by isolating machines and cutting off accounts.
  4. Eradication: remove the attacker's software, hidden access, and created accounts for good.
  5. Recovery: bring services back carefully while watching closely for signs of return.
  6. Lessons Learned: review what worked, fix gaps, and give each fix an owner and a date.

Real-world example

Think of how a hospital handles a patient. First the staff prepare the room. Then they identify the problem with tests. They stabilize the patient before treating the cause, then remove it, then help the patient recover, and finally review the case. An incident follows the same rhythm. Skipping the review means the same problem catches you again.

Key takeaways

  • PICERL gives six phases: Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned.
  • Save evidence before cleanup, and do not confuse restored service with safety.
  • The phases repeat; going back a step is normal, not a failure.

In more detail

PICERL is a six-phase incident response lifecycle - Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery and Lessons Learned - that gives a security team a shared vocabulary and a gated decision path from the moment an alert fires to the moment an incident is formally closed. Preparation covers work done before anything happens: asset inventories, logging coverage, jump bags, out-of-band communication, standing authority to isolate systems, and rehearsed escalation paths. Identification is the analysis phase, where telemetry from endpoints, identity providers, network sensors and SaaS audit logs is correlated into a defensible statement of what is actually happening, which hosts and accounts are involved, and how confident the team is in that conclusion. Containment splits into short-term measures that stop immediate spread, such as network isolation or credential revocation, and longer-term measures that keep the business running while the adversary is still presumed present. Eradication removes the adversary's foothold - malware, persistence, created accounts, modified configuration - and is where teams most often declare victory too early. Recovery restores service under heightened monitoring, validates that the root cause is genuinely closed, and defines what normal looks like again. Lessons Learned converts the incident into durable change: detection gaps closed, playbooks updated, owners named, and remediation tracked to completion. The phases are not strictly linear. A persistence mechanism discovered during Recovery sends the team back to Containment, and that iteration is expected rather than a sign of failure. What matters is that each transition is justified by evidence, documented with timestamps, and communicated to the people who own the resulting risk decisions.

What you will practise

  • Explain each PICERL phase and the evidence-based entry and exit criteria that justify moving to the next one.
  • Build a Preparation checklist covering logging coverage, standing authority to isolate, out-of-band communications and evidence handling.
  • Write an Identification statement that separates confirmed fact from inference and states an explicit confidence level.
  • Choose between short-term and long-term containment actions and defend the tradeoff between spread reduction and business impact.
  • Distinguish eradication from recovery and explain why restored service is not proof that the adversary is gone.
  • Design a Lessons Learned process that converts findings into owned, dated remediation items.
  • Reconstruct a PICERL timeline from multi-source logs and identify the points where the team iterated backwards between phases.
