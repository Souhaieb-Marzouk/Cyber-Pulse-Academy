G1028 APT-C-23

G1028Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

APT-C-23 is a label that security researchers use for a group that spies on people in the Middle East. APT means advanced persistent threat, a patient attacker who stays hidden. The group is known for fake phone applications that look like chat or dating programs. When a victim installs one, the app can read messages and track location.

Why should you care?

Phones carry our most private life, and they are harder to protect than office laptops. A fake app can copy messages, photos, and microphone sound without any warning. This group shows that the first step is often a friendly conversation, not a technical trick. If your team only watches servers, you will miss attacks that begin on a personal phone.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers choose a target and begin a friendly chat on a social platform.
  2. Over time they suggest installing an app, often one for dating or for messaging.
  3. The app is not in the official store, so it avoids the normal safety checks.
  4. Once installed, it asks for permissions such as contacts, files, camera, and location.
  5. The app then sends messages, photos, and even sound recordings back to the attackers.
  6. On computers, similar lures arrive as documents or as compressed archive files.

Real-world example

Think of a stranger who joins a local club and becomes everyone's friend. He lends books and remembers birthdays. One day he offers a free phone app that helps the club organise its events. Members install it because they trust him. The app quietly copies their messages and shares their location. Nobody checks the app, because the offer came from a friend, and the club never had a rule about installing software.

Key takeaways

  • Install phone apps only from official stores, and check who made them.
  • Treat sudden friendly messages that lead to an app as a warning sign.
  • Review app permissions, and remove tools that ask for more than they need.

In more detail

APT-C-23 is a tracking label for a Middle East-focused espionage cluster that public reporting links to Hamas-aligned interests, with activity reported against Palestinian, Israeli, and neighbouring targets over many years. The group is known for social engineering rather than advanced exploitation: operators build fake profiles on social networks and messaging applications, pose as a peer or a potential romantic interest, and spend weeks building trust before sending a file or a link. Researchers describe custom backdoors for Android and Windows that arrive as fake chat or messaging applications, requesting access to contacts, messages, location, camera, and microphone on phones, and to documents on desktops. Vendor names overlap heavily and some reporting describes affiliate arrangements, so deciding which intrusion belongs to which faction is uncertain and should be treated as contested. Evidence appears in application installation records, unusual permissions granted to software installed outside official stores, and messaging metadata rather than in loud network behaviour.
