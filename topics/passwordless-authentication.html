Passwordless Authentication

Identity & AccessHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Passwordless authentication means signing in without typing a password. Instead you use something you already have. That may be your phone, a small security key or a code sent to you. The aim is to remove the secret that people reuse and share. Some methods are much stronger than others, so the details matter a lot.

Why should you care?

Passwords are the most common way attackers get into accounts. People reuse them, and they can be tricked into typing them on a fake page. When there is no password to type, a fake login page has nothing useful to collect. Removing passwords also reduces the number of reset requests that support teams handle. The real risk is a careless design. If you can still fall back to a password, you have only moved the problem. The same is true when an emailed code can be intercepted. Good recovery rules are essential.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You sign up once and link a device, such as your phone or a security key.
  2. The service stores a public key or a phone number instead of a password.
  3. At sign in, the service asks your device to prove it is really you.
  4. You approve the request with a fingerprint, a face check or a device PIN.
  5. The device answers in a way that only works for that one website.
  6. If you lose the device, a special recovery process proves your identity again.

Real-world example

Picture an office with a badge instead of a spoken password. You hold the badge to the reader and the door opens. Nobody can open it by simply saying the right phrase. A photocopy of someone's badge will not work at the reader. If you lose the badge, reception checks your identity before issuing a new one. A careless replacement would defeat the whole system.

Key takeaways

  • Passwordless removes the typed shared secret, but other secrets and devices still need protection.
  • Methods differ in strength; security keys resist phishing far better than codes sent by text.
  • Recovery and any password fallback decide the real security of a passwordless system.

In more detail

Passwordless authentication means a user proves their identity without supplying a shared secret that they know and type, so the primary factor is something they hold or are, such as a hardware key, a platform authenticator, a certificate, or a short-lived code delivered to a registered device. The label describes the removal of the password from the primary authentication step, not the absence of every secret: a one-time code is still a secret, and a private key is still something that must be protected, so the honest distinction is between secrets that are memorised, reused across services and phishable, and credentials that are device bound and origin scoped. Implementations range from links or codes sent by email and text, through push approval applications, to cryptographic methods built on the Web Authentication specification, and they differ enormously in phishing resistance and in how easy they are to enrol and recover. It is adopted to cut credential theft, reduce help desk volume and satisfy strong authentication expectations in frameworks such as those published by NIST, though it shifts risk rather than removing it. The usual mistakes are keeping a password fallback path wide open, treating text message codes as equivalent to a hardware authenticator, and designing recovery flows that let an attacker reset their way in.

What you will practise

  • Define passwordless authentication and distinguish it from multi-factor authentication.
  • Compare common methods such as security keys, push approval, and codes sent to a device.
  • Explain why removing a typed shared secret reduces phishing and credential reuse risk.
  • Describe the recovery and enrolment design choices that determine real security.
  • Explain why password fallback paths and weak recovery can cancel the benefits of passwordless login.
