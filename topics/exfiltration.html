Exfiltration

Offensive SecurityExtremeKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Exfiltration is the act of copying data out of a network without permission. The data leaves a place the organisation controls and arrives somewhere it does not. When this happens, a security incident usually becomes a reportable data breach. It is often the final and most damaging step of an attack.

Why should you care?

Your data is the reason attackers break in. If they can copy it out, the harm continues even after you remove them. Permissive outbound rules make this easy, because almost any website is reachable. If you allow all web traffic, an intruder can hide a data theft inside normal browsing. Watching what leaves your network matters as much as watching what enters it.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The intruder first finds and collects the files that look valuable to them.
  2. They copy that collection onto one middle system they already control.
  3. They shrink the files, often by compressing or encrypting them into one package.
  4. They split the package into smaller pieces to avoid sudden large transfers.
  5. They send the pieces out over a channel that is likely to be allowed.

Real-world example

Picture someone moving furniture out of an office at night. They cannot carry a sofa through the front door without being noticed. So they take it apart, wrap the pieces in ordinary boxes, and carry one box out with the normal rubbish each evening. Nothing looks strange on any single day. Weeks later, the office is empty. Data theft often works the same way: small, regular, boring transfers that no one questions.

Key takeaways

  • Watch outbound traffic as carefully as you watch inbound traffic.
  • Large or oddly timed uploads from your network deserve a closer look.
  • Limit what each account can read, and limit where data can go.

In more detail

Exfiltration is the unauthorised transfer of data from an environment the organisation controls to a destination it does not, and it is usually the point at which an intrusion becomes a reportable breach. The activity normally follows a recognisable sequence: locating and collecting data of value, staging it on an intermediate system, reducing its size or obscuring its content through compression and encryption, splitting it into transferable chunks, and moving it over a channel that is likely to be permitted. Channel choice is a trade-off between volume and stealth. Large transfers over ordinary web or file-sharing protocols are fast but visible; low-and-slow transfers over DNS, commodity cloud storage or a long-lived command-and-control channel are quieter but take longer and depend on the destination remaining available. Defences fail most often at the egress boundary, where broad allow-lists for web traffic, personal cloud storage and remote access tools make almost any destination reachable. Detection combines data-side and network-side evidence: unusually large or oddly timed transfers, access to repositories the account does not normally touch, new external destinations, protocol use that does not match the port, and anomalous DNS query volumes or label lengths. Mitigations that work are egress filtering with explicit allow-lists, data classification with controls that follow the data, scoped credentials that limit what a single identity can read, and monitoring of cloud storage and collaboration platforms rather than only the corporate perimeter.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to describe the collect, stage, compress and transfer sequence and the evidence each step creates.
  • You will be able to compare common exfiltration channels by volume, stealth and detectability.
  • You will be able to evaluate egress controls and explain why permissive allow-lists undermine them.
  • You will be able to use flow, DNS and cloud audit telemetry to build exfiltration detections.
  • You will be able to prioritise data classification and credential scoping as exfiltration mitigations.
