G1012 CURIUM

G1012Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

CURIUM is the name for a group that public reports link to Iranian state interests. It is also tracked under several other names, which is normal in this field. Reports say it has operated since about 2017 and targets defence, aerospace, telecom, and shipping organisations. The group is patient and prefers to stay hidden for a long time.

Why should you care?

This group does not rush. It may spend months building a relationship before it ever sends malware. It also uses the term advanced persistent threat. That means a well funded team that stays inside a network for a long time. Because the first contact looks like a genuine professional conversation, technical filters may never raise a flag. The real risk is quiet, long term theft of research and plans. That loss is hard to notice and expensive to replace.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers create a believable persona, often a recruiter or a researcher in the same field.
  2. They contact employees on social networks and discuss work, pay, and career plans.
  3. Over weeks they build trust and ask for a resume or a sample of the target's work.
  4. They send a file that installs a backdoor, which is a tool for remote control.
  5. They set the backdoor to restart automatically, often using a scheduled task.
  6. They collect documents and messages slowly, keeping traffic small and hard to spot.

Real-world example

Imagine a hotel where a guest checks in, pays for a month, and never causes trouble. He nods to the staff, eats breakfast, and reads the newspaper. Nobody watches him because nothing looks wrong. Meanwhile he photographs the guest list whenever the desk is unattended. The patient guest is the attacker. The lobby is your network. The guest list is the research you spent years producing.

Key takeaways

  • Long, friendly professional conversations can be part of an intrusion, so check who is really writing.
  • Review scheduled tasks and startup entries on key machines, since they are common persistence points.
  • Treat research, proposals, and design documents as high value targets worth protecting by default.

In more detail

CURIUM, tracked elsewhere as Crimson Sandstorm, TA456, Tortoise Shell, and Yellow Liderc, is an intrusion set that public reporting associates with Iranian state interests. It has been active since roughly 2017 and has targeted organisations in the Middle East, Europe, and the United States, with defence, aerospace, telecommunications, and maritime logistics appearing in published research. The group leans on patient social engineering: fake recruiting personas on social networks and messaging platforms, long email conversations, and lures about career opportunities or industry news. Delivered malware is usually a backdoor that gives interactive control, and later stages have included keyloggers and information gathering tools receiving commands through web services and public platforms. Persistence has been reported through scheduled tasks and registry entries, and victims are often left in place quietly for long periods rather than monetised immediately. Attribution in public work rests on infrastructure overlap and targeting patterns, which supports a moderate confidence assessment rather than proof.

What you will practise

  • Describe how a fake recruiting persona is used to build trust before any malware is sent.
  • Explain what persistence means and why scheduled tasks and startup entries matter to defenders.
  • Define advanced persistent threat in plain language and explain the practical implication for detection.
  • Explain why public attribution of this group is stated with moderate confidence and what evidence supports it.
  • List the evidence sources that would help you spot long term quiet access on a workstation.
  • Write a short hiring and social media policy point that reduces this social engineering risk.
