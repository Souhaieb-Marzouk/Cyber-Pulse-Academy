T1491 Defacement

T1491ImpactExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Defacement means changing the content that people see so it says something different. On a public website that often means replacing the home page with a message or a picture. Inside a company it can mean changing an internal page, a login message, or the background picture on a computer. The aim is to send a message or cause embarrassment.

Why should you care?

People trust what they see on your systems. If your website shows an attacker message, customers, partners and the public may lose confidence in you. Inside the company, a changed screen reminds staff that the network is not safe, which harms morale and trust. Defacement is often loud and deliberate, because the attacker wants attention. It can also be a decoy that draws attention away from quieter theft happening at the same time.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gets some way to change content, such as a stolen content management account or a web weakness.
  2. They locate the file, page or setting that controls what users see.
  3. They replace the content with their own message, text, or image file.
  4. They may redirect visitors so that a completely different page is served.
  5. They publish proof of the change, often on social media or a paste site, to claim credit.
  6. The change stays until staff notice and restore the original content from a clean copy.

Real-world example

Picture a corner shop with a chalkboard outside showing today's prices. Someone rubs out the prices at night and writes a rude message instead. The shop still works, the till still opens, and nothing was stolen. But every customer who walks past reads the new message and wonders who is in charge. The damage is to trust, and it lasts longer than the chalk.

Key takeaways

  • Defacement damages reputation even when no data is stolen, so treat it as a real incident.
  • Monitor the integrity of public content and internal banners so you notice changes quickly.
  • Keep a clean copy of your site so you can restore the real content fast.

In more detail

Defacement is the unauthorised alteration of content that people see, used to deliver a message, intimidate an audience, or claim credit for an intrusion. The change itself is usually small, such as replacing a home page, a login banner or a desktop background, but the purpose is psychological and reputational rather than purely technical. The mechanism is ordinary content modification: editing a file in a web root, publishing through a content management system, changing a wallpaper setting in the registry, or redirecting traffic so a different page is served. Because the change is made through legitimate publishing channels, the useful artefacts are content integrity records, web server requests preceding the edit, content management audit logs and file change monitoring on the affected path. Read only file systems, integrity monitoring and signed deployments are the controls that make the same change noisy or impossible.

What you will practise

  • Define defacement and explain the goals an attacker has when using it.
  • Describe how content is changed through both file access and publishing systems.
  • Identify the records that show who changed public facing content and when.
  • Explain why defacement can act as a distraction from other malicious activity.
  • Distinguish internal from external defacement and the audience each targets.
  • Describe controls such as integrity monitoring that detect or prevent content changes.
