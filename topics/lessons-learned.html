Lessons Learned Review

Incident Response & ForensicsHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A lessons learned review is a meeting held after a security incident is over. The team looks at what happened, what went well and what went badly. The word lessons learned means the useful knowledge taken from an event. The aim is to change something so the same problem does not return. It is not a trial and nobody is on the stand.

Why should you care?

Without a review, you fix the immediate damage and forget the cause. The same weakness then returns, often during a busier week when nobody has time. Without a written record, new staff repeat old mistakes. A calm review also shows that reporting a problem is safe and useful. If people expect blame, they hide mistakes and you learn nothing at all.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Wait until the incident is contained and evidence is collected, so the review does not disturb recovery.
  2. A facilitator invites everyone involved, including people from different teams and shifts.
  3. The group builds one shared timeline of events, using logs and notes to settle disagreements.
  4. Participants ask what went well, what went badly and what made recovery slow.
  5. The group looks for underlying causes, such as a missing alert or an unclear runbook.
  6. Each cause becomes one specific change with a named owner and a due date, then gets checked later.

Real-world example

Picture a shared flat after a small kitchen fire. Everyone is safe, the pan is outside and the window is open. Then the housemates sit down together. Nobody shouts at the person who burnt the toast, because next time that person might hide the smoke. They ask whether the alarm was loud enough, whether the fire blanket was easy to reach, and whether anyone knew which key opened the back door. They agree to move the blanket next to the cooker and to test the alarm monthly. One housemate writes it on the fridge and checks it is done by Sunday.

Key takeaways

  • Review to learn, not to blame, because blame stops people sharing what happened.
  • Every finding needs an owner and a due date, or the incident will repeat.
  • Write the lessons down clearly so other teams can use them without repeating the work.

In more detail

A lessons learned review is the structured debrief a team holds after an incident has been contained, in which participants rebuild the timeline, agree on contributing causes and commit to specific changes. It is deliberately blameless: the goal is to find weaknesses in systems, tooling, documentation and decision making, because a culture that punishes individuals causes people to withhold detail and produces a report that no longer matches what happened. As a discipline it differs from root cause analysis in emphasis, since the deliverable is a small set of owned, dated improvement actions rather than one satisfying explanation. Good reviews separate what happened from what we knew at the time, cover detection and response as well as prevention, and record what went well so that useful improvisation becomes standard practice. In incident response frameworks such as NIST SP 800-61, this activity sits in the post incident phase and feeds back into preparation, which is why it is treated as a control rather than a courtesy. The failure mode seen most often is a well written report whose recommendations are never tracked or verified, so the same incident recurs a year later. The second is a review that only blames a person, which stops the investigation exactly where the interesting system questions begin.

What you will practise

  • Explain the purpose of a lessons learned review and where it fits in the incident response lifecycle.
  • Describe why a blameless approach produces a more accurate and useful record.
  • Outline the steps of a review, from building a timeline to assigning owned actions.
  • Distinguish a lessons learned review from root cause analysis in terms of its deliverables.
  • Recognise common failure modes, including untracked recommendations and blame focused meetings.
