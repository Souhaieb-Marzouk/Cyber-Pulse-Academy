G0076 Thrip

G0076Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Thrip is a name used for a spying group first described publicly in 2018. Public reports suggest it works for a state, although the exact sponsor is not confirmed. It attacks satellite, telephone and defence suppliers. It then uses the access it gains there to reach those suppliers own customers.

Why should you care?

Many companies share network access with partners and service providers. That shared trust is convenient and it is exactly what this group looks for. A small supplier with weak security can become an open path into a much larger organisation. Defenders often inspect their own network only and forget that partners hold the same keys.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker identifies smaller providers whose customers are the real intelligence targets.
  2. A quiet foothold is taken in the provider, often with stolen credentials or remote tools.
  3. Legitimate administration software is used so that activity looks like normal support work.
  4. The attacker reads documents about the provider networks and maps the customer links.
  5. Those trusted connections are used to sign in to customer and partner systems.
  6. Documents and credentials are collected slowly so that no alarm is raised.

Real-world example

Imagine you want to enter a guarded research building. Going through the main gate means checks, badges and cameras. Instead you befriend the company that refills the coffee machines. That firm has a service key and an easy entry. Inside with the coffee trolley, you can visit every floor and read whatever is left on a desk. Thrip targets the coffee supplier rather than the tower block, because trust and technical access flow along the same route.

Key takeaways

  • Review which suppliers hold remote access to your network, and limit what that access can reach.
  • Require strong authentication and separate accounts for vendor remote support.
  • Watch for administrative sessions at unusual hours or from unexpected locations.

In more detail

Thrip is the name Symantec published in 2018 for an espionage activity set assessed as likely operating on behalf of a state sponsor, with victims concentrated in satellite communications, telecommunications and defence suppliers rather than in the end customer organisations those suppliers serve. The defining characteristic is the business model of the intrusion: the group reportedly obtained access to smaller providers and used that access to reach the larger customers and partners connected to them, effectively turning a supplier relationship into an intrusion path. Reported tradecraft is quiet and tool light, relying on remote administration tools, stolen credentials and living off the land, with public reporting describing re-use of the same infrastructure and tooling across victims and a pragmatic willingness to adopt whatever administrative access is already available. Collection reportedly focused on engineering documents, credentials and information about the parent customer networks the providers served. Evidence is easy to miss because most activity uses legitimate remote administration software and valid accounts, so detection depends on behavioural baselines: remote administration sessions at unusual hours, logins from unexpected addresses, access to file shares outside the user's role, and new accounts or credentials created near a suspected login. A common misunderstanding is that a small supplier is a small risk; in this activity set the supplier is valuable precisely because of who it connects to, which is why third party risk here is a supply chain and trust problem rather than a software defect problem.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a supplier relationship becomes a technical path into customer networks.
  • Describe why legitimate remote administration tools are hard to detect.
  • Explain how valid accounts reduce the value of malware signatures.
  • List the third party access controls that would limit this kind of intrusion.
  • Describe how to investigate a suspected vendor account compromise.
