Firewall

Network SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A firewall is a security control that sits between two networks and decides which traffic may pass. It checks each connection against a list of rules you write. Anything not clearly allowed is blocked by default. Modern firewalls also look at the application behind the traffic, not just the address and port.

Why should you care?

Without a firewall, every machine on your network is open to every other machine and to the internet. One infected laptop could then reach your file servers, backups and management consoles. A firewall limits that reach and gives you logs of what was blocked. But a rule list full of wide allowances is almost as bad as no firewall at all, so review your rules often.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You split your network into zones, such as users, servers and guests.
  2. For each zone pair you write rules that describe allowed traffic and its purpose.
  3. The firewall reads each packet header and finds the first rule that matches.
  4. If no rule matches, the final rule denies the traffic, which is the default deny idea.
  5. Stateful firewalls remember active connections, so replies come back without a new rule.
  6. You send the accept and deny logs to a central place for review and alerts.

Real-world example

Think of a firewall as the doorman at a private club. The doorman holds a guest list that says who may enter, through which door and at what time. Someone not on the list is turned away, even if they look friendly. Once a guest is inside, the doorman remembers them and lets them leave and return without rechecking everything. If the guest list says everyone may enter, the doorman adds nothing.

Key takeaways

  • A firewall allows only the traffic your rules describe and blocks the rest.
  • Rule order decides the result, because the first match wins.
  • Wide rules and old exceptions quietly remove most of the protection.

In more detail

A firewall is a network security control that enforces an explicit policy over which traffic may pass between two zones by inspecting packet headers and, in modern products, application-layer content. Classic packet filters evaluated a five-tuple of source address, destination address, source port, destination port and protocol against an ordered rule list, stopping at the first match and falling through to an implicit deny. Stateful inspection added a connection table so that return traffic belonging to an established session is permitted without a matching inbound rule, which removed many of the permissive workarounds that plagued early deployments. Next-generation firewalls identify applications rather than ports alone, attach user and group identity to flows, decrypt selected TLS sessions, and integrate intrusion prevention and threat intelligence, so the operational boundary between firewall, proxy and IPS has largely dissolved. Defensive value depends almost entirely on policy quality. A rule base that ends in any-any, that carries shadowed rules which can never match, or that accumulates temporary exceptions long after a project closes provides little assurance regardless of the hardware. Effective programmes enforce default deny between zones, document the business owner and expiry of every permit, review the rule base on a schedule, and forward accept and deny logs to a central platform where volume and destination changes are correlated. Firewalls remain necessary but are not sufficient: they cannot see inside encrypted traffic they do not decrypt, they cannot reliably distinguish a trusted internal host that has been compromised, and they are bypassed entirely when users and workloads leave the perimeter.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to explain how stateful inspection differs from stateless packet filtering and why rule order determines the effective policy.
  • You will be able to read a firewall rule base and identify shadowed, redundant and overly permissive rules.
  • You will be able to design a zone-based policy that defaults to deny and permits only documented business flows.
  • You will be able to interpret accept and deny logs when triaging an alert or scoping an incident.
  • You will be able to describe where application identification succeeds and where it fails for tunnelled or encrypted traffic.
  • You will be able to explain the operational risks created by policy drift, temporary any-any rules and weak change control.
