T1596.001 Search Open Technical Databases: DNS/Passive DNS

T1596.001ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This is when an attacker studies your domain name records. The domain name system, usually shortened to DNS, is the internet's address book. It turns a name like example into a number address that computers use.

Why should you care?

Your records show which outside companies handle your email and your website. They can also reveal forgotten subdomains that nobody watches. An old record pointing at a service you no longer own can be taken over by someone else. Reading these records usually leaves no trace on your systems.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker looks up your main name servers to find who answers questions about your domain.
  2. They request the common record types, which reveal mail servers, verification entries and services.
  3. They attempt a bulk copy of all records, which some servers allow by mistake.
  4. They search passive collections that store past lookups, including names that no longer resolve today.
  5. They test each old name to see whether it still points somewhere and who controls that place.
  6. They note names, hosts and providers that can be reused for impersonation or a later attack.

Real-world example

Think of a building directory that still lists a company which moved out two years ago. Anyone reading the board learns the old name and the old floor. If the new tenant of that floor is careless, a visitor can use the old name to walk in unchallenged.

Key takeaways

  • Review your domain records often and remove entries that point at services you no longer own.
  • Block bulk record copying unless a specific trusted server truly needs it.
  • Passive collections keep history, so retiring a name does not remove it from view.

In more detail

This sub-technique is reconnaissance through the domain name system, either by querying a target's authoritative servers directly or by reading passive collections that record historical query and response pairs observed around the internet. Direct queries against records for mail, text verification, service and name server entries expose which third parties handle mail and identity, where services are hosted, and which subdomains exist. Zone transfer misconfiguration and dangling records that still point at released third party addresses are the weaknesses attackers hunt for, because they allow subdomain takeover or reveal internal naming schemes. The parent technique covers open technical databases broadly, while this one is specifically the naming layer that ties addresses, certificates and services together. Passive records are particularly valuable because they reveal hosts that existed before, including staging systems and internal names that briefly resolved. Only the direct query variant reaches victim infrastructure, so authoritative name server logs and recursive resolver logs are the defender's evidence, while passive collection is invisible and must be inferred from later use.

What you will practise

  • Explain what the domain name system is and which record types matter in reconnaissance.
  • Describe how direct record queries differ from reading passive collections.
  • Explain the risk of stale or dangling records and subdomain takeover.
  • Identify the logs that capture direct queries against your name servers.
  • Describe a routine for auditing and cleaning up domain records.
