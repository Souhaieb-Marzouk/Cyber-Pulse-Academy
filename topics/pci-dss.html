PCI DSS

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

PCI DSS is a set of security rules for anyone who handles payment card data. PCI DSS stands for Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard. The rules come from the card brands and apply through your contract with them. They cover storing, processing and sending card numbers, and the systems that could affect them.

Why should you care?

If you take card payments, you cannot simply decide to ignore these rules. Your contract with the card brands requires them, and losing the ability to accept cards can stop your business. The rules also reduce real fraud, because stolen card numbers are easy to sell. Knowing your scope early saves months of extra assessment work.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Draw a map of where card numbers enter, live and leave your systems.
  2. Shrink that area by separating it, so fewer systems are in scope.
  3. Protect stored numbers with encryption and remove data you no longer need.
  4. Restrict access by job need, and give each person a unique login.
  5. Log activity, watch for unexpected changes and test your systems for weaknesses.
  6. Prove all of this to an assessor each year, with evidence not promises.

Real-world example

Think of a shop that keeps cash in one locked room instead of in every drawer. Staff who never touch cash need no key. That is scope reduction. In the same way, if card numbers pass through one separated payment system, the rest of your network is easier to assess. Small, well-guarded areas cost less to protect and prove than a whole building.

Key takeaways

  • Your contract with the card brands makes these rules mandatory.
  • Reduce scope by separating card systems from everything else.
  • Keep evidence of access reviews, logs and tests all year round.

In more detail

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard is a contractual requirement imposed by the card brands and administered by the PCI Security Standards Council on any entity that stores, processes or transmits account data, or that can impact the security of the cardholder data environment. Unlike principle-based regimes, PCI DSS is prescriptive: it defines requirements for network security controls, secure configuration, protection of stored account data, encryption of transmission over open networks, malware protection, secure development, access restriction by business need to know, identification and authentication, physical security, logging and monitoring, security testing and an information security policy supported by risk assessment. Scope is the central engineering problem, and the standard explicitly encourages segmentation and other scope-reduction techniques so that systems neither connected to nor able to impact the cardholder data environment drop out of assessment. Practitioners should expect annual assessment by a Qualified Security Assessor or completion of a self-assessment questionnaire depending on transaction volume, plus quarterly network scans by an Approved Scanning Vendor and continuous evidence of logging, file integrity monitoring, change control and access review. Version 4.x introduced a customised approach allowing an entity to meet a requirement's intent using a documented compensating control methodology, and shifted many controls into a continually operating model rather than an annual evidence scramble.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to define the cardholder data environment and reduce scope through segmentation and data flow analysis.
  • You will be able to translate the twelve requirement areas into specific configuration and process controls.
  • You will be able to prepare the evidence an assessor expects for logging, file integrity monitoring and access review.
  • You will be able to apply the customised approach and document compensating controls that meet a requirement's intent.
  • You will be able to distinguish self-assessment obligations from a Qualified Security Assessor engagement and quarterly scans.
  • You will be able to detect where stored sensitive authentication data has leaked outside the approved data flows.
