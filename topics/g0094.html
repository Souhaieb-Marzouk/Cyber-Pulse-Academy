G0094 Kimsuky

G0094Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Kimsuky is a name for a group of attackers who work for or with North Korea, according to public security reporting. They mostly spy on people instead of breaking into computers with clever software tricks. They pretend to be someone you trust so that you open their file or share information. Many security companies track the same group under different names.

Why should you care?

This group targets knowledge, not money. They want policy papers, research, and access to the inboxes of people who know useful things. A single reply to a friendly message can give them a way into a whole organisation. Because the messages look normal, technical filters alone often miss them. Training your people to check who is really writing is as important as any tool.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attackers study a target and pick a believable role, such as a journalist or a recruiter.
  2. They send a friendly email or message that starts a normal sounding conversation.
  3. The conversation builds trust before any file or link appears.
  4. They send a document or archive that hides the harmful part inside it.
  5. Opening the file starts a small program that fetches the rest from the internet.
  6. Later messages may ask directly for a document, a password, or a login code.

Real-world example

Picture a stranger who wants into a private club. They do not climb the wall. They stand outside and talk to members for a week, learn the names of the staff, then arrive at the door dressed as a delivery driver with a parcel. The person at the desk sees a friendly face, a familiar uniform, and a real parcel, so they open the door. The door was never broken. Someone was simply convinced to open it. Kimsuky works the same way: the conversation is the entry point, and the file at the end is only the parcel.

Key takeaways

  • A friendly message sent to the right person can beat a technical exploit.
  • Check the real identity behind a contact before you open anything they send.
  • Report unusual requests for documents or passwords, even if they look harmless.

In more detail

Kimsuky is a long-running espionage cluster widely tracked as a North Korea-nexus threat group, and public reporting assesses it with moderate to high confidence as state-sponsored rather than criminal. Its tradecraft is unusually people-centric for an intrusion set of this age: the operators spend their effort on believable pretexts rather than on novel exploits, impersonating journalists, academics, think-tank staff, government officials and job recruiters to open a conversation with a target and then deliver a document, a link or an archive. The initial payload is frequently a weaponised office document or an archive containing a shortcut or script that pulls a later stage, so the visible artefact on disk is often only a loader. Reporting repeatedly describes the use of cloud and web services for command and control and for staging stolen files, which means the network evidence often sits in ordinary HTTPS traffic to reputable providers rather than at an obviously hostile address. Kimsuky has also been observed collecting material by simply asking for it: credential-harvesting pages that mirror a real login screen, and follow-up messages that pressure a contact into handing over a document or a password. Evidence of this activity is therefore distributed across mail gateway logs, mailbox audit records, endpoint process lineage for document readers, and cloud service audit trails. Common misunderstandings are that this group depends on zero-day exploits, that it targets only government networks, and that a blocked message ends the campaign; in practice the same operator usually returns with a new persona, and the useful detection signal is the repeated approach pattern rather than a single malicious file.

What you will practise

  • Describe how social engineering serves as the main entry method for a state-linked espionage group.
  • Explain why a weaponised document or archive is often only the first stage of an intrusion.
  • Identify the log sources that would show a convincing pretext message reaching a mailbox.
  • Summarise how cloud services can be abused for command and control and file staging.
  • Explain why blocking one sender rarely ends a persistent espionage campaign.
  • Recognise the signs of credential harvesting pages that copy a real login screen.
