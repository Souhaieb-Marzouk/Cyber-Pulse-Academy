G0105 DarkVishnya

G0105Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

DarkVishnya is the name for a criminal group that robs banks. Public reporting linked to its ATT&CK entry describes attacks on banks in Eastern Europe in 2017 and 2018. It does not only attack over the internet. It also sends someone into the building with a small hidden computer. That device joins the bank's own network from the inside.

Why should you care?

A firewall protects the edge of the network, not the inside. Once a small device is plugged into an office network port, many of your protections simply do not apply. The intruders can then watch traffic, find shared folders, and collect logins at their leisure. Nothing about this shows up in email filters or website blocks. It is also cheap to do, because the devices used cost very little. If you ignore physical access, you leave a door that no software control can close for you.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Someone brings a small hidden computer into the building and plugs it into a network port.
  2. The device sits inside the network, behind the firewall that protects the edge.
  3. It scans the network to find which machines and services are reachable.
  4. It looks for shared folders and listens for login details crossing the network.
  5. The intruders use those logins, and sometimes guess weak passwords, to reach other systems.
  6. They install a small service on the machines they control so access remains after a restart.

Real-world example

Picture a shopping centre at night. All the public doors are locked and the alarms at the main entrance work perfectly. A cleaner props open a fire exit at the back and leaves a small toolbox beside it. In the morning the toolbox is gone, but a camera has been sitting inside it all night, watching the corridors. Nobody broke a lock. The alarm at the front never rang, because the intruder never used the front. Physical access to a network works the same way. If a device gets plugged in inside the building, it does not need to get past the front door at all.

Key takeaways

  • Physical access to a network is a security problem, not just a facilities problem.
  • Unused network ports in meeting rooms and lobbies should be disabled by default.
  • Watch for unknown devices appearing on internal network segments.

In more detail

DarkVishnya is a financially motivated intrusion set that MITRE ATT&CK describes as targeting banks in Eastern Europe, with at least eight institutions attacked in the region across 2017 and 2018 according to the public reporting linked to that entry. It is a useful case study precisely because it is not a pure remote software campaign. The reported entry method begins with physical presence: cheap small computers, single board devices and low cost laptops brought into the building by a visitor, contractor or employee and attached to the corporate network from inside the office. That step puts the device behind the perimeter firewall, on the same segment as the workstations it wants to reach, and it makes many network based assumptions about untrusted outside traffic irrelevant. From that position the reported activity is patient and unremarkable: port scanning to map live services, hunting for the shared folders that hold documents, and capturing authentication material from the network, which is then used directly or extended by guessing weak passwords against accounts. Movement is reported through well known remote administration and service execution utilities rather than exotic malware, and the operators run lightweight command loaders that are installed as services so they survive reboots. Command channels are placed on unusual ports that no business application uses, which is the kind of signal that is easy to see once you decide that only expected destinations are acceptable. Evidence often sits outside the security stack: door access records, visitor logs, camera footage, unfamiliar hardware found during a walk around, and network switch port history showing an unknown device appearing on an internal segment.

What you will practise

  • Explain why a device placed inside the network bypasses perimeter based controls.
  • Describe the reconnaissance steps that follow a rogue device being connected to a network.
  • Explain how capturing authentication traffic differs from guessing credentials by brute force.
  • Identify the physical and network evidence sources that reveal unauthorised hardware on site.
  • Recommend controls that reduce the risk of unauthorised devices joining internal network segments.
