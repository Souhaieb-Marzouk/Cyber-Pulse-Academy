M1018 User Account Management

M1018Enterprise MitigationVery hardMitigation

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

User account management is the control of every account through its whole life. It covers how accounts are requested, approved, given access, reviewed, and finally closed. It also covers accounts that no person owns, such as shared and service logins. The aim is simple: every account has a named owner, a real purpose, and only the access it needs.

Why should you care?

Old accounts are a quiet gift to an attacker. A contractor who left two years ago may still have a working login. A shared password may be known to a dozen people. Because that login looks normal, suspicious activity blends in. Good account management shrinks that hiding place. It also means fewer credentials are worth stealing in the first place.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. A new starter triggers one request, approved by a manager, and built from a template for that role.
  2. The template grants the least access the job needs, instead of copying a colleague's permissions.
  3. Managers review their team's access on a schedule and confirm or remove each entry with evidence.
  4. When someone leaves, the account is disabled at once and any active sessions are cut off.
  5. Service and shared accounts get an owner, a purpose, and regular checks for dormant use.
  6. Accounts unused for a long period are flagged, so orphaned logins surface before an attacker uses them.

Real-world example

Think of a hotel front desk. Every guest gets one key card for one room, and the card stops working at checkout. No master key is left lying in a drawer. If a cleaner leaves the job, their key is collected that day. If a card is lost, it is cancelled immediately. A hotel that skipped these steps would be robbed by whoever found an old card. Account management is that same discipline for logins.

Key takeaways

  • Tie every account to a real person or a named owner.
  • Remove access on the day someone leaves, and never wait until next month.
  • Review access regularly, or old permissions will quietly pile up over time.

In more detail

User account management is the lifecycle control over identity: how accounts are requested, approved, provisioned, reviewed, and removed. In practice it means a governed joiners, movers, and leavers process tied to the authoritative human resources system, role-based entitlement templates that grant least privilege by default rather than cloning an existing colleague, periodic access recertification with real evidence of manager review, prompt and verifiable disabling of accounts on termination, and explicit handling of the accounts that have no human owner, including service, shared, vendor, and emergency identities. It also covers the hygiene properties of identity itself: unique named accounts rather than shared logins, strong password policy with breach-list checks and no forced rotation without cause, and detection of dormant or orphaned accounts that still hold access. The control matters because stale and excessive accounts are a durable attacker advantage. A valid account obtained from a data breach, a leftover contractor login, or a service identity with a static password provides legitimate-looking authentication that blends into normal activity, and it directly enables initial access through valid accounts. It also reduces the value of account discovery, makes the creation of an attacker-controlled account stand out against a clean baseline, and limits the reach of credential access because there are fewer credentials with access worth stealing. Its honest limitations are that identity governance is slow, political, and expensive: recertification campaigns generate rubber-stamped approvals, entitlement templates drift as people change roles, and exception processes quietly accumulate. Failure modes include leavers disabled but not deleted with their sessions and tokens still valid, vendor accounts shared among several engineers with no attribution, service accounts exempted from password rotation and monitoring, unreviewed privileged group membership, and no alerting at all on dormant account reactivation, which is often the first observable sign of an intrusion.

What you will practise

  • Explain how to build a governed joiner, mover, and leaver process anchored to an authoritative source of identity.
  • Describe how role-based entitlement templates and least-privilege defaults prevent access accumulation.
  • Explain how to run access recertification that produces real evidence rather than rubber-stamped approvals.
  • Identify the account categories that routinely escape lifecycle control and describe monitoring for each.
  • Describe how account discovery, creation, and dormant reactivation appear in identity telemetry.
  • Explain how account hygiene properties such as uniqueness and password policy reduce credential-based risk.
  • Evaluate an identity estate for orphaned, shared, and excessively privileged accounts and prioritise cleanup.
