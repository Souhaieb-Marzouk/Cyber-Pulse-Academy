Smishing

Security AwarenessHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Smishing is a fake text message that tries to make you tap a link or give away information. The name comes from short message service, the technology behind texting, combined with phishing. The message often pretends to be a bank, a delivery company or your manager.

Why should you care?

Texts feel personal and urgent, so people act on them quickly. Your phone may not have the same protection as your work email. One tap can lead to a fake login page that captures your password. It can also install an app that reads your messages.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker sends a short text to many numbers at once from a rented service.
  2. The message invents a problem, such as a held parcel or a frozen account.
  3. It includes a link that leads to a page built to look like a real login.
  4. You type your password or a one-time code and the attacker records it.
  5. The attacker uses those details to reach your accounts before you notice.
  6. Report the message, and then change the password from a different device.

Real-world example

Imagine a stranger knocks on your door holding a parcel slip. They say there is a small fee to release your package and ask for your card. It takes ten seconds and the slip looks official. A smishing text is that stranger with a clipboard, delivered to your pocket. The parcel may not exist at all. The safe habit is to close the message and open the delivery company app yourself. Then check whether anything is really waiting.

Key takeaways

  • Never use a link in a text to reach a bank or a delivery account.
  • Report the message and the number through your company reporting route.
  • Use a login code app instead of codes sent by text where you can.

In more detail

Smishing is phishing delivered over short message service or a comparable mobile messaging channel, and the delivery medium changes both the attacker's playbook and the defender's options. Text messages are short, arrive on a personal device, and are read within minutes, so urgency and brevity do the persuasive work that a longer email would need. Common lures are a parcel awaiting a small fee, a bank fraud warning, a toll or tax notice, and a message that appears to come from a colleague or a delivery driver. The link usually points to a mobile-optimised credential harvesting page or triggers a message to a premium number. Detection is harder than with email because the message never reaches the corporate mail gateway, sender identifiers are trivially spoofed or rented, and the handset records little that a security team can query. Real programmes respond by giving staff a one-tap reporting route that forwards the message and the number, by pushing multi-factor authentication that is resistant to code relay, and by watching for authentication attempts from mobile networks and unfamiliar devices. The usual mistake is assuming the corporate email filter covers this channel; it does not.

What you will practise

  • Define smishing and explain how it differs from email phishing.
  • Describe why mobile messaging evades corporate mail filtering.
  • Recognise common lures such as delivery fees, bank alerts and tax notices.
  • Explain how a reported message helps protect other staff.
  • Identify the authentication method that limits the value of a stolen code.
