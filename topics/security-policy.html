Security Policy

GRC & ComplianceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A security policy is a short set of written rules about how the company protects its information. It says what people must do and who is responsible. Leaders approve it and everyone must follow it.

Why should you care?

Without written rules, two teams make different choices and nobody can explain why. A policy gives staff a clear answer when they are unsure, such as who may see customer records. It also shows auditors and customers that the company manages risk on purpose. Rules that people cannot follow show where better tools are needed.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Leaders decide which information and which systems matter most to the business.
  2. They write short rules that describe the required result, not a product name.
  3. The document names the owner of each rule and the date for the next review.
  4. Standards and procedures spell out the exact technical settings and the daily steps.
  5. Staff read and accept the policy, and managers explain what it means for their team.
  6. The owner reviews it on schedule and records any approved exceptions in writing.

Real-world example

Think of the rulebook at a public swimming pool. One page says children under a certain age must be with an adult. Another page, kept behind the desk, tells staff exactly how deep the water is in each lane. A third page tells the lifeguard what to do when someone slips. The policy is the first page, short and clear. The standards and procedures are the pages behind the desk. If the pool only had the short page, staff would still not know how to test the water. If it only had the long pages, no visitor would know the rules.

Key takeaways

  • Write rules people can actually follow, in short and clear sentences.
  • Name an owner and a review date for every policy you publish.
  • Record approved exceptions in writing so audits show a decision, not a gap.

In more detail

A security policy is a written management statement that says what the organisation requires of its people and systems, and who is accountable when those requirements are not met. Policy sits at the top of a hierarchy: policy states intent and mandatory rules, standards fix the specific technical values, procedures describe the step-by-step tasks, and guidelines offer advice that is not binding. A policy constrains behaviour rather than technology, so it is written in terms of outcomes such as who may approve access to regulated data, how long logs are retained, or what must happen before a laptop leaves the building. In a real environment policies surface in onboarding materials, access request workflows, audit evidence requests from external assessors, and the exception register that records approved deviations. The frequent mistake is writing a long document that copies a framework almost word for word, then never routing it through legal, never setting a review date, and never connecting it to a control that someone actually operates. Unenforceable policy creates audit exposure instead of reducing risk.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a security policy is and who approves it.
  • Distinguish policy from standards, procedures and guidelines.
  • Describe how a policy owner reviews and updates a document on schedule.
  • Identify the records that show a policy is followed in practice.
  • Recognise why an unenforceable rule creates audit and risk exposure.
