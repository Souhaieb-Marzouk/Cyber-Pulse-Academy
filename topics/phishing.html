Phishing

Email SecurityHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Phishing is a trick message that pretends to come from someone you trust. It may look like your bank, your manager or a delivery company. The message tries to make you click a link, open a file or send information. The attacker wants your password, your money or your access.

Why should you care?

Most serious break-ins start with one message that someone believed. A stolen password can let an attacker read your email and reach other systems. The damage is not measured only in money. It also costs time, and it breaks trust with your customers. Filters catch many attacks, but no filter catches everything. Your own attention is an important part of the defence.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker copies a message that you already trust, such as an invoice or a login alert.
  2. They register a lookalike domain, or they use a mailbox they already broke into.
  3. Mail checks can confirm the sending domain, but they cannot tell whether the sender means you harm.
  4. The message carries a link to a fake login page or a file that runs code when opened.
  5. When you type your password or approve a prompt, the attacker uses that access straight away.
  6. Your report of a strange message lets the security team block the campaign for everyone.

Real-world example

Imagine the front desk of a large office at night. A man arrives in a courier uniform and holds a parcel with your company logo on it. He says he must leave it inside, and he asks the receptionist to sign for it. The uniform looks right, and the parcel looks right. So she signs, and then she lets him through the secure door. Nobody checked his name against any list, and nobody opened the parcel. A phishing message wears a uniform you already trust in the same way.

Key takeaways

  • Check the request itself, not only the logo, the name or the spelling.
  • Report strange messages quickly, because one report protects your whole team.
  • A message that passes every technical mail check can still be an attack.

In more detail

Phishing is a social engineering technique in which an attacker sends a message that impersonates a party the recipient already trusts, so that the recipient takes an action the attacker wants: entering a password on a lookalike page, approving a multi-factor prompt, opening an attachment, or paying an invoice into a changed bank account. The message is only the delivery mechanism. The machinery behind it is technical: a lookalike or compromised domain, a mail path that passes or fails sender authentication, a harvesting page that proxies the real login screen, or a reply chain hijacked inside a genuine mailbox. Sender authentication standards such as SPF, DKIM and DMARC assert which domain sent a message, and they can pass cleanly on a message that is still malicious, because they say nothing about intent. The mistake practitioners make most often is treating phishing as a user-awareness problem alone, or judging a message by its display name, spelling and sender address. A compromised partner account sends perfect English from a domain that passes every check. Detection therefore leans on relationships and behaviour: first-contact domains, mismatched reply-to paths, newly registered infrastructure, authentication results weighed against the history of that sender, and reports from staff who notice what the filters did not.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a phishing message reaches a mailbox and what the attacker gains from a click, a reply or an approval.
  • Describe how SPF, DKIM and DMARC validate a sending domain, and why a passing result does not prove good intent.
  • Identify the technical signals that separate a lookalike or first-contact domain from a known correspondent.
  • Explain how a compromised legitimate mailbox changes the evidence available during triage.
  • Describe how staff reporting, gateway filtering and link rewriting combine into a single detection pipeline.
  • Analyse a reported message and justify a containment decision using headers, authentication results and user context.
