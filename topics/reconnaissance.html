Reconnaissance

Offensive SecurityVery hardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Reconnaissance is the careful gathering of information about a target before an attack. It builds a map of systems, people and technologies. Passive reconnaissance uses public sources and leaves no trace on the target. Active reconnaissance sends traffic to the target's own systems, so defenders can see it. Good reconnaissance decides what happens next.

Why should you care?

Reconnaissance is the longest phase of an attack, so it gives defenders the earliest warning. Before anyone breaks in, you may see address scanning or unusual login attempts. Your own public information matters too: job adverts, code repositories and old domain records can reveal your technology and your naming habits. If you know what an attacker can learn about you, you can reduce that exposure honestly instead of just hiding it.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker decides what they need to learn about the target first.
  2. They collect free public information, such as domain records and job adverts.
  3. They look for weak signals, such as exposed cloud storage or leaked passwords.
  4. Then they send probes to the target and record which services answer.
  5. All of this becomes an inventory of systems ranked by value and exposure.

Real-world example

Think of a burglar planning a robbery. First they walk the street and note which houses look empty. They check social media to see when the family is on holiday. They might ring the doorbell during the day to see if anyone answers. Only then do they choose a house and a time. All of that watching happens before any lock is touched. Reconnaissance in cyber security is exactly this planning stage.

Key takeaways

  • Reconnaissance happens before the break-in, so it is your early warning.
  • Check what your own public information tells the world about you.
  • Watch for scanning and probing, and treat it as a signal.

In more detail

Reconnaissance is the disciplined collection and organisation of information about a target before and during an engagement, producing the map of systems, identities and technologies that every later phase depends on. Practitioners divide it into passive work, which draws on third-party and public sources and leaves no trace on the target, and active work, which sends traffic to target-owned infrastructure and is therefore visible to defenders. Passive sources include domain registration records, certificate transparency logs, DNS history, public code repositories, cloud storage naming conventions, marketing material, job advertisements that name internal platforms, and breach corpora that reveal credential exposure. Active work includes port and service enumeration, protocol fingerprinting, virtual host and content discovery, and banner or TLS inspection; each of these produces network, firewall and web server evidence that a mature security operations centre can alert on. The output is normally a structured inventory annotated with ownership, exposure and business value rather than a raw list of addresses. For defenders, reconnaissance matters because it is the phase with the longest lead time before exploitation: high volumes of denied flows, sequential destination sweeps, unusual user agents, and monitoring of certificate transparency entries for the organisation's own domains all provide early warning. Reconnaissance quality also determines engagement safety, because scope, rate limits and prohibited techniques are agreed before any packet is sent. Understanding what an adversary can learn from open sources is the most honest route to reducing an attack surface rather than cosmetically hiding it.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to separate passive from active reconnaissance and state what evidence each leaves in network and application logs.
  • You will be able to inventory the open sources that reveal an organisation's naming conventions, technology stack and staff identities.
  • You will be able to describe how enumeration traffic appears in firewall, netflow, DNS and web server telemetry.
  • You will be able to rank attack surface findings by business impact rather than by count.
  • You will be able to explain how certificate transparency and historical DNS monitoring feed an early warning capability.
  • You will be able to draft reconnaissance limits for a rules-of-engagement document, including rate limits and excluded assets.
