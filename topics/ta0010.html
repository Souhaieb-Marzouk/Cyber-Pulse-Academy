TA0010 Exfiltration

TA0010EnterpriseExtremeTactic

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Exfiltration is the actual removal of data from your network. After collecting and packing the files, the attacker sends them out. They may upload to a cloud storage site, attach files to email, or push data through a normal business channel. Small, targeted theft is often the most damaging.

Why should you care?

Once your data is outside, you cannot take it back. This is the last point where you can prevent harm instead of describing it. Detection is hard because your staff legitimately upload and share large files every day. The useful questions are how much, to where, by whom, and whether that fits their normal work.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker already holds the packed files, usually in a single archive or folder.
  2. They choose an exit route, such as a cloud drive, a web upload or outbound email.
  3. They send the data out, sometimes slowly over days to stay under volume limits.
  4. They may hide the transfer inside normal traffic, such as a video call or software update.
  5. Defenders watch upload sizes and destinations, and compare them with each user's history.

Real-world example

Imagine a removal van leaving an office car park. A single van at lunchtime is unremarkable. Fifty vans leaving at midnight, all from one department, is not. But smugglers rarely use fifty vans. They take one small box each day in a normal-looking bag, and nobody counts bags at the gate. That slow, quiet removal is the hardest kind to spot, and it is why you must know what normal looks like.

Key takeaways

  • Exfiltration is data actually leaving your network, and it cannot be undone.
  • Small transfers through approved tools are easy for defenders to miss.
  • Learn what normal uploads look like for each team you support.

In more detail

Exfiltration is the tactic covering the removal of data from the victim environment after it has been collected and staged, and it is usually the last point at which defenders can still prevent business harm rather than only measure it. Behaviours include transferring files over the existing command and control channel, uploading data to legitimate cloud storage, file-sharing, code-hosting, or webmail services, sending it over alternative protocols such as file transfer or mail, using physical media on endpoints, and throttling transfers to stay under volumetric alerting thresholds. Telemetry is abundant but concentrated at the boundary: proxy and web gateway logs with upload volume and destination category, netflow records showing large asymmetric transfers, data loss prevention alerts on content or volume, cloud audit logs recording file uploads and sharing changes, mail gateway records of outbound attachments, and endpoint events for removable media and archive creation. Detection in isolation is hard because organisations legitimately move large amounts of data to those same services every day, and because the most damaging exfiltration is often small and targeted, such as a handful of documents leaving through an approved channel. Analysis therefore depends on joining collection and exfiltration evidence, comparing transfer volume against the account's own history and peer group, and knowing which destinations and volumes are normal for each business function before an incident begins.

What you will practise

  • Describe the main exfiltration channels and the specific telemetry each generates at the network, cloud, mail, and endpoint boundary.
  • Analyse transfer volume, direction, timing, and destination reputation against per-user and per-host baselines.
  • Join collection evidence to exfiltration evidence to establish a defensible data-loss narrative.
  • Explain how egress control, data loss prevention, and cloud sharing restrictions complement each other and where each fails.
  • Recognise low-volume targeted exfiltration that will not trigger volumetric thresholds and design detections for it.
  • Draft the containment decision points that determine whether an in-progress transfer can actually be stopped.
