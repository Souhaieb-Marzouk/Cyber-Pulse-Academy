Demilitarised Zone

Security ArchitectureHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A DMZ, or demilitarised zone, is a small separate network for servers that the public must reach. It sits between the open internet and your private company network. A firewall controls what can pass on each side. If one of those public servers is broken into, the attacker is still stuck in that middle area.

Why should you care?

Web and mail servers are exposed to the whole internet, so they get attacked every day. If such a server sits among your private systems, one break-in can expose everything. A DMZ limits the damage to a few machines. It also makes the rules easier to write and to check. The name comes from a buffer zone between two armies.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You build a separate network area for services that outsiders must be able to reach.
  2. An outer firewall allows only the specific ports those public services need.
  3. An inner firewall controls and mostly blocks traffic from that area to the private network.
  4. You copy only the data the public service truly needs, and never the whole database.
  5. You monitor and patch the public servers more often than the rest of the estate.
  6. You test the rules regularly to prove that the inner barrier still holds.

Real-world example

Think of a courthouse. Visitors may enter the public lobby, show their bags, and speak at a service window. They cannot walk into the judges' offices behind it. If someone causes trouble in the lobby, the guards deal with it there. Staff pass between the lobby and the offices through a controlled door that logs every movement.

Key takeaways

  • A DMZ only works when both the outer and the inner firewall are in place.
  • Put only the minimum data and services in the public area.
  • The public servers need to be treated as the most attackable machines you own.

In more detail

A demilitarised zone, or DMZ, is a network segment that sits between an untrusted network such as the internet and the protected internal network, and it hosts services that outside users must reach. The design principle is that the DMZ is the only place an external party can connect to directly, so a successful compromise of a public web or mail server does not by itself place an attacker on the internal network. Traffic is controlled by firewall rules on both sides: inbound connections are permitted only to specific published services, and connections from the DMZ into the internal network are restricted or brokered rather than open. The term is borrowed from a neutral buffer area between opposing forces. Real environments often implement it as a separate virtual local area network, a dedicated subnet, or a cloud account with its own routing and security groups. The mistake people usually make is treating the DMZ as a single strong wall, then placing it inside the same network and trust domain as internal servers, which removes the second barrier the design depends on.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a DMZ is and which design goal it serves.
  • Describe the role of the outer and inner firewalls in separating trust levels.
  • Identify which kinds of services belong in a DMZ and which do not.
  • Explain why the DMZ is treated as a separate trust zone rather than a single hardened wall.
  • Recognise common design errors, such as placing a DMZ inside the internal network.
