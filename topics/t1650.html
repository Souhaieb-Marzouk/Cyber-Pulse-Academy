T1650 Acquire Access

T1650Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Sometimes attackers do not break in themselves. They buy access to a system that somebody else already broke into. That could be a password, a hidden backdoor, or a remote login. The buyer then walks in through a door that is already open.

Why should you care?

You may never see the first break-in, because it happened before the buyer arrived. The signs you detect belong to the second stage, and the original entry may be months old. That shortens the time defenders have to react and makes the scope of an incident harder to judge. A single weakness can also be sold to several attackers. Understanding this changes how you investigate a suspected intrusion.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. A broker compromises a target, often with stolen credentials or an unpatched internet service.
  2. The broker hides a way back in, such as a web shell, a remote access tool, or extra accounts.
  3. They advertise the access to known buyers and describe the victim and its privileges.
  4. A buyer pays and receives the details, sometimes along with a small helper program.
  5. The buyer logs in through the existing foothold, so no new exploit is needed.
  6. The original weakness may be reused or resold, so several intruders share one entry point.

Real-world example

Imagine a landlord who finds a spare key in the street and never changes the lock. Instead of using it, the landlord sells the key to a stranger. The stranger then walks into the flat calmly, without breaking anything. Neighbours see nothing unusual and no alarm sounds, because the door was opened with a key. If the landlord copies the key and sells it twice, two different strangers now share the same flat. Acquiring access works like this. The person who broke in first may not be the person who causes the damage you finally detect.

Key takeaways

  • Not every intrusion begins with an attack that your network can show you.
  • Assume a purchased foothold existed quietly for a long time before you noticed it.
  • Hunt for the broker's leftovers, such as web shells, extra accounts, and remote tools.

In more detail

Acquire access is the purchase or transfer of an already compromised foothold rather than the creation of one. An adversary buys entry from an initial access broker, receives a planted backdoor or a set of working credentials, and skips the effort of finding and exploiting a weakness. The commodity being traded is quiet: access to a host that lacks monitoring, an account with high privileges, or a company in a chosen sector. Brokers often leave a load, a small piece of code the buyer can use to install whatever payload they prefer. The defensive problem is that nothing in the victim's telemetry shows a break-in at the moment of use, because the compromise happened earlier and may have been sold more than once. Investigators instead look for the artefacts the broker left, unexplained remote service access, web shells, new local accounts or scheduled tasks, and for the gap between the original intrusion and the first observed malicious action.
