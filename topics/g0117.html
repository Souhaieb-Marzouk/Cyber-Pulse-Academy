G0117 Fox Kitten

G0117Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Fox Kitten is a name for a group of attackers who break into company networks. Security companies believe this group works for the government of Iran, but that link comes from public research, not from a court. The group is known by other names too, like UNC757 and Parisite. It usually attacks the machines that let staff work from home.

Why should you care?

This group shows that state attackers often sell or share stolen access instead of running their own extortion. That means a quiet break-in can turn into ransomware weeks later. If you ignore it, you may face two problems at once. Your company secrets can be copied, and your files can be locked. Fixing remote access tools is the most useful defence against the methods this group uses.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The group scans the internet for open remote access services. These include VPNs (virtual private networks) and web portals that staff use from home.
  2. When it finds an old, unpatched device, it uses a known weakness to get inside. This is like entering through a door nobody repaired.
  3. Once inside, it creates hidden access such as web shells, which are small files that run commands on a server.
  4. It steals usernames, passwords and keys so it can return even after the first hole is closed.
  5. It may then hand the access to another group. That other group runs ransomware and takes the money.

Real-world example

Picture an office with a back door used by delivery drivers. The lock on that door is broken and nobody fixes it. One person notices, slips inside, and copies a master key. That person never steals anything. Instead, they give the key to a different crew. Weeks later that crew walks in through the same back door and empties the safe. Fox Kitten behaves like the first person. It looks for the forgotten door, keeps a copy of the key, and often lets someone else take the money.

Key takeaways

  • Internet-facing systems such as VPN gateways are the first target, so patch them quickly.
  • A quiet break-in can become ransomware later, because access is often sold or shared.
  • Track attackers by their behaviour, not only by one company's nickname for them.

In more detail

Fox Kitten is a state-linked intrusion set that public reporting attributes to Iran. It is tracked under several names, including UNC757, Parisite, Pioneer Kitten, RUBIDIUM and Lemon Sandstorm. The group has been active since roughly 2017 and focuses on breaching internet-facing systems, such as virtual private network (VPN) appliances, remote access gateways and web servers, to obtain an initial foothold inside a target network. Researchers have described repeated exploitation of known vulnerabilities in perimeter devices, followed by web shells, tunnelling tools and stolen credentials used to keep access. Reporting suggests the group has sometimes handed that access to ransomware operators instead of running extortion itself. Defenders see it through unusual perimeter device logins, newly created accounts, unexpected web shell files on external servers and outbound connections from network appliances.

What you will practise

  • Explain what Fox Kitten is and why public reporting attributes it to Iran.
  • Describe how perimeter devices such as VPN gateways are used to gain first access.
  • Recognise evidence of web shells, stolen credentials and new accounts on external servers.
  • List the aliases used for this group and explain why one group can have many names.
  • Summarise why initial access is sometimes handed to a separate ransomware operator.
  • Identify the patch and monitoring steps that reduce exposure to this tradecraft.
