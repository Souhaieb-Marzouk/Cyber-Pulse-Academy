G0046 FIN7

G0046Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

FIN7 is a tracking name for a criminal group that targets money, not state secrets. Security companies also call it Carbon Spider and Sangria Tempest. Several people linked to the activity have been charged in the United States. The group is known for phone calls and emails that pretend to come from suppliers or technical support.

Why should you care?

This group often asks staff to install remote support software, which is a legitimate tool. Nothing is broken into, and no malware signature appears. A worker simply follows a friendly request. It also targets restaurant, hotel and retail chains, where payment systems hold valuable data. Teach staff to verify before they install anything.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Someone calls a help desk and pretends to be a trusted supplier or a manager.
  2. They ask for a small favour, such as installing a support tool to fix a problem.
  3. That tool gives the caller remote control of the computer with the user's permission.
  4. From there they search for payment systems, mail accounts and saved passwords.
  5. They move quietly between computers using ordinary administrator tools and shared accounts.
  6. Later they steal card data, or they lock files and demand a payment.

Real-world example

Imagine a busy restaurant kitchen during the dinner rush. A man in a delivery uniform walks in with a clipboard and says the new card machine needs a quick update. The manager is too busy to check, so she lets him plug in a laptop. He is polite, confident and helpful. Being busy is exactly what he was counting on.

Key takeaways

  • Verify callers through a number you look up yourself, never one they give.
  • Remote support tools are useful, so control who may install them.
  • Keep payment systems on their own network, away from everyday computers.

In more detail

FIN7 is a financially motivated intrusion set tracked by multiple vendors under names including Carbon Spider, Sangria Tempest, ELBRUS, and Navigator Group. Unlike state-sponsored groups, attribution here is largely a criminal-tracking exercise rather than a question of state sponsorship, and several individuals associated with the activity have been publicly charged in the United States, which gives the group an unusually concrete legal dimension compared with most clusters. Reporting describes a long-running campaign against the retail, hospitality, restaurant, and gaming sectors, with additional targeting of banking, insurance, and healthcare organisations, concentrated heavily in the United States with victims also reported in Europe and Australia. The group's best-documented tradecraft is social engineering at scale: telephone calls and email approaches impersonating legitimate service providers, catering suppliers, or IT support, used to persuade employees to open documents or install remote management software. Reporting has described both malicious document delivery exploiting application features and, in later waves, the use of legitimate remote monitoring and management tooling as an initial access vector, which is significant because it makes the intrusion look like normal administrative activity. Post-compromise behaviour in public reporting includes the deployment of custom point-of-sale malware in earlier campaigns, a shift toward ransomware and extortion activity including through affiliates, credential theft, reconnaissance of payment and email systems, and lateral movement using standard administrative protocols. The group has been described as operating with a structured organisational model including separate teams for intrusion and monetisation, and has been reported selling or granting access to other criminal operators. For defenders in retail and hospitality, the practical priority is detecting abuse of remote management software, unusual outbound payment-related traffic, and social engineering that arrives through voice channels that email-focused controls do not cover.

What you will practise

  • Explain how abuse of legitimate remote monitoring and management tooling differs from malware-based initial access and how to detect it.
  • Analyse telephone-oriented social engineering against help desk and finance functions and design verification controls that resist it.
  • Detect point-of-sale and payment environment reconnaissance using network segmentation and process telemetry.
  • Explain how criminal access brokers change the threat model for an organisation that may be a secondary rather than primary target.
  • Build detection coverage for credential theft and lateral movement in a flat retail network and prioritise segmentation work.
  • Compare the group's documented shift from payment card theft to ransomware and extortion and explain the defensive implications.
  • Explain how legal and law enforcement reporting can inform but not replace technical threat intelligence.
