G0054 Sowbug

G0054Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Sowbug is a research name for a group that steals information from government offices and diplomatic organisations. Public reports describe activity in South America and Southeast Asia. Different security companies have linked the group to different countries, so treat those claims carefully.

Why should you care?

This group is quiet on purpose. They do not want to be noticed, so they avoid noisy tools and stay for a long time. That means simple alerts may never fire. If your team only reacts to obvious damage, a patient intruder can read your documents for months without any alarm.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker sends a careful message to a small number of chosen people.
  2. The message carries a file that installs a small hidden program.
  3. That program waits quietly and contacts the attacker only now and then.
  4. Stolen passwords are used to reach file shares and email servers.
  5. Documents of interest are copied slowly to avoid large traffic spikes.
  6. The hidden program is updated or replaced so the access survives cleanup.

Real-world example

Think of a library where a visitor signs in and finds a quiet seat. He stays for hours and reads one page at a time from a book. Each time he puts the book back in the right place. The librarian sees nothing unusual at all. Only the sign-in sheet shows the same visitor returning every week for a year.

Key takeaways

  • Quiet, long-term access needs detection based on patterns over time.
  • Watch for services or scheduled tasks that appear without a change request.
  • Attribution for this group is contested, so report it as an assessment.

In more detail

Sowbug is an espionage cluster that public reporting has associated with activity against government and diplomatic targets in South America and Southeast Asia, with intrusion evidence described from around 2015 onward. Reporting has attributed the activity to a state-linked group, though naming and attribution differ across vendors and should be presented as an assessment rather than a settled fact. The tradecraft described is patient and low-noise: custom backdoors delivered through spear phishing or short-lived web infrastructure, then long periods of quiet collection from file shares and email. Public analyses have highlighted reusable malware families with modular loaders and the use of stolen credentials and remote services for lateral movement. Because the group avoids disruptive actions, the strongest evidence is often timeline-based: a new scheduled task, a service created on a server that never had one, or authentication from an unusual geography followed weeks later by bulk access to documents. The common misunderstanding is expecting loud malware; here the intrusion is defined by persistence and restraint.

What you will practise

  • Explain why low-noise, long-dwell espionage is hard to detect with single alerts.
  • Describe how scheduled tasks and new services reveal unauthorised persistence.
  • Explain the role of stolen credentials in reaching file shares and mail.
  • Interpret bulk document access as a collection indicator.
  • Present contested attribution carefully in written reporting.
