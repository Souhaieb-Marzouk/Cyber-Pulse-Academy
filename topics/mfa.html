MFA

Identity & AccessHardKeyword

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is an authentication control that requires a principal to present evidence from two or more distinct factor categories before a session is granted. The categories are knowledge (something you know, such as a password or PIN), possession (something you have, such as a hardware token, smart card or registered phone), and inherence (something you are, such as a fingerprint or face geometry), with location and behavioural signals sometimes treated as additional context rather than true factors. Because a stolen password alone satisfies only one category, MFA directly disrupts credential replay, password spraying and credential stuffing against externally exposed services, and it is one of the few controls that measurably changes the economics of initial access for ransomware operators. The strength of an implementation depends far less on the label than on the binding between factors: push notification approval, SMS one-time codes and email codes are all phishable or relayable, and adversary-in-the-middle toolkits that proxy the legitimate login page can capture a session token after the user completes a genuine prompt. Practitioners therefore evaluate MFA by the assurance properties it actually delivers: phishing resistance, number matching, origin binding, device-bound keys, and the telemetry the identity provider emits so that anomalous enrolment, factor reset and impossible-travel events can be alerted on. Deployment also has to survive operational reality, including service accounts, legacy protocols that cannot present a second factor, break-glass accounts, and help-desk enrolment fraud, which is why conditional access policy, number matching and FIDO2 security keys are typically combined rather than deployed in isolation.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to distinguish authentication factor categories and explain why two prompts of the same category do not constitute MFA.
  • You will be able to evaluate push, SMS, TOTP and FIDO2 authenticators by their resistance to phishing and adversary-in-the-middle relay.
  • You will be able to design an MFA rollout that covers privileged accounts, legacy protocols and break-glass access without creating lockout risk.
  • You will be able to build detection logic for MFA fatigue, factor enrolment changes, and successful sign-ins from new devices or geographies.
  • You will be able to explain how session token theft defeats MFA and what conditional access or token protection settings reduce that exposure.
  • You will be able to assess help-desk and self-service reset workflows for enrolment fraud and describe compensating verification steps.
