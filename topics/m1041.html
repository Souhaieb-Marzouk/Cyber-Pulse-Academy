M1041 Encrypt Sensitive Information

M1041Enterprise MitigationExtremeMitigation

Encrypting sensitive information means ensuring that data is unreadable to anyone who does not hold the corresponding key, both while it is stored and while it travels between systems. In production the requirement resolves into a small set of concrete patterns: full-disk encryption bound to a hardware root of trust so a stolen laptop yields nothing without the platform, database and column-level encryption for regulated fields, object storage configured for server-side encryption under a customer-managed key, secret managers that remove plaintext credentials from configuration files and build pipelines, and modern TLS with validated certificates for every service-to-service and client connection. Key management is where such programmes succeed or fail. Envelope encryption through a key management service or hardware security module keeps data keys short-lived and auditable, rotation schedules and a cryptographic agility plan limit the blast radius of a compromised key, and separation of duties prevents the team that operates the data from also controlling the keys that protect it. The control blunts exfiltration over command-and-control channels and public web services by reducing what stolen bulk data is worth without its keys, protects credentials and tokens at rest so that an exposed file share or backup does not immediately become account takeover, and makes salted, deliberately slow storage of password material expensive to attack offline. Its limitations are real: data must be decrypted somewhere, and that endpoint, its memory, and its logs are the practical attack surface; keys stored beside the data reduce encryption to presentation; a legitimate session used by an intruder is unaffected; and aggressive inspection or key escrow can quietly undo the protection. Encryption changes the economics of a breach rather than preventing one.

What you will practise

  • Explain the difference between encryption at rest, in transit, and at the application layer, and where each genuinely helps.
  • Design an envelope-encryption scheme with a key management service or hardware security module that keeps data keys short-lived.
  • Identify where keys, tokens, and plaintext secrets leak through configuration files, backups, and pipeline variables.
  • Assess certificate lifecycle and cipher configuration as continuing operational risks rather than one-time build tasks.
  • Evaluate how key custody, escrow, and separation of duties determine the real strength of an encryption deployment.
  • Plan key rotation and cryptographic agility without breaking dependent services or losing historical data access.
