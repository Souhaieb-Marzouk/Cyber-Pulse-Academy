BGP Hijacking

Network SecurityExtremeKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

BGP hijacking is when someone tells the internet's routing system that they own a block of internet addresses they do not really own. Routers believe the claim and send traffic to the wrong place. Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP, is the set of rules networks use to swap these route claims.

Why should you care?

The internet has no central referee for routes. A wrong claim can spread worldwide in minutes. Traffic meant for a bank or a government site may quietly flow through a stranger's network. That can take a service offline, or let someone read and change data in transit. You cannot fix this alone, but you can watch for it and report it fast.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Every network on the internet owns blocks of addresses, called prefixes, and announces them to its neighbours.
  2. Neighbours pass those announcements along, so a route claim spreads across the world within minutes.
  3. Routers always prefer the most specific route, so a smaller fake prefix beats the real, larger one.
  4. In a hijack someone announces a prefix they do not own, and traffic is delivered to them instead.
  5. Sometimes a network does this by accident; we call that a route leak, and the effect looks similar.
  6. Defenders compare watched routes with expected ones and publish signed proof of who may announce what.

Real-world example

Imagine you move house and file a change of address form with the post office. A stranger files the same form for your street, and the post office believes whoever filed last. Now all your letters go to the stranger's door. They can throw your post away, which is like an outage, or open it, copy it and send it on, which is like interception. Routing works the same way, because networks trust announcements unless someone checks them.

Key takeaways

  • Internet routing runs on trust, so a false claim can redirect real traffic.
  • Hijacks cause outages or interception, and both are serious.
  • Watch your routes, and report strange changes to your provider quickly.

In more detail

The Border Gateway Protocol is the routing protocol that exchanges reachability information between autonomous systems on the internet, and it operates on trust: a network announces the address prefixes it can reach, and its neighbours generally believe the announcement. BGP hijacking exploits that trust by announcing a prefix the attacker does not legitimately control, or by announcing a more specific prefix that wins longest-prefix matching, so traffic destined for the real owner is delivered elsewhere. The consequences range from denial of service, because traffic is blackholed or dropped, to interception, because the attacker forwards traffic onward while observing or modifying it. A route leak, where a network accidentally re-announces routes learned from one provider to another, produces similar effects without malicious intent and is a frequent cause of large-scale incidents. Because the protocol has no built-in origin authentication, a bad announcement can propagate globally within minutes and is corrected only after the legitimate operator and its providers respond, which means detection speed matters as much as prevention. Prevention uses cryptographic or registry-based attestation of route origin: a resource public key infrastructure with route origin authorisations lets a network publish which autonomous system may originate which prefix, and BGPsec aims to protect the path itself, although adoption remains uneven. Filtering at the edge, rejecting announcements for prefixes a peer is not authorised to originate and applying maximum prefix limits, prevents a large share of accidental leaks. Detection complements prevention through public route monitoring services, looking-glass queries, and comparison of observed paths against expected paths. For defenders outside routing teams, hijacking matters because it can redirect traffic to an attacker-controlled network, support impersonation of a domain or service, and enable interception of sessions whose protection depends on the endpoints involved.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to explain how prefix announcements and longest-prefix matching make hijacking possible.
  • You will be able to distinguish a malicious hijack from an accidental route leak by evidence.
  • You will be able to assess whether an anomaly should be described as denial of service or interception.
  • You will be able to describe how route origin authorisation and BGPsec constrain unauthorised announcements.
  • You will be able to design edge filtering and maximum prefix limits for peers and customers.
  • You will be able to use route monitoring and looking-glass data to detect and report an anomalous announcement.
