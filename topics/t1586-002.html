T1586.002 Compromise Accounts: Email Accounts

T1586.002Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This is the email part of account compromise. The parent technique covers taking over any online account. Here the attacker takes over a mailbox that already belongs to a person or a company. They can read old messages, send new ones and hide what they do. The messages come from a real, trusted address, which is why this works so well.

Why should you care?

Email carries invoices, contracts and instructions inside most companies. A stolen mailbox lets the attacker watch a deal and send a payment request at the right moment. They can also hide the replies that would expose them. Because the message comes from a real address, filters often allow it and staff trust it. Suppliers and customers can be drawn in through a thread that already exists. Losing one mailbox can lead to a large financial loss before anyone notices.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker obtains the mailbox password through phishing, reuse or a purchase.
  2. They sign in, often from a new location, and read recent conversations.
  3. They create a rule that forwards or hides messages from chosen senders.
  4. They watch a payment or delivery thread until the timing is right.
  5. They reply from the real account asking for new bank details or files.
  6. The mailbox owner may never see the replies because rules moved them away.

Real-world example

Think of a company's incoming mail tray. A clerk decides where each letter goes and which letters the manager never sees. If someone else takes that seat, they can quietly remove the letters that would warn the manager. They can also write new letters and sign them with the manager's name. Everything the outside world sees looks correct, because the letters leave the real office with the real signature. That seat is a compromised mailbox, and it is far more useful than a copied key.

Key takeaways

  • Look for inbox rules that forward or delete mail without the owner's knowledge.
  • Confirm payment changes by phone using a number you already had.
  • Multi-factor authentication stops most stolen passwords from working.

In more detail

A hijacked mailbox is one of the most effective positions in an intrusion, because the attacker can read the organisation's history and speak in the owner's voice from the owner's real address. Payment fraud follows a recognisable pattern: a mailbox rule quietly moves or hides replies, the attacker studies an existing thread, then introduces a change of bank details or a request for files at the moment the conversation was already heading that way. Because the mail originates from a legitimate domain, sender authentication checks pass and reputation filtering raises no objection. Detectable traces are account-side: new inbox rules, unusual sign-in locations, forwarding to external addresses, and outbound volume that shifts away from the owner's normal pattern.

What you will practise

  • Explain why a compromised mailbox supports fraud so effectively.

  • Describe how an existing email thread is hijacked for a payment request.
  • Recommend controls that limit the impact of a mailbox takeover.
