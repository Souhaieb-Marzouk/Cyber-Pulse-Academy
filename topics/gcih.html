Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

GCIH stands for Global Information Assurance Certification Certified Incident Handler. It is a defensive certification about managing a security incident from the first alert to the final report. You learn to confirm what happened, limit the damage, remove the attacker, and restore normal service. The exam uses realistic scenarios rather than plain definitions.

Why should you care?

When an attacker is inside a network, every minute counts, and rushed actions often destroy the clues you need. A trained handler keeps calm, protects evidence, and contains the problem without warning the attacker. GCIH shows employers you can run that process. It also helps you explain the incident to managers in words they can act on.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You prepare in advance: contact lists, tools and clear rules for who decides what.
  2. When an alert fires, you triage it and decide whether it is real and how urgent.
  3. You gather fragile evidence first, such as memory and active connections, before any restart.
  4. You contain the incident, for example by isolating a machine, while keeping the evidence intact.
  5. You remove the attacker access, then restore services step by step and watch closely.
  6. You write a timeline and lessons learned, so the same door is closed for good.

Real-world example

Think of water leaking through an office ceiling. You do not start by ripping out the whole ceiling. First you find where the water comes from, shut that valve, and put buckets under the drip. Then you photograph the damage for the insurance claim before you repair anything. Only later do you replace the pipe and check the rest of the building. Incident response follows the same order: find, contain, record, fix, and check.

Key takeaways

  • Collect fragile evidence such as memory before you restart or rebuild.
  • Contain first, but avoid actions that warn the attacker.
  • A clear timeline helps both engineers and managers understand the incident.

In more detail

GIAC GCIH (Global Information Assurance Certification Certified Incident Handler) is an advanced, vendor-neutral certification centred on detecting, containing, eradicating and recovering from security incidents, including the adversary techniques that create them. It targets experienced security operations analysts, incident responders, forensic examiners and consultants who already understand host and network fundamentals and now need to run an investigation from first alert to final report under time pressure. Passing the exam demonstrates to an employer that the holder can triage an alert stream, form and test a hypothesis, preserve perishable evidence before shutdown, contain a live intrusion without destroying the evidence that explains it, and communicate findings that a non-technical stakeholder can act on. The exam is proctored and open-book, using multiple-choice and multiple-answer items with a scaled passing score, and scenario-style questions dominate: candidates are asked to choose the next correct action or interpret a short artefact rather than recite a definition. Hands-on practice is decisive. Responders who have personally walked through live-response triage, timeline reconstruction, memory and disk acquisition, and containment decisions on systems they built and broke will recognise the intended answer quickly, whereas candidates who rely on courseware alone frequently misread which phase a question sits in and select an action that is sound in general but wrong at that moment. GCIH is generally treated as evidence of operational readiness rather than theoretical knowledge of response process.

Exam domains and objectives

  • Incident response lifecycle: Apply preparation, identification, containment, eradication, recovery and post-incident review to a live intrusion, and justify each decision against the phase it belongs to.
  • Adversary techniques and tradecraft: Interpret common initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation and lateral movement behaviour from the defender's position.
  • Detection and triage: Build hunt hypotheses, prioritise alerts and separate true positives from environmental noise using host, network and identity telemetry.
  • Volatile evidence handling: Order the collection of memory, process, network and session state so perishable evidence survives before any shutdown or remediation step.
  • Host and memory investigation: Interpret process trees, autostart locations, services, scheduled execution and memory artefacts to identify malicious execution and persistence.
  • Network evidence and containment: Use flow and packet evidence to scope an intrusion, then contain it without alerting the adversary or corrupting the record of what happened.
  • Malware and tooling triage: Characterise suspicious binaries and scripts safely, describe observed behaviour, and decide what to block, isolate or hunt for next.
  • Eradication and recovery: Verify removal of adversary access, restore services in a controlled order, and define the monitoring that confirms the environment stays clean.
  • Communication and reporting: Produce timelines, findings, confidence statements and recommendations that serve both technical peers and executive decision makers.
