Alert Triage

SOC & Detection EngineeringHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Triage is the first careful look at an alert. The analyst decides what the alert really means, how urgent it is, and what should happen next. Each alert ends with a written decision, the evidence behind it, and a note on whether to escalate. It is a bounded task, not an open-ended investigation.

Why should you care?

Triage is where a real attack is either caught or missed. A rushed decision sends a serious incident home as harmless, and the attacker gets days of quiet time. A careless escalation floods the response team with nothing. Doing triage the same careful way each time is what makes a security team reliable.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Check that the alert is real and not a duplicate or a known scheduled job.
  2. Identify the entities involved: which accounts, machines and network addresses are named.
  3. Open the raw logs and rebuild the timeline yourself instead of trusting the summary.
  4. Look for evidence from other sources that confirms or contradicts the alert.
  5. Now decide whether the activity is harmless, merely suspicious, or confirmed malicious.
  6. Write down your decision, your confidence and the evidence, then escalate if needed.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital emergency department. A nurse sees every patient first and sorts them fast: the chest pain goes straight in, the sprained ankle waits. The nurse does not treat anyone, but that quick sorting decides everything that follows. Alert triage is that first assessment. Get the sorting wrong, and the wrong patient waits while the serious one sits in the chairs.

Key takeaways

  • Read the raw logs; never decide based only on the alert title.
  • Write your reasoning down so another analyst can check it.
  • Sort the queue by asset importance and account privilege, not just severity.

In more detail

Triage is the first analytical pass over an alert or a queue of alerts, in which an analyst decides what the signal means, how urgent it is, and what happens next. It is a bounded activity with a defined output, not open-ended investigation: for each alert the analyst produces a disposition, a confidence statement, the evidence that supports it, and an escalation decision. Good triage follows a fixed sequence. Validate that the alert is real and not a duplicate, a scheduled job or a known test; establish the entities involved, meaning accounts, hosts, source addresses and business context; reconstruct the timeline from raw logs rather than trusting the alert's own summary; look for corroborating or contradicting telemetry from independent sources such as identity, endpoint and network records; and only then decide whether the activity is benign, suspicious or confirmed malicious. The consistent failure modes are well understood. Analysts anchor on the alert title and never open the underlying events. They accept an enrichment field as fact when it is stale or unverified. They close an alert because a similar one was benign last month. They escalate too early and burn responder capacity, or too late and let a foothold mature. Effective SOCs reduce these errors structurally: clear severity and escalation criteria, checklists for the highest-volume alert types, suppression with an expiry date rather than permanent closure, and requirement that the disposition evidence be written down so a reviewer can reproduce the judgement. Triage quality is best measured not by alerts closed but by escalation precision and by whether real incidents were initially triaged as benign.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to apply a repeatable triage sequence that validates, contextualises, reconstructs and corroborates an alert before disposition.
  • You will be able to reconstruct an event timeline from raw telemetry instead of relying on an alert's generated summary.
  • You will be able to write a disposition that records evidence, confidence and escalation rationale in a form another analyst can reproduce.
  • You will be able to prioritise a mixed queue using asset criticality, identity privilege and observed adversary behaviour rather than raw severity labels.
  • You will be able to recognise and correct common triage biases such as title anchoring, stale enrichment trust and precedent-based closure.
  • You will be able to decide when to suppress rather than close an alert, and specify an expiry and review condition for the suppression.
