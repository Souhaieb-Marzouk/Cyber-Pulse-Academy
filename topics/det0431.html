DET0431 Detection Strategy for Email Spoofing

DET0431Detection StrategyVery hardDetection Strategy

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This detection looks for email that pretends to come from your company but was not sent by your company. The rule reads the message headers, which are the technical notes attached to every email. It reports the messages where those notes do not agree.

Why should you care?

A fake message from a known name is very convincing. Staff may pay a false invoice or enter a password on a fake page. The email text itself can look perfect, so you cannot judge by reading it. The headers carry proof of where the message came from. Checking them is the reliable way to spot a forgery.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. An email arrives at your mail gateway from somewhere on the internet.
  2. The gateway checks the sending server against the domain records that list allowed senders.
  3. It writes the results into the message headers as pass, fail or none.
  4. The rule reads the claimed sender domain, the envelope sender and the reply to address.
  5. If the claimed domain is yours but the check failed, the message is a candidate.
  6. Volume and recipient count decide whether the analyst sees an alert or only a report entry.

Real-world example

Think of a bank clerk checking a withdrawal slip. The signature may look perfect, but the clerk also checks the account number against the identification card. If the slip says one account and the card says another, the clerk stops. The slip is the email header. The mismatch is the reason to stop and ask a question.

Key takeaways

  • Spoofed mail usually passes a quick visual check, so read the headers instead.
  • Authentication failures alone are normal for third party senders and mailing lists.
  • Combine a failed check, a claimed internal domain and a high recipient count.

In more detail

This strategy detects email that claims to come from your organisation or from a trusted partner but fails sender authentication or arrives from an unauthorised sending source. It matters because a spoofed message is the first step in invoice fraud, credential phishing and executive impersonation, and because the message body itself is often clean, so the headers are the only reliable evidence. Detection depends on mail gateway and email security telemetry that records authentication results, envelope and header sender addresses, reply to addresses and any domain alignment mismatch. The main way it fails in practice is third party senders and mailing lists, which legitimately fail strict authentication checks, so a rule that alerts on every failure produces a constant low value stream and the real spoof is lost in it.

What you will practise

  • Explain how sender authentication checks such as SPF, DKIM and DMARC prove message origin.
  • Identify the header fields that record authentication results and the envelope sender.
  • Recognise a display name that claims an internal identity while the domain does not match.
  • Separate third party sending services and mailing lists from true spoofing attempts.
  • Use recipient count and message volume to grade the severity of a spoofing campaign.
  • Describe how forwarding breaks authentication and why that must be baselined.
