T1071.003 Application Layer Protocol: Mail Protocols

T1071.003Command and ControlExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This is when malware uses email protocols to receive orders and to send back results. Email protocols are the rules used to send and to read mail. The attacker hides their instructions inside email messages.

Why should you care?

Almost every network allows email traffic. A command channel that looks like email can pass through filters that would stop other traffic. It also works slowly and quietly, which makes it hard to spot by size or speed.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker creates a mailbox on a mail service, or takes over a mailbox inside your company.
  2. Malware on a victim computer uses a mail protocol to log in and check that mailbox for new messages.
  3. The instruction is written inside the message, sometimes in the body, the subject, or a hidden header.
  4. The malware carries out the instruction and writes the result into another message that it sends out.
  5. Traffic is encrypted in most modern setups, so the content cannot be read by a network filter.
  6. The mailbox owner looks like a normal user, so the activity continues until someone notices the account.

Real-world example

Think of a post office box that nobody uses for real letters. One person drops instructions into the box each night, and another person collects them and leaves a reply. The post office sees normal mail going in and out of a normal box.

Key takeaways

  • Mail is trusted by design, so a mail channel can survive filtering that stops other protocols.
  • Check whether a device is reading a mailbox that no person in your company owns or uses.
  • Mail flow logs on the server often show a sender that never passed through the approved relay.

In more detail

Mail protocols as a command channel work because every business network already carries electronic mail, and mail servers are expected to accept connections from inside the network and to send traffic outward. Within the parent technique of application layer protocol abuse, this sub-technique covers the mail delivery and retrieval protocols: Simple Mail Transfer Protocol (SMTP) and its encrypted form for sending, and Post Office Protocol version 3 (POP3) or Internet Message Access Protocol (IMAP) for reading. An implant can use a mailbox that the attacker controls as a relay, embedding commands and results in message bodies, subject lines, custom header fields, or attachments, or it can reach a compromised internal server so that the conversation never leaves the organisation. Traffic is well formed by design and often encrypted, so content based inspection is weak. The stronger discriminators are contextual and infrastructural: an internal workstation or server sending mail directly to an external address without passing through the approved relay, a client polling a mailbox provider that no business unit uses, mailbox protocols in use by a device or service account that has no human owner, very regular connection intervals or message sizes, and mail flow records or proxy logs that show an unusual internal sender reaching an external mail destination.
