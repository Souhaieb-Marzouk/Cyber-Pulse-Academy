G0080 Cobalt Group

G0080Enterprise GroupExtremeThreat Group

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

The Cobalt Group is a criminal gang that used computers to steal money from banks. Public reports describe them breaking into bank networks and then reaching the systems that control cash machines. They often started with fake emails carrying harmful documents. They used the same tools that security testers use, so their activity is hard to separate from normal testing work.

Why should you care?

This group shows how far one stolen mailbox can lead. The attackers did not stop at the first computer, they moved step by step toward the money. If office computers can talk directly to payment systems, that path is open to them. Separating networks and watching for unexpected traffic between them breaks the chain.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Staff receive an email that looks like a normal business message with a document.
  2. Opening the document starts harmful code on an ordinary office computer used by that person.
  3. The intruder then uses common testing tools to explore the network and find servers.
  4. Passwords and saved sign-in details are quietly copied from the machines they reach.
  5. They then move toward the servers and services that control cash dispensing.
  6. Money is withdrawn from cash machines or transferred into accounts that criminals control.

Real-world example

Picture a hotel where guests can walk from the lobby straight into the cash office. The connecting door was left unlocked. A thief in the lobby needs only that one door. Once inside, he has time to read the safe code and take whatever he wants. Good security keeps guests out of the cash office even when they have already walked past the front desk.

Key takeaways

  • One compromised mailbox can open a path to your most valuable systems.
  • Keep office networks separate from payment and control networks so the path ends.
  • Watch how administrative tools are used, not only whether they exist on a machine.

In more detail

The Cobalt Group, also tracked as Cobalt Gang and Cobalt Spider, is a financially motivated criminal operation that public reporting links to large scale intrusions of banks and payment networks with coordinated cash withdrawals from automated teller machines, with the most documented activity between about 2016 and 2018. The intrusion pattern typically began with spear phishing carrying a document macro or a script based loader, followed by the use of commercial penetration testing tooling and custom implants for credential theft, reconnaissance and lateral movement toward internal banking systems. Public reporting described unusual attention to the machines and services that control cash dispensing, and later reporting on related criminal crews showed similar tooling reused for file encrypting extortion. Analysis also noted the group's habit of observing an environment for some time before acting, which makes early detection unusually valuable. Evidence in a network includes macro or script execution from mail attachments, penetration testing tools running on user workstations, credential dumping behaviour and tool artefacts on disk, authentication from administrative accounts at unexpected hosts, and configuration or software changes on payment processing servers. The common misunderstandings are that bank intrusions require exotic techniques, when the initial steps are ordinary phishing and unpatched externally facing services, and that endpoint detection alone is sufficient when the decisive control is network segmentation between office, corporate and transaction environments, backed by monitoring of the paths that are supposed to be closed.
