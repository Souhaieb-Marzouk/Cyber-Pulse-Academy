Pretexting

Security AwarenessHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Pretexting is when a person tells you a made up story to get information or access. The story is called a pretext. The caller may pretend to be from your IT team, a supplier or a bank. The aim is to sound so normal that you do not check.

Why should you care?

A confident story can beat a technical control, because the request looks ordinary. Small details gathered over several calls can unlock a bigger request later. The harm may include lost money, leaked records or an intruder inside the network. Technology cannot spot a friendly voice, so people and process must do that work.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker studies the target to learn names, roles, suppliers and everyday language.
  2. They choose a role that gives a natural reason to ask for something, such as a helpdesk engineer.
  3. They make contact, often claiming urgency or a problem that must be fixed right now.
  4. They ask for a small thing first, such as a detail that seems harmless to share.
  5. The detail is used to sound credible when they ask for the real request.
  6. Defences work by verifying identity through a separate, known channel before you act.

Real-world example

Think of a hotel reception desk. A caller says he is a guest's assistant and left a key card in room four one two. He asks the receptionist to read out the room number on the card so he can describe it. The receptionist has a rule: never confirm guest details to an unknown caller. Instead she says she will pass the message to the guest. The rule protects the guest even though the caller sounded polite and busy.

Key takeaways

  • Verify identity through a channel you already trust, not one the caller gives you.
  • Treat small details as valuable, because they build a bigger request later.
  • Praise staff who question odd requests so that checking feels normal.

In more detail

Pretexting is a social engineering technique in which the attacker invents a believable situation, or pretext, to make a target release information, access or a favour. The pretext supplies three things: a role the caller claims, a reason the request is routine, and pressure that makes refusal feel rude or risky. It leans on authority, urgency, familiarity and the human wish to be helpful, and it is often combined with email or voice to harvest small details such as an internal extension, a supplier name or a ticket reference that make a later request credible. Pretexting is hard to defend with technology because the request arrives through legitimate channels and asks for something the target is entitled to do. The effective controls are procedural rather than technical: independent verification of identity through a known channel, a callback to a number from the directory rather than one supplied by the caller, cautious handling of information that seems harmless, and a culture where challenging an unusual request is praised. The mistake defenders make is assuming only gullible people fall for a pretext; experienced staff are targeted precisely because they hold useful access, and a good pretext exploits process gaps rather than a person's intelligence.

What you will practise

  • Define pretexting and explain how it differs from a generic phishing email.
  • Identify the psychological levers a pretext uses, such as authority, urgency and helpfulness.
  • Explain how small pieces of information support a later, larger request.
  • Describe procedural defences including callback verification through a known number.
  • Explain why technical controls alone cannot stop a convincing pretext.
  • Recognise the information your own role exposes and how it could be misused.
