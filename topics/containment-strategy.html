Containment Strategy

Incident Response & ForensicsHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Containment means stopping an attack from spreading further while you work out what happened. It is the moment you decide to cut a network link, disable an account, or switch a system off. The choice is a balance between speed and safety. Contain it too slowly and the attacker moves on. Contain it too roughly and you destroy the clues you need.

Why should you care?

Every minute without containment gives an attacker more time to copy data or create new ways in. But a rushed shutdown can wipe the memory that shows how the attacker entered. It can also warn the attacker, who then hides deeper or destroys evidence. Bad containment can also stop a hospital or a payment system, which harms real people. A planned strategy lets you choose the smallest action that stops the spread.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Decide in advance which actions are allowed for each scenario, and who may approve them out of hours.
  2. Confirm the scope first, so you know which accounts, machines and networks are already affected.
  3. Choose the lightest action that breaks the attacker's path, such as blocking traffic to one address.
  4. Keep the machine powered on and copy its memory and logs before you rebuild anything.
  5. Watch for a second path in, because attackers often keep a backup login or a scheduled job.
  6. Move to long term containment while you build clean replacements, then return to normal service in stages.

Real-world example

Imagine a burst pipe flooding one flat in a large building. You do not demolish the building. First you close the valve for that flat, which stops the water but keeps the lights on. You take photographs of the damage before clearing anything away. Then you check the floor below to see whether water already reached it. Containment in security follows the same careful steps. You close the smallest valve that stops the spread, save the evidence, and check where the damage already reached.

Key takeaways

  • Containment is a decision with trade-offs, so agree the approved actions before an incident starts.
  • Preserve memory and logs before switching anything off, because powering down destroys the best evidence.
  • Check for a second entry path, since attackers often leave more than one way back in.

In more detail

Containment strategy is the deliberate plan for stopping the spread of an incident while preserving evidence, intelligence value and business function. It sits between detection and eradication in the incident response lifecycle, and is normally split into short-term containment, which buys time with the least disruptive action available, and long-term containment, which stabilises the environment until clean systems can be rebuilt. The core tension is that the fastest actions are often the most destructive. Powering off a host stops the attacker but erases memory and may trigger destructive fallback behaviour, while aggressive blocking can alert the adversary and cause them to burn or hide their access. Practitioners therefore scope the incident first, prefer the smallest action that breaks the observed access path, and hunt for secondary persistence such as additional credentials, scheduled tasks and trust relationships. The common mistake is containing one host while the identity that granted access remains valid, which simply moves the attacker to another system.

What you will practise

  • Explain what containment means within the incident response lifecycle.
  • Describe the trade-offs between speed, evidence preservation and business continuity.
  • List common containment actions and the situations where each one is appropriate.
  • Explain why a full shutdown can harm an investigation.
  • Outline how a team moves from short term containment into recovery.
