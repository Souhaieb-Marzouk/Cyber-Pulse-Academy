Purple Teaming

SOC & Detection EngineeringHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Purple teaming is when the attack testers and the defence team work together on the same exercise. One side tries a technique, and the other side checks whether it was seen and understood. They fix the gaps immediately, while they are still together. The word purple comes from mixing red and blue.

Why should you care?

Attack tests often end with a report, and the fixes wait for months. Defenders may never learn whether their tools would have caught the technique. Working together turns a finding into a working alert the same day. It also builds trust between two teams that usually meet only through reports.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The two teams agree the goal, the rules and the small set of techniques to test.
  2. The attack side runs one technique in a controlled way, with the defenders watching.
  3. The defence side checks whether the data it collects recorded the activity.
  4. They confirm together whether an alert appeared and whether it was clear enough to act on.
  5. They write down the gap and one of them fixes the detection before moving on.
  6. The team repeats the cycle with the next technique and records what improved.

Real-world example

Imagine a fire drill run with the firefighters standing in the building. They light a safe test fire in a metal tray. The alarm should ring, the staff should move, and the control room should see which sensor triggered. If the alarm stays silent, the technicians fix the sensor before anyone goes home. Nobody waits for a report three months later.

Key takeaways

  • Defenders should be present and hands on, not just briefed afterwards.
  • Always record the technique, the data source and the detection result together.
  • Small repeated cycles improve detection more than one large yearly event.

In more detail

Purple teaming is collaborative adversary emulation in which offensive and defensive staff work the same scenario together, side by side or in tight cycles, so that each attack technique is tested against live telemetry and each detection gap is closed while the people who found it are still in the room. The colour term is descriptive rather than a formal standard: red team work emulates an adversary, blue team work defends and detects, and purple describes the joint activity that joins the two. A typical purple exercise is small, repeatable and instrumented. A technique is selected, often mapped to a framework such as MITRE ATT&CK, the red side performs it under agreed rules, the blue side confirms whether the expected telemetry was collected, whether a detection fired, and whether an analyst could act on it, and the result is recorded as technique, expected data source, observed outcome and the gap between them. That record is what feeds detection engineering, so success is measured in detections created or tuned rather than in systems accessed. The mistake organisations make is running purple teaming as an annual presentation in which the red team narrates what it did last quarter; without hands on keyboard time for defenders, no detection changes, and the exercise becomes a briefing. A second mistake is treating purple teaming as a replacement for independent red teaming or a mature blue team, when it depends on both and works best as continuous small cycles.

What you will practise

  • Explain what purple teaming is and how it relates to red team and blue team work.
  • Describe a repeatable cycle for running a purple team exercise on a single technique.
  • Explain how techniques are mapped to a framework such as MITRE ATT&CK during planning.
  • Describe the record kept for each test case, including expected telemetry and observed outcome.
  • Explain why detection improvements, rather than systems accessed, measure success.
  • Recognise the failure mode of turning purple teaming into a retrospective briefing.
