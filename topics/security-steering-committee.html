Security Steering Committee

GRC & ComplianceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A security steering committee is a regular meeting for senior people. The group decides how much risk the company will accept. It also decides who will pay to reduce that risk. Members come from business, finance, legal and security. They meet on a fixed schedule and write down every decision.

Why should you care?

Security work competes with every other project for money and time. Without one meeting to settle those choices, teams argue by email for weeks. Decisions then get made by whoever asks the loudest. A committee gives each risk a named owner and a date. It also leaves a record that auditors can read later.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The company writes a charter that states what the committee may decide and who must attend.
  2. Members bring items from the risk register (the list of known risks), such as gaps and audit findings.
  3. Each item is presented with its business impact, the proposed treatment and the cost of that treatment.
  4. The committee then chooses one option: accept the risk, reduce it, transfer it or stop the activity.
  5. Every choice is written in the minutes (the official record) with an owner, a due date and a review point.
  6. At the next meeting the group checks whether the agreed actions actually happened.

Real-world example

Imagine a family that wants to repair an old house. Everyone has a wish list: a new roof, a better kitchen, a safer staircase. There is only one pot of money for all of it. So the family sits down on the first Sunday of every month. They list the dangers first, then agree what to fix now and what to leave for later. They write the choices on the fridge door so that nobody forgets them. Six months later they can see what was done and what was quietly skipped.

Key takeaways

  • A committee is useful only when it is allowed to say no to a project.
  • Write down who owns each risk and when the group will review it.
  • Bring costs and business impact, not only technical findings.

In more detail

A security steering committee is the standing governance forum where an organisation makes the security decisions that cannot legitimately be settled inside the security function: funding a control, accepting a residual risk, approving an exception to policy, or agreeing that a project will ship with a known weakness. The committee is defined less by who attends than by its decision rights. A written charter should state what the group may approve, what it must escalate to the board, who counts toward quorum, and how often it meets. The useful work happens through a small number of mechanisms: a risk register reviewed against a stated risk appetite, control testing and audit findings, an exception and risk-acceptance process with expiry dates, incident and third-party risk reporting, and a forward agenda tied to regulatory or contractual deadlines. Outputs are minutes that record a decision, a named owner, a due date and a review point, because an undated action is only an intention. The recognised failure mode is a meeting that becomes a presentation slot where security reports activity and nobody commits resources. Other common weaknesses are membership without authority, an agenda disconnected from the risk register, risk acceptances that never expire, and no feedback loop to confirm that agreed actions were completed. When information flows in but no decision flows out, the committee has become a briefing, and the choices it should have made are taken quietly elsewhere by whoever is willing to carry the risk unexamined.

What you will practise

  • Explain what a security steering committee decides, and how those decisions differ from operational incident response.
  • Describe how a charter, quorum and escalation path give the committee authority that it can defend.
  • Explain how a risk register and a stated risk appetite determine which items reach the agenda.
  • Describe how exceptions and risk acceptances are approved with an owner, an expiry date and a compensating control.
  • Identify the signs of a committee that has become a briefing rather than a decision-making body.
  • Draft a decision record that captures the option chosen, the residual risk and the date of the next review.
