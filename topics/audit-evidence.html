Audit Evidence

GRC & ComplianceHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Audit evidence is the proof you show an auditor that a control works. A control is a rule or a tool that reduces risk. The evidence may be a log, a screenshot, a ticket or a signed approval. The auditor reads it and decides whether your claim is true.

Why should you care?

An audit is a test of trust, not a memory game. A good control with no evidence looks the same as no control at all. Evidence should come from the system, not from a person's summary. Weak evidence leads to repeat findings and extra work next year. Good evidence also helps you fix problems faster.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You write down the control and how it is meant to work.
  2. You find the place where a system records that the control ran.
  3. You collect records for the whole period that the auditor asks about.
  4. Each record shows a date, a source and the person responsible for it.
  5. You explain any gaps and show what was done to correct them.
  6. You store the evidence where it cannot change without leaving a trace.

Real-world example

Think of a hospital night desk that must check every ward each hour. A manager later asks how the checks went last month. A note saying that all was fine is not enough. The desk shows the signed log, with a time beside each round. The log is the evidence, and the friendly note is only a claim.

Key takeaways

  • Collect evidence while the control runs, not the week before an audit.
  • Prefer system generated records, because they are harder to argue with.
  • Keep a simple index that maps each control to its evidence and owner.

In more detail

Audit evidence is the recorded information an auditor examines to test whether a control exists and operated as described throughout the period under review. Useful evidence must be relevant to the specific control, sufficient in quantity to support a conclusion, and reliable, which in practice means produced by the system rather than assembled by the person being assessed. Common types are configuration exports, system generated logs with trustworthy timestamps, tickets and approval records, access review attestations, training records, change history, screenshots with context, and interview notes. Because most frameworks and standards, including common ones such as PCI DSS and ISO 27001, expect demonstration of operation over a period rather than at a moment, a single screenshot is weak: the auditor will ask who produced the record, when, from which system, and whether it could have been altered afterwards. The mistake teams make is assembling everything in the final week, which produces undated images, unexplained gaps and no trail for the corrective actions that were promised last year.

What you will practise

  • Explain what audit evidence is and what makes it relevant, sufficient and reliable.
  • Distinguish system generated evidence from a written summary or assurance.
  • Describe how to cover a review period rather than a single point in time.
  • List the common evidence types used for access, change and monitoring controls.
  • Explain how gaps and corrective actions should be documented and tracked.
