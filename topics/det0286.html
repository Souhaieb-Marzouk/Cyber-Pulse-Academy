DET0286 Detection Strategy for Impersonation

DET0286Detection StrategyVery hardDetection Strategy

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Impersonation means an attacker pretends to be someone you trust. They may copy a colleague name, a company brand, or the support desk. The goal is to make you act without stopping to check. You may open a file, approve a payment, or hand over a password. This detection looks for signs that the sender is not who they claim to be.

Why should you care?

The message or the call carries no harmful file, so virus scanning will not help you here. The target is a human decision, and one successful call can cost more than many technical intrusions. Attackers reuse the same tricks many times, so real patterns do exist in the data. Catching the fake identity early protects both your money and your accounts.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The mail gateway stores a record of each message it handles. The record holds the sender address, the display name, the reply to address and the authentication results.
  2. The rule compares the display name and the domain with your trusted list. That list holds internal people, brands and partner organisations.
  3. It looks for failures in the sender authentication checks. Those checks are meant to prove a message came from the domain it claims.
  4. A domain feed tells you whether the sending domain was registered very recently or closely copies a trusted name.
  5. Sign in records are checked for a browser login to a sensitive application from an unfamiliar place.
  6. The service desk queue is checked for a password or multi factor reset that matches the same name or mailbox.

Real-world example

Imagine a stranger phones your reception desk and says they are from head office. They know your manager's name, and they use all the right words. Reception cannot see a face through a phone, so it checks other things. Did the call come from the usual number, and does the request match the normal process? When several answers are wrong, reception calls back on a number the company already trusts.

Key takeaways

  • You stop impersonation by checking identity, not by scanning a message for harmful files.
  • One weak signal means very little, so combine several before you raise an alert.
  • Always confirm unusual requests through a channel you already trust.

In more detail

This strategy detects impersonation, where an adversary presents itself as a trusted person, brand, or internal system in order to obtain access, credentials, or payment. Because the identity being imitated is genuine, detection cannot rely on hostile content; it relies on inconsistencies in the presentation itself. The telemetry that makes this possible is mail gateway records carrying envelope sender, display name, reply to address and authentication results, domain registration or passive DNS feeds showing freshly created lookalike names, identity provider sign in logs carrying application and client agent details, and service desk records for account recovery. It fails in practice because a well written message from a first time external sender is unremarkable on its own, so the rule has to score several weak signals together rather than alert on any single one.

What you will practise

  • Describe how impersonation differs from a technical account compromise.
  • Read mail authentication results and explain what a failure does and does not prove.
  • Correlate lookalike domain data with inbound mail and identity sign in records.
  • Recognise account recovery requests that belong to an impersonation campaign.
  • Design a scoring rule that combines weak signals into a single ranked alert.
