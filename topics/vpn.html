VPN

Network SecurityVery hardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A virtual private network, or VPN, builds an encrypted tunnel between your device and another network. Traffic inside the tunnel cannot be read or changed by anyone in between. Remote-access VPNs let one person connect to the office, while site-to-site VPNs join two networks, such as a branch and head office, as if they were neighbours.

Why should you care?

People work from cafes, homes and airports on networks you do not control. A VPN protects their traffic from anyone watching that network. It also lets them reach internal systems without exposing those systems to the internet. But a VPN is not a magic shield. Once connected, the device is usually on your internal network with wide access, so a compromised laptop can spread trouble. Treat the VPN as a door, not a guarantee.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The user's device and the VPN gateway agree on keys, which are secret values used for encryption.
  2. Both sides prove who they are, so you cannot tunnel into a fake gateway.
  3. Traffic is wrapped in encryption and sent across the internet to the gateway.
  4. The gateway unwraps it, checks the user's permissions and forwards it internally.
  5. Replies travel back through the same tunnel, so the session stays protected.
  6. If split tunnelling is on, other traffic goes straight to the internet, outside the tunnel.

Real-world example

Think of an armoured van carrying parcels through a busy city. Nobody on the street can open the doors or swap the contents. When the van arrives, the depot checks the driver's badge before accepting the delivery. The protection covers the journey, not the depot. If the driver then has keys to every room in the building, the van has protected the parcels and handed over the whole site anyway.

Key takeaways

  • A VPN protects traffic in transit, not the device at either end.
  • Require multi-factor authentication, because gateways face constant password attacks.
  • Give each user only the internal access their role needs.

In more detail

A virtual private network establishes an encrypted tunnel between endpoints or gateways so that traffic crossing an untrusted network cannot be read or modified in transit, and so that the endpoints can be treated as members of a logical private network. Site-to-site tunnels join branch offices or cloud networks using IPsec or comparable protocols, while remote-access VPNs connect individual users through a concentrator that authenticates them and places them on an internal subnet. The security properties come from the cryptographic handshake, mutual authentication of peers, and integrity protection of each packet, but the trust model is the weak point. A remote-access VPN that grants full internal reachability reproduces the flat network for every connected device, so a compromised laptop inherits the same access as a workstation on site. Split tunnelling compounds the problem by allowing the endpoint to talk directly to the internet while holding an internal tunnel, which turns the endpoint into a bridge between the two. Operationally, VPN concentrators concentrate risk. They are internet-facing, they authenticate users, and they are frequently targeted by credential stuffing, password spraying and exploitation of unpatched appliances, so they must be patched aggressively, protected with phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, and monitored for unusual session behaviour such as logins from unexpected geographies or impossible travel. Capacity planning matters because a saturated concentrator becomes an availability incident. Many organisations are reducing reliance on broad network-level access in favour of application-level access brokers that authorise a specific service rather than placing a device on the internal network. Where VPNs remain, good practice includes per-user authorisation profiles, session timeouts, device posture checks before access is granted, and complete logging of tunnel establishment and termination.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to explain what a VPN does and does not protect once a device is connected.
  • You will be able to compare site-to-site and remote-access designs and their trust assumptions.
  • You will be able to assess split tunnelling and full tunnelling against endpoint risk.
  • You will be able to triage VPN authentication logs for spraying, stuffing and anomalous session behaviour.
  • You will be able to harden a concentrator with multi-factor authentication, patching discipline and per-user profiles.
  • You will be able to justify moving sensitive access from network-level tunnels to application-level brokers.
