DET0075 Internal Proxy Behavior via Lateral Host-to-Host C2 Relay

DET0075Detection StrategyVery hardDetection Strategy

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A relay is a middle computer that passes traffic between two sides. It does not look unusual to either side. This detection looks for an internal machine that takes orders from another internal machine. It then looks for that machine sending the results out to the internet.

Why should you care?

Most companies watch what leaves the network edge. They see the relay computer, which looks like an ordinary worker. They do not see where the orders came from. The real controller stays hidden behind the relay. Finding the relay is one of the few ways to map the whole path.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The firewall writes a record for each connection. It includes both addresses, the port and the duration.
  2. Flow records add the amount of data sent in each direction.
  3. The detection groups traffic by internal address. It marks who started the connection.
  4. It flags any address that does both, towards peers and towards the internet.
  5. Further scoring uses the shape of the traffic, such as steady timing.
  6. Internal machines that are expected to accept connections, such as servers, are excluded.

Real-world example

Think of a school office where every visitor signs in at the front desk. One student starts carrying notes between two classrooms. The same student also posts letters for both of them. The front desk only sees a student walking out with post. It never sees the teacher who wrote the letters, because that teacher never leaves the building.

Key takeaways

  • A relay hides the real controller from anyone watching only the network edge.
  • The clue is role mixing, where one machine is both a client and a server.
  • Know which internal hosts may accept connections before you alert.

In more detail

This detection strategy looks for an internal host that has been turned into a relay, receiving commands from another internal system and forwarding them outward so that the true controller never touches the monitored perimeter. It matters because the perimeter already sees the relay host egress, and one more innocuous looking internal hop hides the origin of the traffic from both the firewall and the proxy. The telemetry that makes the pattern visible is firewall session records and network flow exports that show a workstation both accepting connections from peers and holding long lived outbound sessions, together with connection duration and byte symmetry that differ from ordinary client traffic. The distinguishing feature is role mixing: the same internal address behaves like a server towards other internal hosts and like a client towards the outside, often on the same port and often with regular timing. It fails in practice because internal traffic is frequently unlogged, encrypted and full of legitimate peer relationships, so the rule detects the shape of the traffic rather than its content and needs a strong baseline of which hosts are allowed to accept connections at all.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a host to host relay conceals the origin of command and control traffic from perimeter monitoring.
  • Interpret flow and session records to determine which internal host initiated or accepted a connection.
  • Detect role mixing where one internal address serves peers and also egresses to untrusted destinations.
  • Build a baseline of internal hosts that are permitted to accept inbound connections.
  • Describe how to trace a suspected relay to its controller and to any victim hosts it serves.
