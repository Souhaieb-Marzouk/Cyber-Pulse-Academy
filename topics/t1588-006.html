T1588.006 Obtain Capabilities: Vulnerabilities

T1588.006Resource DevelopmentExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A vulnerability is a weakness in software or hardware that an attacker could abuse. This technique is about collecting information about such weaknesses. Attackers study them before they try to use them.

Why should you care?

You cannot defend what you do not know about. Attackers read the same public advisories that you do, often sooner. If your updates and asset lists are slow, you give them free time to plan and test.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker gathers details about the software that your organisation probably uses.
  2. They read the public vulnerability databases, the vendor notices, and the security research.
  3. They also look for private sources, such as broker research or a closed vulnerability database.
  4. They compare the weakness with the versions they believe you are running.
  5. They decide whether to find an existing exploit or build one themselves.
  6. Defenders can use the same information to prioritise patching and exposure checks.

Real-world example

Picture a neighbourhood watch group. One member reads the council newsletter and learns that a certain gate latch is faulty. The burglar reads the same newsletter. The difference is that the watch group can fix every gate this week, while the burglar can only pick the ones still unfixed.

Key takeaways

  • Vulnerability information is shared, so speed matters more than secrecy.
  • Balance vulnerability information against a real inventory of your assets.
  • The gap between vendor disclosure and your patch date is the exposure window.

In more detail

This sub-technique is about acquiring knowledge of a vulnerability rather than acquiring working exploit code. The object of the behaviour is information: which weakness exists, in which product and version, how severe it is, and whether a fix exists. Adversaries collect it from public databases, vendor advisories, mailing lists, conference material, and patch diffs, and they also pursue closed sources such as private vulnerability databases, bug bounty submissions, broker research, and the systems of research organisations. The distinction from the exploit sub-technique matters because knowing a weakness is a precondition, not a capability: the adversary may then search for an existing exploit or attempt to build one. Defenders have a structural advantage here, because the same information flows to them. Public vulnerability identifiers, vendor advisories, and severity scoring let a defender decide what to patch first. The adversary's advantage is timing. There is normally a gap between private discovery and public disclosure, and a further gap between disclosure and enterprise patching. Whoever closes that second gap first wins: an adversary who knows about a weak internet-facing service can test it for weeks, while an organisation that has not inventoried that service does not even know it is exposed. Continuous exposure management, not the vulnerability feed itself, is what changes the outcome.

What you will practise

  • Define a vulnerability and explain how it differs from an exploit.
  • Describe the public and private sources of vulnerability information.
  • Explain the disclosure timeline from private discovery to enterprise patching.
  • Explain how knowledge of a vulnerability leads to obtaining or developing an exploit.
  • Describe how asset inventory and prioritisation reduce vulnerability exposure.
