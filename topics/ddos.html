Distributed Denial of Service

Network SecurityVery hardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

A distributed denial of service attack, or DDoS, is an attempt to knock a service offline by flooding it with traffic from many machines at once. The word distributed matters, because the requests come from thousands of places. You cannot simply block one address and make it stop.

Why should you care?

Any service that people depend on can be pushed offline, from a shop to a hospital booking system. Attackers may also use a flood as a distraction while they steal data somewhere else. Without preparation, your team will panic and guess. With a written plan and upstream help, the same attack becomes a routine event you can absorb.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Attackers build a botnet, which is a large group of infected computers they can command remotely.
  2. They point all those machines at one target and send far more traffic than it can handle.
  3. Some floods fill the network link; others exhaust connection tables or slow application logic.
  4. Real users then cannot connect, so the service looks broken even though nothing was hacked.
  5. Your provider diverts traffic through a scrubbing centre, which filters the flood and passes good traffic.
  6. You also rate-limit and cache at the application, then review what happened afterwards.

Real-world example

Picture a small shop with one door. Ten customers can come and go comfortably. Now imagine a thousand people crowded into the doorway, not buying anything, just standing there. Real customers cannot get in, and the shop loses money without anything being stolen. That is a flood attack. If you hired a bigger doorway and a doorman who only lets genuine shoppers through, you would be doing what a scrubbing service does.

Key takeaways

  • DDoS means many machines flooding one service until real users cannot get in.
  • You cannot absorb a large flood locally; upstream filtering does the heavy lifting.
  • Write and rehearse the plan before the attack, not during it.

In more detail

A distributed denial of service attack attempts to make a service unavailable by exhausting a resource, using many sources so that no single host can simply be blocked. Volume-based attacks saturate network links with floods of packets or bytes. Protocol attacks consume state on network devices or servers, for example by exhausting connection tables or abusing a handshake so that half-open sessions accumulate. Application-layer attacks send requests that look individually legitimate but are expensive to serve, such as repeated searches, login attempts or interface calls that trigger heavy database work, so modest traffic volumes can still cause outages. Modern campaigns are frequently multi-vector and short, blending floods with application requests to defeat single-layer defences, and they are sometimes paired with extortion, used as distraction from another intrusion, or used as cover for data theft. Mitigation is layered and depends on where capacity actually exists. Upstream scrubbing services and content delivery networks absorb volumetric traffic and filter close to the source, which is essential because local links are usually far smaller than the attack. On-premises appliances handle smaller floods and can act quickly, but they cannot exceed the size of the circuits they protect. Application defences include caching, rate limiting per identity rather than per address, restricting expensive endpoints, and requiring a challenge for suspicious clients. Preparation matters more than any single tool: a written runbook that names who can enable scrubbing, how traffic is rerouted and how providers are contacted turns a panic into a procedure. Monitoring should include baseline traffic profiles, alerts on abnormal connection counts and response latency, and periodic testing of failover and mitigation paths, while post-incident review should separate genuine capacity failures from outages caused by a mitigation that blocked legitimate users.

What you will practise

  • You will be able to classify volumetric, protocol and application-layer attacks and the resource each exhausts.
  • You will be able to baseline normal traffic and recognise the onset of an attack from monitoring data.
  • You will be able to design layered mitigation across upstream scrubbing, local appliances and application controls.
  • You will be able to write and rehearse a mitigation runbook with clear decision rights and provider contacts.
  • You will be able to distinguish a real capacity failure from an outage caused by over-blocking.
  • You will be able to rank multi-vector attack scenarios by business impact for a given service.
