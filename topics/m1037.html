M1037 Filter Network Traffic

M1037Enterprise MitigationVery hardMitigation

Filtering network traffic is the practice of permitting only the protocols, destinations, ports, and services an organisation has explicitly justified and denying everything else at control points the defender owns. In production it is never one device but a stack of them: perimeter and internal firewalls enforcing zone-to-zone policy, web proxies and secure web gateways applying category and reputation decisions, recursive resolvers with policy zones that block or sinkhole unwanted domains, cloud security groups and network ACLs, Kubernetes network policies and service mesh authorisation, and host firewalls that drop traffic before it reaches the wire. The engineering reality is that filtering is a change-management problem at least as much as a technical one. Rules accumulate, business owners resist removals, emergency openings become permanent, and a single any-any rule quietly rebuilds the flat network that segmentation was meant to replace. Mature teams therefore keep rule ownership metadata, review rulesets on a schedule, use hit counters to retire rules that never match, and ship denied traffic into detection pipelines instead of leaving it in a device buffer. The control interrupts command-and-control over common application-layer protocols by forcing beacons toward destinations the organisation does not permit, and it makes DNS-based tunnelling visible and blockable by channelling all resolution through controlled resolvers. It raises the cost of exfiltration over C2 channels and public web services, constrains remote service abuse such as unrestricted RDP or SMB across zones, and removes reachable services that opportunistic exploitation depends on. Its limitations are honest: inspection degrades against encrypted traffic, attackers ride allowed SaaS and cloud endpoints, domain fronting and fast-changing infrastructure defeat static allow-lists, and a filter that generates constant false positives will eventually be bypassed by the operators it inconveniences.

What you will practise

  • Explain how traffic filtering policy is enforced at perimeter, internal, cloud, cluster, and host layers, and where each layer's blind spots sit.
  • Design an egress allow-list that constrains command-and-control over web protocols and DNS without breaking legitimate business traffic.
  • Triage firewall, proxy, and resolver deny logs for beaconing, tunnelling, and exfiltration patterns rather than dismissing them as noise.
  • Identify rule sprawl, shadowed rules, and unowned any-any entries during a review of an existing ruleset.
  • Assess how TLS inspection, domain fronting, and permitted SaaS traffic limit what network filtering can actually observe.
  • Justify where inline blocking is worth the availability risk and where a monitor-only position is the defensible choice.
