T1590.001 Gather Victim Network Information: Domain Properties

T1590.001ReconnaissanceExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

This is a smaller part of network information gathering. It focuses on the registered domain names an organisation owns and the details attached to them. Those details include the registrar, the contact addresses, and the name servers that answer for the domain.

Why should you care?

Domain records are easy to look up and often leak useful facts. A public contact email can be used to guess other email addresses in your organisation. Name servers may point to hosting you had forgotten about. Attackers also use cloud discovery services. These check whether your domain exists in a hosted email platform before an attack is planned.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker looks up your domain in a public registration directory to read the listed administrative details.
  2. They note the registrar, the expiry date, and the name servers that answer for your domain.
  3. They collect any contact emails, phone numbers, or postal addresses left in those public records.
  4. They query cloud discovery endpoints, such as autodiscover, to test whether your domain uses a hosted email service.
  5. They compare your current domain list with older records to find names you stopped using but still own.
  6. They use all of it to build a picture of your organisation before sending any email or probe.

Real-world example

Imagine a shared flat with six residents. The mailbox outside carries one label with the building name, and the post inside is sorted by flat number. A stranger who wants to reach one resident studies the mailbox labels and the names on old delivery notes. They learn who handles the post and which flat receives the official letters. All of that is visible from the street, and nobody has knocked on the door yet. Domain records work like that mailbox label. They show who is responsible for the name and where messages should go. Only the delivery attempts themselves appear in your logs.

Key takeaways

  • Public domain records are visible to everyone, so treat any contact detail you list there as public information.
  • Review old domain registrations regularly and remove details you no longer need to publish.
  • Discovery requests in your logs show who is checking whether your domain exists in a hosted service.

In more detail

This sub-technique narrows the parent activity to the registration and administrative facts attached to a victim's domain names, rather than the network itself. Adversaries pull registrar and registrant detail, expiry dates, name servers, and the contact email addresses, phone numbers, and postal addresses left in public registration records. Because third-party cloud identity providers expose discovery endpoints, a request naming a domain can also confirm whether that organisation is present in a hosted service, which is a distinctly modern signal that pure registration lookups do not produce. Almost none of this appears in victim telemetry, and the small part that does shows up as ordinary-looking queries to well-known cloud endpoints. Detection is therefore weak and heavily dependent on baselining, because mail clients, mobile devices, and provisioning tools perform the same discovery requests constantly; the useful discriminators are source diversity, query volume against a single domain, and the timing relative to later authentication attempts.

What you will practise

  • Explain what domain property gathering collects and how it differs from wider network information gathering.
  • Identify the public registration fields that most often expose useful detail to an attacker.
  • Describe how cloud identity discovery endpoints can confirm that a domain exists in a hosted service.
  • Recognise the small telemetry footprint this activity leaves and explain why baselining is necessary.
  • Recommend which registration details an organisation should review or remove.
