T1558 Steal or Forge Kerberos Tickets

T1558Credential AccessExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Kerberos is the system Windows uses to prove who you are inside a company network. Instead of sending your password everywhere, it gives you a ticket that services accept. Stealing or forging tickets means copying a real ticket, or making a fake one, so you can enter services without the password. The two main versions are stolen tickets and forged tickets, and they leave different traces in the logs.

Why should you care?

A stolen ticket is as good as the password that created it, at least until it expires. A forged ticket can last far longer if the attacker controls the key behind it. Because the tickets have a genuine format, ordinary users and services notice nothing strange. Recovering from a ticket attack often means changing passwords for service accounts and resetting sensitive keys. If you do not know this technique exists, you will hunt for a stolen password that was never used.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You log in to the domain, and the domain controller gives you a ticket-granting ticket (TGT).
  2. When you open a service, you show that TGT and receive a service ticket for it.
  3. An attacker with access to your machine may copy a ticket from memory or from disk.
  4. They can then use the copied ticket from another computer, without your password.
  5. Alternatively, they may obtain the long-term key behind tickets and create their own.
  6. Forged tickets are accepted by real services, because the signature inside them looks correct.

Real-world example

Imagine a theme park where you buy a wristband once at the gate. Every ride accepts the wristband instead of asking for money again. A thief who copies your wristband can ride everything you paid for until the park closes. A thief who can print perfect wristbands can ride for as long as they like, and the staff at each ride have no reason to question them. Both cases look normal at the gate of every ride, so the park must study patterns, such as one wristband entering rides in two different places at once.

Key takeaways

  • A stolen or forged ticket needs no password, so watch ticket use and not only logins.
  • Short ticket lifetimes and regular service password rotation limit the damage.
  • Compare who requested a ticket with who actually used it.

In more detail

Kerberos is the default authentication protocol in Windows domains, and it issues tickets instead of sending a password each time. A client authenticates to the Key Distribution Center (KDC) on a domain controller and receives a ticket-granting ticket (TGT), then presents that TGT to request ticket-granting service (TGS) tickets, also called service tickets, for individual services. An attacker who steals a ticket can present it without knowing the password, which is the basis of pass-the-ticket, and an attacker who obtains the long-term key behind a ticket can forge one that no legitimate issuance produced. The parent technique covers both paths: dumping ticket material from memory or disk, extracting service account keys, requesting tickets for accounts that should not be reachable, and crafting tickets offline. Detection rarely rests on a single event, because valid tickets are valid. Defenders instead look at anomalies: TGS requests for many different service principal names from one account in a short time, ticket encryption types that do not match the domain baseline, logons with no preceding authentication to the KDC, and service tickets used from hosts the user never signs in from.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a stolen ticket can be reused without the account password.
  • Identify the domain controller and host events that reveal unusual ticket activity.
