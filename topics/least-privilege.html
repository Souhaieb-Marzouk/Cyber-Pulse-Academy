Least Privilege

Security ArchitectureHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

Least privilege means giving each person and each program only the access it needs to do its job, and nothing extra. Access includes reading files, changing settings or using a service. If a task ends, the access should end too. It is a limit you choose, not a punishment you apply.

Why should you care?

Extra access is like leaving spare keys on a hook for everyone. When one account is stolen, the thief gets everything that account could touch. If that account can only read one report, the damage stays small. Limits also make honest mistakes smaller, because a wrong command cannot delete the whole company. Auditors and insurers ask about this, so it supports the business too.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. Start from deny by default, so a new account begins with no access at all.
  2. Identify what the role truly needs, which is a job function rather than a person's name.
  3. Create roles that bundle those rights, and give the role to a group instead of to individuals.
  4. Grant the highest risk rights only when needed, with approval, and let them expire quickly.
  5. Review the list of who holds which rights on a fixed schedule and remove what is unused.
  6. Remove all access at once when someone changes job or leaves the organisation.

Real-world example

Think of a large library with a locked back office. A shelver needs a trolley key to put books back on the shelves. The cataloguer needs the catalogue screen, and the cleaner needs the store cupboard. Nobody needs a key to the rare book safe except the archivist, and that key is signed out for one afternoon at a time. If the cleaner loses a key, only the cupboard is at risk. If the library gave everyone a master key for convenience, one lost key would open the whole building, including the safe.

Key takeaways

  • Access should match the current job and should not last forever.
  • Least privilege limits the damage when one account or program is taken over.
  • Review rights regularly, especially when people move teams or leave.

In more detail

Least privilege is the principle that every user, process, service account and device should hold only the access required to perform its current task, for only as long as that task lasts. It is one of the oldest ideas in computer security, usually traced to the early 1970s, and it underpins most modern control frameworks. Mechanically it is implemented by starting from deny by default and adding permission only in response to a documented need: role based access control groups rights by job function, roles are assigned to groups rather than to individuals, and standing rights are replaced where possible with just in time elevation that expires on its own. The payoff is blast radius containment, because a phished account, a leaked service token or a compromised process can then do only what that identity was already permitted to do. Enforcement depends on identity and entitlement review, so it fails quietly without usage data, periodic recertification and prompt removal on role change or exit. The common mistake is turning it into a vague aspiration of giving nobody anything, instead of a measurable process of narrowing well understood rights. Real deployments need a workable middle step: coarse roles first, then refinement guided by logs.

What you will practise

  • Define least privilege and explain how it limits the impact of a compromised account or process.
  • Describe how deny by default, group based roles and just in time elevation implement the principle.
  • Explain why entitlement review and prompt offboarding are needed to keep access accurate.
  • Distinguish least privilege as a measurable process from the vague idea of granting nothing.
  • Discuss the practical trade off between tight access and the friction it creates for staff.
