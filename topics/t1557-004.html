T1557.004 Adversary-in-the-Middle: Evil Twin

T1557.004Credential AccessExtremeTechnique

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

An evil twin is a fake Wi-Fi access point that uses the same network name as a real one. Your phone or laptop sees the familiar name and connects automatically. Because the network looks friendly, you may also see a login page that asks for your work password. The parent technique is about standing between two devices, and this version does it through a wireless network that you choose yourself.

Why should you care?

Devices remember networks they have joined before and reconnect without asking you. If a fake access point copies that name, your device may join it on its own. Everything you send then passes through the attacker, including logins on unencrypted sites. A fake login page can capture a password that also works elsewhere. Public places such as airports and coffee shops are popular hunting grounds, because many people are looking for a known name.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. The attacker sets up a wireless access point with the same network name as a real one.
  2. They may boost the signal or force your device off the real access point.
  3. Your device, or you, then connects to the fake network that looks familiar.
  4. The fake network gives you settings that send your traffic through the attacker.
  5. A fake login page or welcome portal may ask for your username and password.
  6. Once you are connected, the attacker reads and changes your traffic as it passes.

Real-world example

Think of an airport cafe where two counters have the same sign above them. The real counter is busy, so a second counter with an identical sign opens next to it. You hand your passport and boarding pass to the second counter, because the sign looks right. That counter copies your details and then walks you to the real counter, where everything continues normally. Nobody at the real counter knows you were copied. The only clue is that the second counter has no staff list and no till, even though the sign is perfect.

Key takeaways

  • A familiar network name is not proof that the network is real.
  • Turn off automatic joining for open networks, and use a trusted connection instead.
  • Protect accounts with a second factor that a copied password cannot satisfy.

In more detail

An evil twin is a fraudulent wireless access point that copies the Service Set Identifier (SSID) of a legitimate network so that devices and users connect to it voluntarily. The attacker may broadcast a stronger signal, jam clients away from the real access point, or wait for probe requests that name previously known networks and answer them, so automatic reconnection does the work. A captured client portal or fake login page then collects credentials, and a rogue Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) or Domain Name System (DNS) service on the twin points traffic through the attacker. Detection is mostly wireless side: access point inventories and wireless intrusion prevention systems find radios that broadcast a known SSID but have an unknown basic service set identifier, an unexpected vendor or no wired uplink, and clients that associate to them. Deauthentication frames and extra radios also appear in legitimate testing and mesh networks, so defenders confirm the radio rather than the name.

What you will practise

  • Explain how a fraudulent access point can imitate a legitimate wireless network name.
  • Describe how probe requests and preferred network lists cause automatic reconnection to a rogue device.
  • Explain how this sub-technique differs from the other adversary-in-the-middle sub-techniques.
  • Identify wireless inventory and client association data that exposes an evil twin access point.
  • Recommend settings and habits that reduce the risk of joining a rogue wireless network.
