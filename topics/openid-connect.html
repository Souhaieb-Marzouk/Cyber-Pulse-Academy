OpenID Connect

Identity & AccessHardKeyword

New to this? Start here

Explained in plain language

Written for beginners. No security background needed.

Simple definition

OpenID Connect is a standard way to sign in to a website. You use an account you already have somewhere else. The name describes a layer built on an older system called OAuth 2.0. The other site proves who you are and sends a short signed statement back. Your password is never given to the website you are signing in to.

Why should you care?

People reuse passwords, so every extra site that stores one is another chance to leak it. Using one trusted login reduces the number of passwords and makes it easier to switch off access when someone leaves. It also lets you add strong checks, such as a security key, in one place instead of many. If the identity statement is not checked carefully, an attacker can copy it and pretend to be another user. Getting the standard right protects both convenience and safety.

How it works, in plain steps

  1. You press a sign in button on a website and are sent to your identity provider.
  2. The provider asks you to log in, often with an extra check such as a code on your phone.
  3. After you agree, the provider sends the website a short signed statement about you.
  4. The website checks the signature, who issued it, and that it was meant for this website.
  5. It also checks the statement is fresh and was not reused from an earlier sign in.
  6. Only then does the website create your session and show you your own account.

Real-world example

Picture a members only library. Instead of giving every branch its own membership card, the library accepts a city identity card. A clerk reads the card, checks the hologram and the expiry date, and confirms it belongs to this branch. A photocopy of someone else's card fails the hologram check. The clerk also refuses a card issued for a different city, even if it looks genuine.

Key takeaways

  • OpenID Connect adds a verified identity statement on top of OAuth 2.0 authorisation.
  • The website must check signature, issuer, audience and freshness before trusting the statement.
  • Central login means one place to enforce strong authentication and one place to revoke access.

In more detail

OpenID Connect, usually shortened to OIDC, is an identity layer built on top of the OAuth 2.0 authorisation framework. OAuth 2.0 was designed to grant an application scoped access to a resource without handing over a password, and it deliberately left the question of who the user is to other specifications; OIDC answers that question by defining an identity token, a signed JSON Web Token that the identity provider issues to the client and that contains claims such as a subject identifier, the issuer, the audience and the time of authentication. The protocol defines discovery metadata, several flows tuned for browser applications, mobile applications and machine-to-machine callers, and it adds an identity-specific endpoint for session management and logout. It is what sits behind most single sign-on and social login buttons. The frequent mistakes are conflating the two token types, treating an access token as proof of user identity when it was meant for a resource server, and mis-validating the identity token by skipping checks of issuer, audience, signature algorithm or nonce, which is exactly what allows token substitution and replay attacks against otherwise correct deployments.

What you will practise

  • Explain what OpenID Connect is and how it relates to the OAuth 2.0 framework.
  • Describe the role of the identity token and the claims it carries about a signed in user.
  • List the validation checks a client must perform before trusting an identity token.
  • Distinguish an access token from an identity token and explain why the difference matters.
  • Identify where OpenID Connect appears in single sign-on and third party login flows.
