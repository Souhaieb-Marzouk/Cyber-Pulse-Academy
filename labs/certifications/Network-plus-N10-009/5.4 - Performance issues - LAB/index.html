Block 02 · Service Manual

How performance breaks — and how it gets fixed

The bench manual for N10-009 objective 5.4: congestion/contention, bottlenecking, bandwidth & throughput capacity, latency, packet loss, jitter — plus wireless interference from this lesson. Every term is also in the Glossary (bench header).

Bandwidth is the rated capacity of a link — “1 Gbps” printed on the spec sheet. Throughput is what you actually measure moving through it over a period, and goodput is the slice of that which is usable application payload once protocol overhead (TCP/IP headers, ACKs, retransmissions) is subtracted. A healthy 1 Gbps path typically measures in the mid-900s of Mbps of TCP throughput — the difference is overhead, not fault.

CompTIA’s bullet “bandwidth — throughput capacity” is about comparing rated capacity against measured demand: a link whose sustained utilization runs near its ceiling is a candidate for upgrade, and a link that measures far below its rating has a different problem — errors, duplex mismatch, or a bottleneck somewhere else in the path. You prove capacity with a throughput test (iperf between two known hosts, or a speed tester) — never by trusting the spec sheet alone.

Why the exam cares

N+ wants you to distinguish “the pipe is too small” (capacity/congestion) from “the pipe is fine but something eats it” (errors, interference, bottleneck) — the fix and the cost are completely different.

Congestion happens when offered traffic approaches or exceeds a link’s capacity: router and switch buffers (queues) fill, and devices either hold packets (adding queuing delay) or drop them (queue drops). You see it on utilization graphs — a link pinned at 90–100% during business hours — and in interface counters as climbing output drops. Contention is the same squeeze at the source: devices competing for a shared medium, most commonly Wi-Fi airtime (all stations on a channel take turns) or any half-duplex segment.

Classic amplifiers of congestion: a single top talker hogging the link (a rendering workstation, a backup job), oversubscribed uplinks (many 1 Gbps access ports feeding one 1 Gbps uplink), and broadcast storms — a looping L2 path or misbehaving NIC that floods broadcasts until they crowd out real traffic, which is why loops are killed with Spanning Tree Protocol and broadcast domains are carved up with VLANs.

Fixes (in the order you’d actually try them)

  • Identify the top talkers first (SNMP/flow monitoring, packet capture) — cheapest win is often stopping one noisy host.
  • QoS: give latency-sensitive traffic (voice) a priority queue so congestion degrades the bulk traffic instead of the calls.
  • Rate-limit / shape the heavy, delay-tolerant flows (that product-video loop doesn’t need full line rate).
  • Add capacity or balance it: upgrade the uplink, aggregate links (LACP), or move traffic to another path.
  • Reschedule bulk traffic (backups at night) and segment busy groups into their own VLANs.

A bottleneck is the single slowest segment in a path — the pipe that caps throughput for everything behind it, no matter how fast the rest is. End-to-end throughput ≈ the capacity of the tightest choke point (minus overhead and errors). Bottlenecks are often asymmetric: a site’s 50 Mbps circuit carries requests out fast but chokes on responses coming back; or many fast access links oversubscribe one narrow uplink (a 20:1 ratio means the uplink saturates long before any access port does).

The discipline: trace the path hop by hop (traceroute timings, per-link utilization, throughput tests between segments) and fix the choke point itself — upgrade that circuit, shape the traffic feeding it, or reroute around it. Upgrading anything else is money spent on symptoms. The Harbor Kiosk case on this bench is a textbook choke point; find it before you spend.

Latency is how long a packet takes to get where it’s going — usually measured as RTT (round-trip time, what ping reports). It is the sum of four components you should be able to name in order: processing delay (device reads the header, does the lookup), queuing delay (packet waits in a buffer — the component congestion inflates), serialization/transmission delay (clocking the bits onto the wire at link speed), and propagation delay (physics: distance ÷ speed of light in the medium — ~5 ms per 1,000 km of fiber, labeled approximation). Propagation sets a floor you cannot engineer away: a geostationary satellite path costs hundreds of milliseconds RTT no matter what you buy.

Jitter is the variation in that delay between consecutive packets. Real-time traffic (VoIP, video calls) tolerates average delay better than it tolerates inconsistent delay: receivers smooth jitter with a de-jitter buffer, but a wildly varying stream either overflows the buffer (drops) or the conversation stalls. Rule-of-thumb numbers (industry practice, simplified): VoIP one-way latency ≲150 ms, jitter ≲30 ms, and each of these degrades calls noticeably beyond that.

Measuring & fixing

  • ping gives RTT and variance; run it continuously to see patterns by time of day.
  • traceroute / pathping / mtr time each hop — the hop where RTT jumps is where the delay (usually queuing) begins.
  • Fixes: QoS priority queuing for real-time classes, better routing (fewer hops), caching/CDN to keep traffic local, shaping at the edge — and accepting the propagation floor instead of buying snake oil.

Packet loss is packets that are dropped somewhere between sender and receiver. The two causes you must separate on the exam — and on this bench — have different fingerprints:

  • Congestion drops: full buffers discard packets when a link saturates. Fingerprint: output drops climbing on the overloaded interface, loss that follows the busy-period pattern.
  • Physical/RF errors: damaged frames discarded on arrival. Fingerprint: CRC errors and input errors climbing on one specific port while output drops stay flat — the classic signature of a failing patch cable, connector, transceiver or NIC (cabling/physical territory from objective 5.2). On Wi-Fi, interference and weak SNR produce the same effect over the air: retransmissions spike, rates drop.

Where to see loss: interface counters, ping/pathping/mtr loss percentages, a protocol analyzer showing TCP retransmissions and duplicate ACKs, or iperf in UDP mode reporting the loss rate directly. Impact escalates fast: TCP treats loss as congestion and collapses its sending rate, so even ~2–3% loss can wreck a file transfer (rule of thumb, simplified), and VoIP turns choppy around 1%+.

Fixes follow the fingerprint: re-terminate or replace the cable/transceiver, correct speed/duplex mismatches, relieve the congested link (MAN 2), or repair the RF path (MAN 6).

Wi-Fi shares a licensed-free radio band, so “capacity” is airtime, and interference is anything that ruins it. Two flavors matter for the exam:

  • Co-channel interference (CCI) — neighboring APs on the same channel must take turns transmitting; every station on the channel shares airtime, so throughput sags with contention (annoying but orderly).
  • Adjacent-channel interference (ACI) — APs on overlapping channels (in 2.4 GHz, anything closer than 5 channels apart, e.g. 6 and 10) corrupt each other’s frames; receivers hear two conversations at once. This is the worse neighbor and shows up as retransmissions and rate collapse.

In 2.4 GHz only channels 1, 6 and 11 are non-overlapping at 20 MHz width — the classic channel plan. Wider bonded channels (40 MHz+) buy throughput on paper but eat adjacent channels and range in the real world. Non-Wi-Fi devices crowd the same band: microwave ovens (bursts around 2.45 GHz), Bluetooth, cordless phones, and heavy motors, which raise the noise floor — the background RF power everything competes against. Your margin is SNR = signal strength (RSSI) − noise floor; poor SNR or attenuation through walls and fixtures, plus multipath reflections, all push clients to retransmit and drop.

Detect & fix

  • Wi-Fi analyzer: sees other APs — channel, signal, overlap. Spectrum analyzer: sees non-Wi-Fi energy — the only tool that catches the microwave.
  • Fixes: re-plan channels (1/6/11, 20 MHz), steer dual-band clients to 5 GHz, tune AP placement and power, and physically separate or replace the noise source.

Everything on this bench is organic failure — but the same four symptoms can be caused. Flooding a link until its queues overflow is the mechanic of network denial-of-service (MITRE ATT&CK T1498, Impact tactic): congestion, latency, loss and jitter arrive together, exactly like a badly planned backup. On wireless networks, deliberate RF jamming produces the same “interference” signature as a kitchen microwave. That is the defender’s trap and the reason baselines matter: you can only call a graph “abnormal” if you know what normal looks like. (Educational note — this bench teaches recognition and mitigation only; it does not instruct in attack technique.)

BLUE-TEAM TAKEAWAYS
  • Baseline before you alarm: the 02:00 backup and the lunch microwave are “attacks” until the baseline says otherwise.
  • Fingerprints before fixes: CRC-climbing-with-flat-drops = physical; drops-with-utilization = congestion; RTT-jump-at-one-hop = choke point; time-of-day Wi-Fi loss = RF.
  • Fix the choke point, verify with numbers (iperf before/after), then document — the handover IS the detection content.
  • Separate detection from alarm: not every anomaly deserves a page. Tune thresholds + exclusion windows.
RED-TEAM MINDSET (defender’s view)
  • Flooding a link manufactures every symptom on this bench at once — network DoS (ATT&CK T1498, Impact) hides inside “the network is slow” tickets.
  • Deliberate RF jamming is indistinguishable from a microwave unless you own a spectrum analyzer and a baseline.
  • Attackers prefer causing degradation over outage — it stays under alarm thresholds and out of change logs.
  • Your utilization graphs are a sensor for someone else’s activity: watch for patterns that don’t match any known job.
