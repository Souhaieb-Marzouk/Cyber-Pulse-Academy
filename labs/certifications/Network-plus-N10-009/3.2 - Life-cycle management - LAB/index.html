Welcome to the yard, clerk.

You are the new IT yard clerk at Trailhead Freight Co., a regional freight firm running six depots on aging gear. On November 9, 2026, our operations manager walked the floor and found the mess every network team eventually finds: gear the vendors no longer sell, gear they no longer support at all, updates that never got scheduled, and a storage room full of equipment waiting to leave without its paperwork.

Your shift has one goal: get every asset to the right stage of its life — know where each device stands (EOL vs EOS), keep supported software current the safe way (patches, OS upgrades, firmware — tested, windowed, reversible), and send retiring assets out the gate sanitized, legal, and documented.

This is the exact work CompTIA Network+ N10-009 tests under Domain 3.0 Network Operations — Life-cycle management. Master the yard and you can answer every lifecycle question on that exam.

EOL — end-of-life (a planning signal)

End-of-life means the vendor stops making and selling the product. It is not an emergency: the devices already deployed usually keep working and keep receiving vendor support until the end-of-support date. EOL is the moment to start planning — budget the replacement, schedule the refresh, and stop buying more units.

Exam tip: EOL ≠ broken. An EOL device that is still supported is a refresh-planning item, not an incident.
EOS — end-of-support (a risk condition)

End-of-support (also called end of service life, EOSL) means the vendor ends everything: no security patches, no bug fixes, no technical support, no spare parts. A device still in production after EOS silently accumulates unpatchable vulnerabilities and can fail compliance audits. Nothing "breaks" on EOS day — that's what makes it dangerous.

Exam tip: past EOS you cannot patch your way out — the fix is replacement/upgrade plus interim compensating controls.
Software management — patches, OS, firmware

Inside the supported window, software management keeps assets healthy through three kinds of change: patches and bug fixes (small, frequent — security patches close vulnerabilities and go first), OS upgrades (major version moves that need testing and a migration plan), and firmware updates (the low-level code in switches, access points, firewalls and server BIOS/UEFI). Every change follows the same safe pattern: test first → schedule a maintenance window → prepare a rollback plan → apply → verify.

Exam tip: never update production firmware or an OS without a rollback plan — the previous image or configuration backup that lets you undo it.
Decommissioning — the controlled exit

Decommissioning is more than unplugging. Back up and transfer the data, then sanitize or destroy the media matched to data sensitivity (secure wipe for working drives, shredding for media that can't be wiped, degaussing only for magnetic media), honor legal holds and records-retention rules, route the hardware (resale, recycle, destruction) with certificates as proof, and close the loop in documentation: asset inventory, network diagram, licenses.

Exam tip: "sanitize media or destroy" — and disposal of desktops, laptops, tablets and mobile devices may be a legal issue when regulated data is involved.
Real-world context (not part of the scenario):
  • Major network vendors (e.g., Cisco) publish end-of-sale / end-of-support bulletins with exact dates for every product — yard clerks read them like weather reports.
  • Microsoft publishes fixed end-of-support dates for Windows versions — Windows 10 reached end of support on October 14, 2025, and machines still running it past that date receive no security updates.
  • Regulated data (payment cards, health records, driver-license scans) usually carries a legal retention period, so "just shred it" is not always the legal answer.
