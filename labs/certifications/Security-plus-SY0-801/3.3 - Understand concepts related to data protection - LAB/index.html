1 Why attackers want your data

Data is the loot of almost every intrusion. Attackers collect it where it lives (MITRE T1005 Data from Local System, T1530 Data from Cloud Storage) and exfiltrate it over web services or physical media (T1567, T1052 — both appear on our watch desk today). Protection strategy = make data hard to find, hard to read, hard to move, and worthless once moved.

2 The lifecycle — five stages of custody

Create / captureStoreUse / shareArchiveDestroy

Data enters (forms, sensors, email), is stored (databases, cloud, paper), is used and shared (analysts, vendors, applications), rests in archives (backups, cold storage under a retention policy), and finally must die — verifiably. A chain is only as strong as its weakest stage: an unencrypted archive betrays a perfectly encrypted production database.

3 The three states of data

StateMeaningDominant safeguard
At restOn media: disks, backups, paperEncryption (full-disk / volume / file), physical control
In transitMoving across networksTLS / IPsec / VPN
In useIn RAM, on screens, being processedIRM, least privilege, session controls, clean desks

Same record, three different leak vectors: a stolen drive, a captured connection, a photographed screen.

4 Classification — the MVHA ladder

  1. Public — approved for anyone; release harms nothing.
  2. Private — internal only; embarrassment at worst (org charts, rosters).
  3. Sensitive — limited harm if disclosed: internal financials, contracts.
  4. Confidential — serious harm; regulated personal data (PII/PHI/PCI) and trade secrets live here.
  5. Critical — severe or irreparable harm; crown jewels whose loss halts operations (encryption keys, patent-pending research).

Some organizations add a restricted rung above confidential for named-individual-only access. Classification is chosen by harm, and it dictates handling — never the reverse.

7 Methods to secure data

MethodWhat it does
EncryptionReversible scramble; needs key custody (TPM on devices, HSM enterprise-wide)
HashingOne-way fingerprint; proves integrity, never "unlocked"
MaskingPermanently hides part of a value (SSN ***-**-1234) — for display and test data
TokenizationSwaps a value for a surrogate; only the vault can map back
MinimizationCollect only what the stated purpose needs — every extra field is a liability
IRMRights (view/print/forward) travel inside the file itself
DLPWatches content + destination, blocks or alerts on sensitive data leaving
Retention & destructionKeep only as long as policy/law requires; then clear (overwrite), purge (unrecoverable: degauss or destroy), crypto-shred (destroy keys), and issue a certificate of destruction for regulated data. Degaussing does not work on SSDs — shred those.
Sovereignty & geoData obeys the law of the land where it is stored/processed; geo-blocking denies access by geography

8 Cases every custodian studies

Equifax (2017) — unencrypted PII of ≈147M people on poorly segmented systems; ≈$700M settlement. Lesson: encrypt at rest, minimize what you hold.
Anthem (2015) — 78.8M PHI records; a $16M HIPAA settlement. Lesson: classify PHI and lock access down.
Meta (2023) — €1.2B GDPR fine over EU→US data transfers. Lesson: sovereignty and residency are legal walls, not preferences.
Target (2013) — ≈40M payment cards via a third-party HVAC vendor. Lesson: your processor's weakness is your breach; segment and monitor.

    Field notes — what the bureau learned

    Detection opportunities (watch desk)

    • Content + destination DLP rules beat volume rules: alert when payloads match PHI/PCI patterns (SSN, PAN, diagnosis codes) and the destination is off the approved list. Our noisy rule improved in the exercise you tuned.
    • Baseline before you judge: the 02:00 "scary" 4.1 GB flow was SOP backup BK-07; the harmless-looking 10:05 email was the real leak. Correlate with SOPs, service accounts and roles first.
    • Human-readable data leaks on paper and screens: 212-page print jobs to a lobby MFP and phone photographs of portals are exfiltration too (in-use state).
    • Device control: USB mass-storage events need user + role + volume tracking (MITRE T1052); unsanctioned cloud uploads need egress and API logging (T1567, T1530).

    Mitigation recommendations (what MVHA must fund)

    • Per-state controls: full-disk/volume encryption at rest, TLS 1.3 / IPsec in transit, IRM + session controls in use. One state unprotected = one breach vector open.
    • Minimize, mask, tokenize: collect only what the purpose needs; mask display copies; tokenize card data so PANs never live in MVHA systems (PCI DSS scope reduction).
    • Custody chain: named owner per dataset, controller decides processing, processors contractually bound to notify the controller, DPO owns regulator clocks (GDPR 72 h; HIPAA ≤60 days).
    • Verifiable destruction: degauss + destroy magnetic media, shred SSDs and paper, crypto-shred cloud archives — certificates of destruction for regulated data.
    • Sovereignty by architecture: EU-partner records pinned to EU-West residency; geo-blocking on admin planes.

    Blue-team takeaways

    • Inventory and classify first — you cannot protect what you have not stamped. Classification is decided by harm and then drives handling.
    • Retention is protection: the cheapest record to defend is the one you destroyed on schedule.
    • Roles are controls: "who approves?" and "who keeps the keys?" must have names attached.
    • Rehearse the clock: contain → preserve evidence → assess with legal → notify within the clock → remediate.

    Red-team mindset (so you can spot it)

    • Attackers follow your classification labels to find the crown jewels; unmasked copies (dev databases, exports, printouts) are the softest target.
    • Exfiltration prefers trusted, sanctioned channels — personal cloud drives, email, USB — because they ride past the firewall looking like work.
    • Collection is quiet; volume and hour anomalies help, but content inspection is what actually catches it.
