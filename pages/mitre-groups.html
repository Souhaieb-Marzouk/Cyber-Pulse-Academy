MITRE ATT&CK Groups

ATT&CK tracks 16 threat groups that CyberPulseAcademy covers as study topics: state-linked intrusions such as APT28, APT29 and Volt Typhoon, financially motivated crews such as FIN7, Lazarus Group and Storm-0501, and long-running espionage operations such as Turla and Ke3chang. Each group topic collects the tradecraft that public reporting links to it — the techniques, the tooling habits, the infrastructure patterns — and deliberately avoids writing a biography of an adversary nobody outside the reporting can see.

Read a group page as a lens on behaviour, not as a scoreboard of adversaries. The techniques attributed to a group are the same techniques you already study on their own topic pages; the group simply tells you which combinations tend to appear together, which is useful when you are deciding what to hunt for next.

Attribution and confidence

CyberPulseAcademy reports attributions; it does not make them. Every group topic here reflects third-party reporting — vendor threat intelligence, national CERT advisories and MITRE's own group profiles, each cited on the page — and this project takes no position of its own on who is behind any intrusion. Attribution is contested analytical work, not a fact that can be looked up, and the same set of observed behaviours is regularly attributed differently by two competent teams looking at the same evidence.

Some attributions are disputed between reporting organisations, and some are held at only moderate confidence, meaning the reporting supports a link without proving it. Where that applies, the group topic says so in plain language and states the confidence level the source used, rather than flattening every claim into the same confident register. Treat a group name as a label over a set of observed behaviours, and study the behaviours.

What to do with threat group reporting

Group reporting is useful in proportion to how carefully you convert it into things you can check on your own estate. Four habits make that conversion work.

  • Prioritise behaviours over names. Group names are a vendor convention and they change: one organisation's APT29 is another's Cozy Bear, a cluster gets renamed when new reporting splits it, and a single actor is regularly tracked under several labels at once. Tradecraft persists across all of that renaming. When a report gives you a technique, a tool or an infrastructure pattern, you have something you can hunt on Monday morning; when it gives you only a name, you have a headline.
  • Use groups to build hunts, not blocklists. The value of a group page is the combination of techniques, because combinations narrow a hunt to something worth running. Volt Typhoon's reported focus on living-off-the-land behaviour means the hunt is for legitimate binaries invoked with unusual lineage and parentage, not for a file hash that was stale the week it was published.
  • Check the confidence level before you escalate. A moderate-confidence attribution is a reason to look more closely, not a reason to name an actor in a board report or to brief a regulator. Say what you observed, say who reported the link, and say how strong that reporting is; conflating the three is how an incident review turns into an argument about geopolitics.
  • Watch the mirror problem. Reading only about named groups biases you towards the activity that someone has already named, and named activity is a minority of what a SOC actually sees. Unattributed intrusions still use the same techniques. Study the group pages for the patterns, then apply the patterns to everything, including the activity no report has claimed yet.
