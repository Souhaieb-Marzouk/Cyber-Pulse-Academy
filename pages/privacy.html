Privacy Policy

CyberMastery collects as little as it can and keeps it on your device. There is no account, no email address, and no cookie of any kind.

Last updated: 1 June 2025

The short version
  • No account. No email address. No password.
  • The name and country you enter stay in your browser.
  • Statistics default to local-only and never leave your device.
  • You can delete everything with one button in the footer.

Who is responsible

The controller of anything processed here is one individual running CyberMastery as a free, non-commercial community project. There is no company behind it, no employees, and no organisation of any kind.

There is no data protection officer, because there is no organisation that would appoint one. Contact is through the GitHub repository issues page: https://github.com/<user>/<repo>/issues. Use it for access requests, deletion requests, corrections, objections and questions about this policy. Do not put personal data in a public issue.

What is collected and why

Everything below is stored in your browser's local storage. It is on your device, under your browser's control, and you can delete it at any time. In the shipped default mode, local mode, the site owner never receives any of it at all. The site owner cannot see it, cannot look it up, and could not hand it over if asked, because it never reaches any server the owner controls.

The legal basis for this small amount of processing is consent: the checkbox in the identity gate, which you tick before your first exercise and can withdraw at any time by deleting your data. Reading pages requires no consent, because reading pages collects nothing.

What is never collected

To be explicit, this site does not collect, store, request or transmit:

  • your email address, in any form, at any time, for any reason
  • your real name, which is never required: the identity gate asks for a name you choose
  • any password, because there is no account to protect
  • your IP address, which is never stored by this site
  • a device fingerprint, a canvas fingerprint, a font list or any other device identifier
  • an advertising identifier, a mobile ad ID, or a cross-device identifier
  • your location, beyond the country code you select yourself from a list
  • your browsing history, on this site or anywhere else
  • your contacts, files, camera, microphone or any other device permission
  • third-party cookies, or any cookie at all
  • cross-site tracking data, or a profile of you built across websites

Even in remote mode, the raw username is never transmitted. What leaves the device is a truncated hash, and a truncated hash cannot be reversed back into the username it came from.

Statistics modes explained

The site has three statistic modes. One of them ships enabled. The other two are described here because you are entitled to know what they would do if the site owner switched to them.

Local mode (the shipped default)

Counters live only in your browser. Your results, the distinct-user count and the country breakdown are computed on your device, from your device's own data, for the statistics page you are looking at. Nothing is sent. There is no network request for statistics in local mode, and the site owner never receives anything. What you see on the statistics page is your own study history plus nobody else's.

This is the default because collecting anything centrally without a published privacy policy is a real risk to the people using the site, not a formality. Local mode is the honest default for a project this size.

Remote mode (opt-in by the site owner)

In remote mode, completed exercise attempts are posted to an endpoint the site owner hosts. This mode is chosen by the owner, in a configuration file, and it is not something an individual visitor can turn on. It exists so a public statistics page can show aggregate numbers across everyone.

What leaves your device in remote mode is exactly this, and nothing more:

  • a truncated SHA-256 hash of a public salt plus your username, cut to the first 16 hexadecimal characters
  • a country code, taken from the country you selected
  • a topic id
  • a batch number
  • a score
  • a percentage
  • a pass or fail flag
  • a timestamp

Those fields are posted as a small JSON object to /event on the endpoint the owner hosts. The salt used in the hash is public and is published in the site's configuration file. It exists only to make the hash stable, so that the same username produces the same hash on every visit and across deployments, so distinct users can be counted. It is deliberately not a secret, because a secret salt would imply a level of protection this project is not claiming.

The hash is truncated to 16 hexadecimal characters precisely so that it cannot be reversed into a username. That is a deliberate privacy property: it means the owner can count you, but generally cannot identify you. It is also a real limitation, and it is described honestly under "Your rights" below. A short, common username could in principle be matched by someone who already had a list of candidate usernames and the public salt, which is one more reason the site never asks for your real name or your email address.

The request carries no cookies, and it is sent with credentials omitted, so it does not carry any session or login context. If the network is unavailable, the event is held in cm.stats.queue.v1 on your device and sent later; the queue is capped at the 50 most recent events.

Off mode

Off mode collects nothing at all. No attempt is written to your device, no distinct-user count is kept, and nothing is sent. The statistics page reports zeros rather than inventing numbers. Exercises still work and are still scored on screen for the session; the score simply is not recorded anywhere. If you see off mode, the site owner has chosen to collect nothing, and there is nothing for you to opt out of.

Cookies

This site sets no cookies at all. No first-party cookies, no third-party cookies, no advertising cookies, no analytics cookies, no session cookies. Zero. Nothing in this site's code calls document.cookie, and there is no embedded third-party script that could do it on the site's behalf.

The storage notice you may have seen exists because the site uses your browser's local storage for the keys listed in the table above. A cookie is sent to a server with every request. Local storage is not: it stays in your browser until you remove it, and it is only read by the page itself. The notice tells you that local storage is being used, and it is dismissible and non-blocking. Declining to interact with it does not restrict any content.

The full detail, key by key, is on the Cookies and local storage page.

Your rights

You have the right to access, correct, delete, object to and port the personal data held about you. Here is how each one works in practice on this site.

Access. In local mode you already hold all of it. It is in your own browser, and the site shows it to you: your identity in the header chip, your attempts on the topic and statistics pages. There is no hidden copy, because there is no server copy.

Correction. Use Edit identity in the footer to change your chosen name or country. Correcting the stored hash means deleting your data and starting again, because the hash is derived from the name you chose.

Deletion. In local mode the footer's Delete my data button is the complete deletion. It removes every key listed above from your browser. There is no server-side copy to delete in local mode, because the owner never received one.

Objection. Switch the site off for yourself at any time by deleting your data and not entering a name again. Reading pages collects nothing, so there is nothing to object to until you start an exercise.

Portability. Your data is plain JSON in your browser's local storage. Any browser that shows site data can export or copy it. There is no proprietary format and no lock-in.

In remote mode the position is different, and it is worth stating plainly. The aggregate sits with the site owner, so a deletion or access request has to go through the owner, using the GitHub repository issues page. But because the data is keyed to a truncated hash rather than to an identity, the owner may not be able to tell which records are yours at all. That is a deliberate privacy property, and it is also a real limitation: an honest answer here is that complete erasure of your records cannot be guaranteed, because finding them may be impossible in the first place.

How to delete everything

With the button in the footer

  1. Scroll to the footer at the bottom of any page.
  2. Select Delete my data.
  3. Confirm in the dialog that appears.
  4. Every cm.* key is removed from this browser: identity, attempts, distinct-user counts, theme, language, notice dismissals and any queued statistics events. The site then behaves as if you had never visited.

This action only removes keys that begin with cm., which is every key this site owns. It cannot touch anything else in your browser.

Through your browser settings, on a desktop

  1. Chrome or Edge: open Settings, then Privacy and security, then Third-party cookies or Site data, and find this site under "See all site data and permissions". Select it and delete.
  2. Firefox: open Settings, then Privacy and Security, then Cookies and Site Data, then Manage Data. Select this site and remove it.
  3. Safari: open Settings, then Privacy, then Manage Website Data. Find this site and remove it.

Through your browser settings, on a phone or tablet

  1. iOS Safari: open Settings, then Safari, then Advanced, then Website Data. Find this site and delete it. You can also use Settings, then Safari, then Clear History and Website Data to remove everything.
  2. Android Chrome: open the browser menu, then Settings, then Site settings, then Storage, and clear this site's data, or use "Delete browsing data" with site data included.
  3. Firefox for Android: open the menu, then Settings, then Delete browsing data, and include site data.

Menu names differ between versions and languages. Look for the browser's site data or storage section; that is where local storage lives on every browser.

Children

This site is not directed at anyone under 16, and it is not intended for them to use it. The content assumes a professional or advanced student context, and the exercises are written for people working in or training seriously for security roles.

If you are under 16, do not use the exercise features, and do not enter a name. There is no age check, because there is no profiling and no account: the site simply states its intended audience and relies on it being respected.

The identity gate asks for a chosen username. It never asks for a real name, never asks for an email address, and never asks for a date of birth. If a parent or guardian believes a child has entered data, the fix is the same one button in the footer, or clearing site data in the browser. Both remove it completely.

International transfers

In local mode there is no transfer, because nothing leaves your device.

In remote mode, the owner's endpoint may be hosted outside your country, and the hosting provider's infrastructure may store or route data in a third country. The payload sent contains no directly identifying information: a truncated hash, a country code, a topic id, a batch number, a score, a percentage, a pass flag and a timestamp. It carries no email address, no real name and no IP address stored by this site. Because the payload is keyed to a truncated hash rather than to a person, it is designed so that it cannot readily be tied back to an individual, wherever it is processed.

Changes to this policy

If this policy changes, the new version is published on this page and the "Last updated" date at the top changes with it. The date is the announcement. There is no email list and no newsletter, because the site does not collect email addresses.

If a change ever expands what is collected, it is described here in plain language before the change takes effect, and the identity gate remains the point at which consent is given. Continuing to use the exercise features after a change means you accept the version published at that moment.

How to complain

Raise it with the owner first. Open an issue on the GitHub repository issues page and describe the problem. Most concerns are answered the same day, and a specific complaint with a page and a description is the fastest route to a real fix.

You may also complain to your local data protection authority or supervisory authority. You do not need the owner's permission to do that, and raising it with the owner first does not take that right away.
